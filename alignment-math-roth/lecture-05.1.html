5Pairwise Margins Put a Tie at Zero

Okay. Let's rescale things a bit to make them symmetric. I want to say that if completion \(i\) wins against completion \(j\), someone prefers \(i\) to \(j\), then we award completion \(i\) one point. And if instead completion \(j\) wins against completion \(i\), meaning completion \(i\) loses against completion \(j\), we take away a point from \(i\). We award negative one point. So you get a point for winning, you lose a point for losing.

And then when completion \(i\) is fighting against completion \(j\), we'll say that the margin of \(i\) relative to \(j\) is just the expected number of points it gets. So with probability \(P_{ij}\), remember that's the probability that a random labeler prefers \(i\) to \(j\), \(i\) gets one point. With probability \(P_{ji}\), that's the probability that a random labeler prefers \(j\) to \(i\), \(i\) loses one point. Now, \(P_{ji}\) is just \(1 - P_{ij}\), because the only two outcomes that are possible are that \(i\) is preferred to \(j\) or vice versa. And so I can write this as just the difference between \(P_{ij}\) and \(P_{ji}\), which is just \(2P_{ij} - 1\).

We're still just talking about win probabilities. We've just scaled things in this way so that a positive value of this margin means that \(i\) is preferred to \(j\) on average, and a negative value of this margin means that \(j\) is preferred to \(i\) on average. And if it's a tie, if the probability that \(i\) wins is 50%, the margin is exactly zero. So we've rescaled things so that positive values mean \(i\) is winning over \(j\), negative values mean \(j\) is winning over \(i\), and zero, it's an exact tie.

And one reason this normalization is a little bit useful is that when we write down the matrix that records all of these margins, the matrix \(M\) such that entry \(ij\) is the margin that completion \(i\) has over completion \(j\), this matrix is antisymmetric. The margin of \(ji\) is just the negative of the margin of \(ij\), because if \(i\) wins over \(j\) with some probability, \(j\) wins over \(i\) with one minus that probability. And so conveniently this gives us a matrix that is equal to its negative transpose, \(M = -M^\top\). Nothing really going on here, just rescaling things so that that is the case, and taking advantage of the fact that the probability that \(i\) wins over \(j\) is just one minus the probability that \(j\) wins over \(i\). So far so good? Okay.

6General Policies Can Be Compared Pairwise

And so now, let's talk about what exactly we mean when we say we're going to compare two randomized policies. I've got some distribution \(P\), you've got a different one over completions, \(Q\). And now I want to fight \(P\) against \(Q\). I want to see if, in aggregate on the population, people prefer \(P\) to \(Q\) or vice versa.

So how am I going to do that? Well, I'm going to conduct a randomized trial as follows. I will select a random completion from \(P\). I will select a random completion from \(Q\). The one I selected from \(P\) was \(i\). The one I selected from \(Q\) was \(j\). And then I'm going to ask, and the probability is over a randomly selected user: what is the probability that a randomly selected user prefers this randomly selected completion \(i\) to this randomly selected completion \(j\)? I would like, if \(P\) is my distribution, that this probability be at least half, to tie or defeat your distribution.

And so when we just write down this comparison experiment under our margin scaling, what is that? Well, it's the expectation, when I sample a random completion from \(P\) and I sample a random completion from \(Q\), of the margin that \(i\) has over \(j\). Remember, this is positive exactly when \(i\) is preferred more than half the time on average, negative otherwise. And this is just the sum over all of the possible pairs of completions, \(i\) and \(j\), of the probability that my policy \(P\) puts on \(i\), times the probability that your policy \(Q\) puts on \(j\), that's the probability that \(i\) and \(j\) actually end up fighting with one another, times the margin of \(i\) relative to \(j\), this quantity that encodes the probability that \(i\) beats \(j\). And this is just the quadratic form \(p^\top M q\).

So again, what is the expression? Well, under this margin rescaling, if my distribution \(P\) defeats your proposed distribution \(Q\), that means that the outcome of this thought experiment will be that \(P\) wins. And under this margin scaling, that means that the value of this quantity, \(p^\top M q\), will be positive. If your distribution defeats mine in this thought experiment, the value of this quadratic form will be negative, and if they're tied, it'll be exactly zero. So this is just some convenient algebraic expression that encodes what we mean when my distribution \(P\) defeats your distribution \(Q\) or not. My distribution defeats yours when this quadratic form is positive, yours defeats mine when the quadratic form is negative, and it's equal to zero if we tie.

Does that make sense? This is just some algebra to write this down, but really the thought experiment to have in your mind is a very natural one. My distribution \(P\) defeats yours if on average, where I average over the completion \(i\) drawn from mine, the completion \(j\) drawn from yours, and I average over people in the population, people prefer my answers to your answers.

9The Robustness Question Is a Zero-Sum Game

And we'll come back to this margin game after we've established some facts about general zero-sum games. So for a general zero-sum game, let's define an arbitrary payoff matrix. That's what this margin matrix happens to be in this lottery game. And a general zero-sum matrix doesn't have to have the same symmetry properties that this comparison game has. In fact, the strategy sets for the two players don't even have to have the same cardinality. So in general, the payoff matrix in a zero-sum game is going to be some rectangular matrix, doesn't have to be square, that has some number of rows. Let's call that \(d_{\max}\), because those will be the strategies of the maximization player. And I'll have some other number of columns. Let's call that \(d_{\min}\). Those will be the strategies of the minimization player.

And in general, in a zero-sum game, what Max is going to get to do is choose what's called a mixed strategy, just a distribution over his pure strategies, a distribution \(P\) over the \(d_{\max}\) different rows of this matrix. And what Min gets to do is she gets to choose a distribution over her pure strategies, a distribution \(Q\) over the \(d_{\min}\) columns of this matrix. And what Max wants is for the payoff in this game to be large. That's why his name is Max. And what Min wants is for the payoff of this game to be as small as possible. That's why her name is Min. Their goals here are strictly adversarial. Max is trying to maximize the same quantity that Min is trying to minimize. That's why this is a zero-sum game. There's no room for cooperation.

And if Max chooses some distribution \(P\), and Min chooses a distribution \(Q\), then the payoff, this is the thing that Max wants to maximize and Min wants to minimize, is just the expected value of the entry \(ij\) in the payoff matrix, when Max randomly chooses his strategy \(i\) from his distribution and Min randomly chooses her strategy \(j\) from her distribution \(Q\). And this quadratic form \(p^\top G q\) is just the expected value of the entry of the payoff matrix that is indexed when we randomly sample a strategy from \(P\) and \(Q\) respectively.

Okay, so rock, paper, scissors, for example, is an instance of a zero-sum game. It's a three-by-three game where both players have three pure strategies, rock and paper and scissors, where rock beats scissors beats paper. And if I describe some distribution over rock and paper and scissors, and you describe some distribution over rock and paper and scissors, this expression would just specify what the probability was that I win when I sample an action from my distribution and you sample an action from yours. In general, the payoff matrix can be anything, the strategy space can be anything. What makes this a zero-sum game is that one of the players wants to maximize this quantity and the other wants to minimize it.

What we've been talking about so far, this comparison game in which we've been talking about lotteries over completions, is just a special case of this. In that case, the game matrix is just the margin matrix, and the strategy space for both players is just the set of completions. So we're going to establish some facts for general zero-sum games, but then we'll specialize back down to the comparison game we were talking about earlier.

14An Online Learner Faces an Adversary

So the first thing I need to do is teach you about this learning algorithm, Hedge. So first of all, in what environment does it even operate? Hedge is an algorithm for solving what is called an online adversarial learning problem. Unlike classical statistical learning problems, there's not going to be any distribution over data. Hedge will be operating interactively against an adversary who is choosing, at every round, a vector of losses. And we need an algorithm that can do something non-trivial even in this extremely difficult environment.

So here's the extremely difficult environment. In each round, the learner will choose some distribution over \(d\) actions. And then an adversary, with knowledge not only of the entire history so far, but of the distribution over actions that the learner just picked, gets to choose a loss vector specifying, for each of those actions that the learner might play, what the loss is going to be for that action. We're going to normalize the losses to be between minus one and one, where the convention is the learner wants to minimize loss. So a loss of one is bad, a loss of minus one is good. And the loss vector is chosen, again, by an adversary who we make no distributional assumptions about at all, and can be chosen as a function of the strategy announced by the learner at that round, which is the distribution over actions they're going to take.

And then the learner has committed to this distribution over actions. They have to actually sample from it. They can't change their distribution after the adversary chooses the loss, and so they get the expected loss that results when they sample an action from their distribution and experience the loss of the action they took, as specified by the adversary's loss vector. So if \(z_t\) is just the vector representation of the distribution over actions chosen by the learner and \(\ell_t\) is the loss vector, then the inner product between \(z_t\) and \(\ell_t\) is just the expected loss of the learner. And after the learner experiences this loss, they also get to observe the loss vector, which they can use to change what they do next round. They're allowed to adapt over time. There is no distribution, there's no training set, there's no test set. All of the action is in how the learner chooses to adapt to the losses presented by the adversary.

And so this is really quite a difficult learning environment. The adversary is totally unrestricted. We want to prove guarantees for the learner that hold in the worst case over adversaries. And so you might ask, what could we possibly hope to guarantee a learner in such an environment? For example, a standard thing you might want to prove about a learning algorithm in a distributional setting is that it obtains low loss. It's clearly impossible to show that any learner in this environment obtains low loss, because there may be nothing that gets low loss. It is entirely within the adversary's strategy space to make the loss of every action maximal, one, every single day, in which case the learner is definitely going to have average loss one every day, right? So whatever we're going to prove about learners in this environment, it can't possibly be that they're guaranteed to have low loss. That's just impossible in a setting where the adversary could make the loss maximal for every action every single day.

What can you do? Well, what we're going to do is evaluate the learner not in absolute terms, not by saying your loss has to be small, but by comparing how well the learner did with how well they could have done using some simple strategy after the fact, after we saw what the loss functions were. For example, if the adversary chooses the all-ones loss vector every single day, makes the loss of every action one, well, the learner is going to have average loss one over the sequence, but we're going to evaluate them as having done well, because anything else they could have done would have also had average loss one over the sequence. Everything had loss one over the sequence because the loss vector was all ones. And so the learner did great compared to the other things they could have done.

So that's going to be how we approach analyzing algorithms in this adversarial setting. We're not going to evaluate their loss in absolute terms. We can't make that small. We're going to evaluate their loss compared to other things they could have done on the same loss sequence.

15External Regret Compares Against the Best Fixed Action

So that is motivating this notion of regret, or let's call it external regret, because there are other kinds of regret; once you get into no-regret learning algorithms, you can try to get stronger guarantees. This is the simplest kind of regret. Here's what it is.

We're going to compare, on the one hand, the learner's actual expected loss over the trajectory of play by this adversary. Every day the learner proposed some distribution over actions, \(z_t\), the adversary proposed a loss vector, \(\ell_t\), and the inner product was the expected loss that day. This first term, summing up all of those inner products, is just the learner's actual expected loss given the realized trajectory of play. We're going to compare that to something else. We're going to look, first of all, after the fact, at all of the loss functions the adversary actually realized. This next term knows what all the loss functions are that the adversary chose. And we're going to ask, with the benefit of hindsight, how well could the learner have done by playing any fixed strategy?

So this comparator, on the one hand, has an advantage the learning algorithm doesn't. It knows ahead of time what all the loss functions are. On the other hand, it also has a disadvantage. The algorithm can play a different distribution every day, whereas the comparator is going to play a fixed distribution. The comparator is how well the learning algorithm could have done, with the benefit of hindsight, with the best fixed distribution it could have played. If it had to play the same distribution every single day, what distribution would have minimized its loss, where we get to perform that minimization after we know what the adversary is going to do? That's what the comparator is.

Regret is the difference between those two things. So you regret not having played this minimizing distribution \(z\) if you did worse than it, if this gap was positive. If the gap was zero or negative, you don't regret not having played any fixed distribution. That's why it's called regret.

So this notion of regret is a little bit funny, but maybe starts to make sense after you realize that in the adversarial learning setting, there's no way to guarantee anything in absolute terms about the learner's payoff, but you can start to think about promising things in relative terms. If the adversary makes the loss one for every action every day, the absolute loss is going to be high. It's going to be one every day, but the regret in this sense will be zero, because there's nothing else you could do, even with knowledge of those loss functions, that would have gotten lower loss. And more generally, the regret is comparing how well you actually did on the trajectory of play with how well you could have done, given the loss functions chosen by the adversary and optimizing against them with the benefit of hindsight, amongst the class of static distributions that do the same thing every day. Does regret make sense?

And one thing you might note is that with the benefit of hindsight, you don't actually need to randomize. When I say, what is the distribution that I could have played in hindsight, fixed every single day, that would minimize my loss? Well, because everything's linear here, the best distribution always puts all of its weight on one action. It's like in rock, paper, scissors: it's important that I randomize if I go first, right? If I'm going to announce a strategy to you first, I'd better not say rock. I'd better say I'm going to randomize between rock and paper and scissors. But if I have the benefit of hindsight, if I get to best respond after you've frozen what you're doing, there's no reason for me to randomize. If you announce that you're going to randomize between rock and paper and scissors, at that point I can just play rock. That's a best response. So is paper, so is scissors. And so the distribution that realizes this minimum is always going to be what's called a pure strategy, something that puts all of its weight on just one action. And so another, simpler way to express it is that an algorithm with low regret will have the property that its cumulative loss is no larger than the loss it could have obtained with the best fixed action in hindsight. There's no need to randomize when your opponent has had their strategies frozen already. Does that make sense?

And so this regret is a sum over \(T\) terms. It's the difference between my payoff summed over the \(T\) rounds compared to the payoff I could have obtained by playing my best fixed action in hindsight summed over the \(T\) rounds. So it's natural to normalize this thing by \(T\), to look at my average payoff per round. And if I can show you an algorithm that promises that the regret grows sublinearly, which means that the regret normalized by \(T\) goes to zero as \(T\) goes to infinity, then we'll call this a no-regret learning algorithm. And it'll guarantee that the algorithm does nearly as well as the best fixed action in hindsight, if only I play the game for long enough.

16The Hedge Algorithm

But let me show you an algorithm that gets this guarantee. It's actually a remarkably simple and natural algorithm, and it's called Hedge. So what is Hedge? There's going to be a single parameter it has, called the learning rate. And it's going to do something super simple. It's going to maintain over time weights for each action. Initially, every action gets weight one. Why not? We don't know anything in the beginning. And those weights that it's maintaining will form the distribution that it plays at each round. How do we turn weights into a distribution? We just divide by the cumulative sum of the weights. The probability that I play action \(a\) is just going to be the weight that I have on action \(a\) normalized by the sum of all of the weights. So all I'm doing is maintaining some weights, and every day I am playing the distribution on actions that results from just normalizing these weights into a probability distribution.

Now, all of the magic has to be in how I then update the weights in response to the losses that the adversary chooses. So I play this distribution, the adversary chooses a loss vector, and I get some expected loss. What do I do now? Well, I'm going to do something kind of natural, which is that I'm going to reduce the weight on actions that have high loss more than I reduce the weight on actions that have low loss. So I'm trying to reward low-loss actions and penalize high-loss actions. If an action has consistently had high loss, I want to play it less in the future, and so I want to reduce the weight on it.

And the particular way I'm going to do that is I'm just going to take the weight that I have on the action today, and I will down-weight it by multiplying it by \(e^{-\eta \ell_{t,a}}\): \(\eta\) is our learning rate parameter, times the loss the adversary chose for that action. So if the loss is one, then this gets the maximal down-weight, which is \(e^{-\eta}\). Otherwise, it gets down-weighted less. The weights are only going down, right? The weights never go up, they only go down. But we always play a distribution, because we're always normalizing the weights, and the weights of high-loss actions go down faster than the weights of low-loss actions.

That's the whole algorithm, that's it. Does this make sense? Not totally obvious how to analyze what's going to happen, but hopefully the algorithm is pretty transparent. It's pretty simple. We're just maintaining weights. The distribution we play is just the normalization of those weights into a distribution, and we penalize high-loss actions by cutting their weight more than other actions.
