3Forecasts Are Decision Interfaces

If we wanted to, for example, find a policy that maximizes social welfare, that was kind of a capstone result from last lecture. But in lots of settings, utility isn't actually derived directly from the text that an LLM, say, provides to you. The reason you are talking to the LLM is because you'd like to learn something that is decision relevant. You're actually going to make some choice in the real world. You're going to take an action. And how happy you are with that action depends in some way on some state of the world that you don't know, and you were talking to the machine in order to learn more about it so that your action would be better informed.

And so what I want to study now and in the next few lectures is the question of AI systems that we view as decision aids, that will be making forecasts that inform downstream decisions of users. And it's those downstream decisions that are actually the things that give users utility or not. What should the forecasts look like? What properties should they have so that they can, in that downstream decision-making process, actually give guarantees about the utility that users have for these downstream decisions?

And conceptually, we face some of the same problems that we faced in the elicitation part of the course, which is that different people have different objectives, right? This was the fundamental issue for the course. If there was only one user who had some preferences, we could just ask them their preferences and report them. Always return their favorite option. All of the difficulty that arose, the reason, for example, that the utility-maximizing policy wasn't specified from the elicited pairwise preferences, was because different people in the population had different preferences. And the same thing is going to be true here. If I knew exactly who my user was and what their utility function was, then maybe it would be at least somewhat more straightforward to figure out how I could aid them in maximizing that utility. But I don't. Either I don't know it, or maybe I have just many different downstream users who have different utility functions.

And so the thing we want to grapple with is how to deal with that. How can I create a single model that is able to make probabilistic forecasts about some decision-relevant state that will give guarantees simultaneously to many downstream users as they each try to solve their own decision problem, which might correspond to, for example, maximizing a different utility function? So that's at a high level the question I want us to start studying now and over the coming lectures.

And yeah, there's sort of an easy conceptual answer to this question in a world where there's some underlying distribution. Suppose there's some underlying distribution governing whatever is the decision-relevant state of the world. If I just always perfectly predicted what that distribution was, if I just communicated perfectly what that was, or even better, what the outcome was going to be, then this would be a sufficient statistic for all downstream decisions. But that's kind of hard. Probabilistic forecasting is hard. We shouldn't pat ourselves on the back if we just say, "Oh, I know how to solve this problem. I will just make perfect probabilistic predictions."

We're going to ask for something that still has this qualitatively similar guarantee, that it can aid many different downstream decision makers, but with a guarantee that's substantially weaker than perfect prediction. And one thing we will ask of the kinds of forecasts we're going to talk about is that they should provide the same interface, basically, between prediction and decision making as perfect probabilistic predictions. Say I give you some probabilistic forecast about the likelihood of some decision-relevant event for you. You should be able to safely make decisions by treating my forecast as if it were correct and optimizing for your own utility under the presumption that my forecast is correct, even though, of course, the condition that my forecast will actually have is substantially weaker than that. I want a condition that suffices for users to be able to, quote unquote, trust the forecasts and act accordingly. That is to say, even knowing that our forecasts are not perfect, are not necessarily correct, they should be justified in acting as if they were.

So you should also think about what we're going to talk about as developing a simple API, an interface if you like, between the prediction layer, that's what the AI system is doing, and the decision layer. That's what many different downstream users are doing, and we want a single interface even though they have many different decisions.

4The User Maximizes Expected Utility Under the Forecast

Okay. So let's write down a model, and we should think about this. There is some underlying decision-relevant state of the world. Let's say that this state of the world could take \(d\) possible values. The state of the world could be one through \(d\). \(d\) could be big maybe, but let's say for now it's finite. And a forecast specifies a probability distribution over these different possible values of the state of the world.

If there are just two outcomes, like it might rain or it might not rain, then I can specify a forecast with a single number. I can say there's a 60% chance of rain, which implicitly means there's a 40% chance of not rain. But more generally, if there are \(d\) outcomes, then I have to specify a full distribution over these \(d\) outcomes, which I can specify with \(d-1\) numbers, since the last one has to be set so that they sum to one.

And users: the reason I am making forecasts about these states of the world is because there are users downstream who care about them. How do they care about them? Well, they have actions that they can take, that they need to choose amongst. And how happy they are with those actions is specified by a utility function, and the utility function depends not only on the action that they take but on the state of the world. For example, you can choose to bring an umbrella or not, but you don't know how happy you are with the decision to bring an umbrella with you until you know whether it was raining. And that's why the rain forecast is going to inform your downstream decision.

Okay, so users, and this is sort of a departure from the way we talked about utility functions in the previous lectures. Before, we imagined that users just had utility for the completions themselves, the thing that the model told you. Now we're looking one step beyond that. We're saying, no, no, the value of the thing the model tells you is just informational. You have utility that is specified by something that actually manifests in the world, as well as the decision that you make. So the role of the prediction is now just informational, rather than the thing that you directly care about.

So there are \(d\) outcomes. For each action, I can just think about representing your utility function as a vector of numbers, which is just a vector that specifies: what's your utility for taking action \(a\) if the outcome is one, what's your utility for taking action \(a\) if the outcome is two, and so on. So I'll just think about these utility functions as vectors, because that will be convenient.

And if indeed it was true that the outcome was going to be drawn from some distribution \(p\), then we could talk about your expected utility for taking action \(a\) when the outcome is \(Y\), where \(Y\) is a random variable drawn from this distribution. It's just the sum over all of the outcomes of the probability of the outcome times the utility that you have for action \(a\) under that outcome. And the reason it was convenient to write your utility function as a vector is because this expected utility is just the inner product \(\langle u_a, p\rangle\) between your utility function vector and the distribution. If you knew the truth, if you knew the true underlying process from which this decision-relevant state was drawn, you could figure out what your expected utility was for each action with this inner product.

Okay. Now, if you knew what the truth was, if you knew the distribution from which the outcome was drawn, what should you do if you care about maximizing expected utility, if you're von Neumann–Morgenstern utility maximizers? Well, you should play a best response. So let's just give some notation for that. If you have utility function \(u\), and you happen to know that the underlying state of the world is drawn from some distribution \(p\), then the way you should act to maximize your expected utility, by definition, is just to take the action \(a\) that maximizes your expected utility: the maximum of these inner products for each of these utility vectors. So that's just by definition, but we'll give this a name, \(\mathrm{BR}_u(p)\). If there was some underlying state of the world that was truly drawn from some distribution, the best case for you in terms of how to act would be if you knew that distribution, and if you knew that distribution, you would always take the best response action.

5Running Example: Deploy, Hold, or Audit

Okay, so let's just give maybe a toy example, where the state of the world is just binary. Nothing here is tied to a binary state of the world, but maybe that's the easiest thing to understand. Let's say that there's some system that we are going to have to decide whether to deploy or not, but the system could be safe or it could be harmful. And it's useful for us to deploy a safe system. It'll actively hurt us to deploy a harmful system. We could decide, of course, not to deploy the system, in which case we don't get the benefit if it's good. We also don't experience the cost if it's harmful. Or maybe we could invest costly effort, possibly, maybe we get a team of human beings to read all the code or something. Invest costly effort to audit the system. If we audit the system, we can determine for sure whether it is safe or harmful. But we have to pay the cost of the auditing.

So let's say the cost of the auditing is one-fifth. If it is safe, we get the benefit of deploying a safe system, which is one, minus the cost of the auditing, so our payoff is only four-fifths. If it's harmful, we discover that and we don't deploy it, but we had to pay the cost of the auditing, so it's minus one-fifth.

So this is just our toy little game. You can deploy without auditing a safe system, in which case you get payoff one. You can deploy without auditing a harmful system, in which case you get payoff minus four. This would be the correct move given that the state of the world was safe. This would be the incorrect move otherwise. You can be cautious and just not deploy the thing, in which case your payoff is zero no matter what. This would be the correct move if the state of the world was harmful. Otherwise, it would be the incorrect move. Or there's this middle-ground option, which is you can pay one-fifth to audit and always get the payoff of the correct move minus the cost of auditing. Okay, so a simple toy game, but does the example make sense?

And so now suppose you've got some probabilistic system that is making a forecast about the relevant state of the world, which is: is this thing safe or not? Okay, so there are two outcomes. The forecast is a vector of dimension two, but it's a probability distribution, so the forecast is summarized in one number. Let's call that number \(r\). It's just supposed to represent the probability that the system is safe.

8Unbiased Forecasting On Average Is Not Enough

Towards trying to understand properties that good forecasts need to have, let's start by looking at really bad forecasts. Here's a maximally bad forecast, despite the fact that it's statistically unbiased. Suppose half the time the thing we're considering is harmful and the other half of the time it is safe. And our forecast has it exactly wrong. Every time it encounters something that's really harmful, it predicts a 99% probability of the thing being safe and causes us to deploy, and therefore get utility negative four. And on the other hand, whenever it's safe, it actually predicts that there's a 99% chance that it's harmful, which causes us to hold. So this is kind of a maximally bad forecast. It's always causing us to take the worst decision.

But despite that, the forecast does satisfy one sanity check, which is that it is unbiased. If you average over examples, it predicts on average that the frequency of safe proposals is one half. And that is correct: safe proposals do occur with probability one half. The reason why we could be unbiased even while inducing maximally bad decisions was because when we made one of the predictions, we had very high positive bias, and when we made the other prediction, we had very high negative bias. And in aggregate, those biases cancel out. And the problem was, even though those biases cancel out statistically, we make different decisions for each one. So they don't cancel out in the region in which we are making a fixed decision.

9Calibration Conditions on What Was Reported

Okay. So now I want to talk about a simple condition that will be strong enough to give decision-making guarantees. And I want to maybe generalize the setting a little bit. Rather than thinking about a fixed distribution of outcomes together with maybe features describing them, I want to think about sequences of predictions and outcomes, where maybe there's some notion of time now. At the first time step I make a prediction and then some outcome is realized. At the second time step I make a prediction and then some outcome is realized. This is only more general than the standard statistical setting, because if there is a distribution, I could just imagine the outcomes are drawn i.i.d. every day. But this will let us talk about the statistical property of calibration without needing to assume there's a fixed distribution. This will handle arbitrary distribution shift, for example.

Okay, so let's imagine we have just a sequence of predictions paired with outcomes. And now let's look at this sequence, and for any particular prediction \(p\) we could have made, let's just count how many times we made that prediction. Call that \(N_p\). Predictions are vectors of length \(d\). Let me just talk about outcomes also as vectors of length \(d\). The way I'll encode an outcome \(y\) as a vector of length \(d\) is just as the indicator vector that has a 1 in the \(y\) coordinate.

And now what I want to do is look at the empirical frequency of outcomes that were actually realized on those days when I predicted distribution \(p\). I want to look at, on average, over all of the days for which I predicted that outcome distribution, what was the empirical outcome distribution on those days. And we'll say that my sequence of probability forecasts is calibrated if for every prediction \(p\) that I ended up making, the empirical frequency of outcomes, averaged over the days on which I made that prediction, was also equal to \(p\).

So, for example, in the binary case, say I'm predicting the probability of rain: it should be that on those days when I forecast a 70% chance of rain, it should have rained 70% of the time. On those days when I forecast a 20% chance of rain, it should have rained 20% of the time. And this is just the generalization of that to arbitrary \(d\)-dimensional forecasts.

Okay, so it's saying my forecasts should be statistically unbiased. The average of my forecasts should equal the average of the outcomes. Not just overall: we saw that low statistical bias overall is not enough to guarantee any kind of good decision making downstream. But now I want this low-bias condition to hold conditional on the value of the forecast itself. If that holds, we'll say that the sequence of forecasts is calibrated.

11Could Another Rule Use the Same Report Better?

So what we would like is that people should be able to trust the predictions we make and act accordingly, no matter what their utility functions are; that they have some safety guarantee that it is safe to do so. How can we formalize that?

So, first of all, let's think about someone who does that. We don't know what their utility function is, but whatever it is, whenever we make a forecast \(p_t\), the action they take will be the one that trusts our predictions and acts accordingly. They will best respond to our forecast as if our forecast was correct. And we'll ask: was that a good idea? Or will they come to regret that? So we can ask after the fact, could they have done better by using our forecasts in some other way?

Ultimately, what they need to do is figure out how to map our forecasts to actions. We want them to be able to safely trust our forecasts and act accordingly, but they could have done something else. They could have used what I'll call a reinterpretation rule, which is just any other mapping from forecasts to actions. And what I'd like to be able to promise, the sense in which I'd like to be able to promise that it is safe to trust forecasts, is that if they trust the forecasts and act accordingly, they will not come to regret it. They will not come to regret not having done something different.

And so let's say that the average realized gain of some reinterpretation rule over the best response rule is just this regret-like notion. It's just, averaged over the sequence, the difference in utility that this decision maker would have obtained had they, instead of trusting the forecasts and acting accordingly, reinterpreted them with this rule \(\pi\). If this could be a big number, then they might not want to best respond to the forecasts. They might not want to trust them, because they realize there's the possibility that they could do much better by doing something else. On the other hand, if we promise that this number can never be big, that is a safety guarantee that says: amongst all of the things you could have done to interpret the forecasts we are giving you and convert them into actions, just trusting the forecasts and acting accordingly is the best thing you could have done, even in hindsight.

12First Group the Realized Gain by Report

So the claim is going to be that if the forecasts are calibrated, then they are trustworthy in this sense. If the forecasts are calibrated, then best responding to the forecasts is, uniformly across all policies you could have used to map the forecasts to actions, the best one. You will have zero regret to any other reinterpretation map if you just act accordingly.

And so let's see why this is. Let's define \(a(p)\), the action that you take if you trust the forecast when I make forecast \(p\); that's just the best response policy. And these expected utilities are linear in these action vectors. Remember, the utility that you'd get from taking action \(a\) if the state is \(y\) is just the inner product between the utility vector and the state vector. And so I can write down your regret with respect to any other reinterpretation map as a sum over all time of the difference between the utility that you would get under the action induced by the reinterpretation map minus the utility you would get by the best response action. Because everything is linear, it's this inner product: it's just the inner product between the difference between the utility vector for the reinterpreted action and the utility vector for the best response action, that vector, and the outcome.

And again, linearity is very convenient. We can rearrange this sum: rather than summing up over time, we can first sum up over the different predictions that we made, and then we can sum up over those rounds at which we made each prediction. And that second sum we can bring inside the inner product. So your regret is just the sum over all of the predictions that you made over the sequence of the inner product of the vector, which is the difference between the utility vector for the reinterpreted action you would take at that forecast minus the best response action you take at that forecast, with the sum of the outcomes realized on the days when you made that particular prediction.

Now, remember this definition of these buckets: the bias of the outcome conditional on making the prediction \(p\). It was the difference between the sum of the outcomes on the days when you made the prediction \(p\) and the sum of the predictions. Just rearranging terms, the sum of the outcomes on the days when you made the prediction \(p\), the thing that shows up in this inner product, is just the number of days you made the prediction \(p\) times the prediction vector itself, plus the bias, because the bias is just subtracting off the prediction and adding the outcome.

13Optimality Handles Forecasts; Calibration Handles Errors

So, substituting that in, we decompose this regret, the regret you have to not reinterpreting the predictions in some other way, as a sum of two terms. The first term is the inner product of this action difference with the forecast. And the second term is the inner product of the action difference with the bias for each of the different predictions that I could have made.

Now, let's think about that first term. The first term is the difference in utility you would have realized in expectation between reinterpreting the forecast and best responding to the forecast in this hypothetical world in which the forecast was actually correct. This is actually your expected utility difference between these two actions if the outcome really is realized according to this distribution. And in that world, you are behaving optimally, because by definition what you're doing is best responding. You're choosing the action that maximizes your expected utility in the world where the forecast is correct. Now, the forecast isn't correct, but this first term is your expected utility in the world where the forecast is correct. The correction is in the second term.

And so by definition, the first term is non-positive. The first term is zero or less, because the best response action is exactly the one that maximizes the inner product here, maximizes your expected utility. So this first term is only helping us out here. It can be negative: you can be doing something strictly better than the alternative. You can never be doing something worse, because definitionally you're doing the best thing possible in the world where the forecast is correct. And that first term lives in the world in which the forecast is correct. Now, the forecast, of course, is not correct, so that's why we have the second term. And so what we're going to find is that whatever regret you have in the end, it's bounded entirely by the second term, and so that's what we need to understand.

16Calibration Promises Interpretation, Not Informativeness

Now, you might wonder, is this too good to be true? So let's reflect for a moment on a very important limitation of this guarantee, and think about how to address this limitation in later lectures. Calibration is kind of this self-referential guarantee. It asks that my forecast be unbiased conditional on the value of the forecast. And so if I make a simpler forecast, it is easier to satisfy the guarantee. There are many ways to be calibrated. If I make perfect probabilistic predictions, I will be calibrated. So calibration is not at odds with making really good predictions. Perfect predictions are in particular calibrated.

But I can also make really terrible predictions. Well, there's a limit to how terrible they can be. They can't be as terrible as in our initial example. I can't always make the wrong prediction. But what I can do is make a constant prediction. I can ignore any observation I might have about the world and just always report the mean. That'll be calibrated as well. The reason is because calibration imposes these self-referential consistency conditions, conditions on the forecasts you make yourself. But if you make entirely uninformative forecasts, then that conditioning doesn't do anything. It's just a constant conditioning event. And so it's just asking that your forecast be statistically unbiased.

Okay, so we saw this example in which we had a forecast that was statistically unbiased and it always caused you to make the wrong decision. Calibrated predictors can't do that. That forecast wasn't calibrated, because although it was unbiased, it was not a constant forecast. And so unbiasedness was not the same as calibration. But if I make a constant forecast, then the calibration constraints all collapse down to a single constraint: just statistical unbiasedness.

So, for example, going back to this setting where half of the proposals are safe and half are harmful, calibration does stop me from having this maximally bad forecast, but I can still have a pretty bad forecast. The worst calibrated forecast is the one that will ignore any information it has about the instance and just always predict that there's a probability one half that the proposal is harmful. It's calibrated, and best response will audit everything, deploy exactly the safe proposals and not the others, but it will pay this auditing cost. So the expected utility for best response under this calibrated forecast is three-tenths.

This is consistent with the guarantee we just proved. This is indeed better than any other mapping from the forecast to actions you could have taken, but because our forecast didn't make any distinction between days, neither would any remapping of it. So in this case, this just degrades to a simple regret bound. It says always auditing is better than always deploying or always holding, right? It could be that there's additional information out there that would have allowed you to make perfect forecasts, that would have allowed you to do better than this. And this is not in contradiction with the theorem we proved, because essentially the forecast didn't contain that information. And calibration, of course, does not promise that you can recover from nothing information that was not in the forecast. So we will think about strengthening the guarantee of calibration so that it promises something stronger than this. But this is a good thing to keep in mind.

18A Weaker Form of Hindsight Criticism

So let's think about another way of evaluating regret for our downstream decision maker. Remember, our downstream decision maker is trusting the forecasts we make and acting according to them. They're pretending the forecasts we make are correct and they're best responding. They're choosing the action that would be the best response in the world in which our forecasts were correct. And this induces a sequence of actions.

Now, they might ask themselves: okay, in hindsight, was there some beneficial way I could have reinterpreted the actions that I took? Could I have remapped, using some rule \(\phi\), the actions that I took to a different action? For example, could it be that, looking back, every day that I audited, I would have been better off just deploying instead?

This is a weaker form of reinterpretation than the one we talked about for calibration. There we were looking at alternative mappings from the predictions themselves to actions. Those alternatives could map two predictions differently, even if they both induce the same best response action. Here we're looking at a weaker form of reinterpretation that is kind of a post-processing of best response. I can remap your best response actions to other things, but I've lost the ability to make distinctions between two forecasts that were collapsed into the same best response action.

But we can look at the same kind of regret, which in this case is going to be called swap regret. So we can say: okay, what you really did is you best responded to my forecast every day. Is there any one of these action remappings that, had you applied it in hindsight, would have increased your average utility? If so, you have regret to not having done that.

19Swap Regret Tests Every Action Remapping

We call this swap regret. So the swap regret that a decision maker has is the regret that they have to the best possible remapping they could have deployed with the benefit of hindsight. To figure out your swap regret, you wait until the end of the interaction. Then you look back and think about the best possible remapping you could have deployed in hindsight, the one that would cause your utility to be highest. And then you feel bad about not having done that. So it's the worst possible regret you have to any swap function. It's always non-negative.

And if I promise you that you have no swap regret, just to contextualize this with the regret bounds we proved for the multiplicative weights algorithm a few lectures ago, this is only a stronger guarantee. The thing we proved for multiplicative weights is that it would give you a way to make decisions so that your payoff in hindsight was at least as high as that of the best fixed action, with the benefit of hindsight. Here we're promising only something stronger, because you still have the ability to regret not having played a fixed action. That's just the constant remapping that remaps everything to that fixed action. And now you've developed the means to have more nuanced regrets as well. So at a mathematical level, saying that you have no swap regret is a qualitatively stronger statement than saying that you have no external regret, the kind of regret that we established for the multiplicative weights algorithm, because that corresponds to just a subset of these remapping functions. 20Decision Calibration Uses Action Cells

Okay. And so let me tell you about this weakening of calibration called decision calibration. What was calibration? Calibration was saying: I want the forecast to be unbiased conditional on the value of the forecast itself. And I'm going to require that for every value of the forecast, you have to have low bias.

So say I'm forecasting the probability of rain and you're deciding whether to bring an umbrella or not. Maybe you're going to bring the umbrella if there's more than a 40% chance of rain, because you don't like getting wet. But calibration is evaluating me on fine-grained distinctions between a 99% chance of rain and a 98% chance of rain and a 97% chance of rain that are all decision irrelevant to you. And in one dimension, this is a small cost to pay, because there are only so many forecasts, up to discretization, that I can make in one dimension. But this becomes extremely expensive in \(d\) dimensions. The number of distinct forecasts I can make grows exponentially in \(d\), and calibration asks for some constraint to hold on all of them.

Now, there was a good reason for that: it was because we didn't know what the downstream utility function was. But if we did know, there would be something that we could do that was more directly connected to decision making and wouldn't have this problem of blowing up with the dimension. Which is to say, I'm still going to bucket your predictions in some way and ask that the bias of the predictions within each bucket is small. But now I'm not going to bucket your predictions by exactly the value of the prediction. I'm going to bucket them by what action they cause the downstream decision maker to take. So I'm just going to collapse together into the same bucket all of the predictions that induce the same action for the downstream decision maker.

Now, rather than there being one bucket for each prediction, there's one bucket for each action \(a\). And it is the aggregate bias of the predictions, still the sum of the difference between the outcome and the prediction. But now I'm summing up over all of the days for which your prediction induced best response action \(a\). The forecast now might be different on different ones of those days. Under the bucketing of full calibration, within each bucket the forecast was constant. Now it's not anymore. If you're going to bring an umbrella whenever I make a 40%-chance-of-rain prediction or more, the umbrella bucket could contain any set of predictions between 40% and 100%. So this is a much coarser bucketing.

And if you have \(k\) actions, the nice thing is: how many buckets are there? Well, there are only \(k\), no matter how high dimensional the decision space is. The computational problem with full calibration, which we'll appreciate more in a lecture when we think about algorithms, is that the number of buckets is going to grow exponentially in \(d\). Decision calibration does not have that problem. The number of buckets is fixed. It's equal to the number of actions, independent of the ambient dimension of the state space.

24Decision Calibration Controls Swap Regret

So again, we've proven a theorem. We've said, look, your forecasts are not fully calibrated. They're merely decision calibrated. And this is much easier to obtain in \(d\) dimensions, because the number of buckets whose bias we need to control is only equal to the number of actions, rather than something like two to the dimension. Even under this weaker decision calibration condition, we can still promise you something about why you might want to best respond. If you trust our forecasts and act accordingly, then we promise you that if you best respond, even in hindsight you will have very little swap regret. Your swap regret will be bounded by the decision calibration error. Remember, this is only a stronger guarantee than we derived for, for example, having someone run the multiplicative weights algorithm. That promised them only no regret to the best fixed action. This is promising something stronger.

There is, of course, something meaningfully weaker here compared to full calibration, which is that decision calibration is defined relative to a particular user's utility function. One of the great things about full calibration is that it wasn't defined in that way. And so a single guarantee could promise there's no regret simultaneously for every downstream decision maker. What we just established is just a guarantee of this sort for one downstream decision maker, whose utility function was used to define this decision calibration.

So looking forward, we're going to want a way to make forecasts that satisfy decision calibration not just for one person; that would be throwing away this whole idea of our prediction technology as being something that is deployed to inform many downstream decision makers. We want a way to promise decision calibration simultaneously for many different downstream utility functions.

26Decision-Calibrated Forecasting Appears Circular

What we derived here was just the consequences of having well-calibrated forecasts, either full calibration or decision calibration. If your forecasts happen to be calibrated, then you can promise things to downstream decision makers. But of course, that's only interesting if we have some way of actually guaranteeing that. And that's what we're going to do next class.

And looking ahead just a moment, we intentionally defined calibration here as a property of sequences of predictions, rather than as a property of a static forecaster and a distribution. We could have done that; that would have been a weaker condition. The reason we did it is that we're going to see that underlying this is going to be the same kind of phenomenon that allowed the multiplicative weights algorithm to give guarantees even in adversarial environments. We're going to see a powerful algorithmic technique called Blackwell approachability that will allow us, in an adversarial environment, so we don't have to assume that there's a distribution, things could be changing dynamically in arbitrary ways, to guarantee that we have forecasts that, for example, are decision calibrated simultaneously for many downstream utility functions.

And there's this slightly self-referential thing, right? Calibration is this self-referential thing. We're asking for unbiasedness conditional on the forecast. Our forecast should be unbiased conditional on some property of the forecast itself, either the full value of the forecast, for full calibration, or the action it induces. And so that's something we need to untangle when trying to derive an algorithm for this. We can think about what our running biases are in each of these buckets we need to control. But where we increase or decrease the bias is not independent of what that bias is per round, because in choosing what forecast to make, we also self-referentially choose which bucket we need to update. And that's just something we're going to need to resolve somehow in deriving these algorithms.

27Summary

Okay, so to summarize, the perspective I want to take on forecasting for the next few lectures is viewing probability forecasts as decision interfaces. The reason we are forecasting something is only because this forecast is decision relevant for someone. It's going to inform some decision. And the reason we want an interface is because we're going to provide some machine learning service that will be consumed by many downstream people who will have different utility functions. They'll be facing different decision problems. And we want a single set of forecasts to be a useful interface for all of their decision-making problems.

And we want the interface to be simple. They should just be able to act as if the forecasts are correct and best respond. And calibration gives us an interface like that. It says it is safe for you to just interpret the forecast as if it is correct and best respond. The form of the safety guarantee is that you could not have done better with our forecasts by reinterpreting them in any other way. That's what these regret guarantees mean. There's a stronger version of that guarantee for full calibration and a somewhat weaker version of it for decision calibration. And the reason to be aware of the weaker condition, even though it also has a weaker conclusion, is that it will be much easier to obtain in high-dimensional settings.
