3Every Single Completion Can Be Beaten

To briefly recap what the issue is and the proposed fix that we had. The thing that we don't want to happen is that we deploy, say, some language model that gives some distribution over prompts, and then have somebody else immediately be able to say, here's a better one. How do I know it's better? Well, I've polled people and two-thirds of people say that it's better.

And the reason why it is not immediately obvious that we can avoid this unfortunate situation is exactly this cyclic set of preferences that we've seen repeatedly now in this class. We can have a population, each of which has coherent preferences, that results in three outcomes, \(a\) and \(b\) and \(c\), that has the property that if I deploy \(a\), two-thirds of the people will agree that \(b\) is better. If I deploy \(b\), two-thirds of the people will agree that \(c\) is better. But if I deploy \(c\), two-thirds of the people will agree that \(a\) is better. So there's kind of no way. And this is just a set of preferences, so it's not a matter of picking which of these three completions to use. It seems like just a fundamental obstacle.

4Randomization Can Balance the Cycle

So our Hail Mary attempt to get around this problem is to use randomization. We noted that for this particular set of preferences, the cycle is actually not such a big deal, because we could deploy a policy that randomizes. That's what models do generally. And if we just randomize uniformly across all three of the options, then indeed there is no other of these three — there's not even any distribution over completions — that anyone would be able to deploy and claim that their language model is better than ours, in the sense that a majority of people prefer it. At worst, they could deploy something that would tie.

And so the basic premise from what we started last time was that if we are going to evaluate language models, say, by determining whether there's any other language model that can be deployed that people on the whole prefer to ours, that maybe randomization can save us.

5Pairwise Margins Put a Tie at Zero

So, just recalling the basic setup. We normalized the win probabilities into this margin matrix. We rescaled these win probabilities, which are numbers between zero and one, into what we called margins, which are numbers between minus one and one. And we wrote them down in this matrix so that \(M_{ij}\) is just the margin by which \(i\) beats \(j\), where positive margin means \(i\) beats \(j\) more frequently than \(j\) beats \(i\), negative margin means \(i\) beats \(j\) less frequently than \(j\) beats \(i\), and zero margin means they're exactly tied.

One nice thing about this normalization is it means this margin matrix is antisymmetric. \(M_{ij}\) is the negative of \(M_{ji}\), which is going to be convenient for us.

6General Policies Can Be Compared Pairwise

And just to formalize how exactly we're comparing two distributions over completions to one another — what causes us to say that some distribution \(q\) beats our distribution \(p\) — well, it was just the result of the following randomized experiment. We draw a random completion from \(p\). We draw a random completion from \(q\). We draw a random evaluator from the population. And we ask them, do you prefer \(i\), the thing we drew from \(p\), to \(j\), the thing we drew from \(q\), or not? And if they're more likely to say yes than no, we say \(p\) defeats \(q\). If they're more likely to say no than yes, we say \(q\) defeats \(p\), and otherwise we say they tie.

And in the language of this margin matrix that we've written down, whether or not \(p\) defeats \(q\) can be established by just taking the quadratic form \(p^\top M q\), that's just giving us the expectation of this outcome over these random draws. Since we've rescaled everything to this \([-1, 1]\) margin scale, \(p\) defeats \(q\) exactly when \(p^\top M q\) is greater than or equal to zero. If it's less than zero, \(q\) defeats \(p\), and otherwise they're tied.

7A Robust Policy is Evaluated Against the Strongest Challenger

And the idea that we had of coming up with a maximal lottery — that's really just a name for a policy that cannot be defeated, even after our competitors know what it is. So the idea was we would be able to deploy some policy, some distribution over completions. Our competitors should get to look at it, and they should, with knowledge of what we deployed, try to defeat it, and they still shouldn't be able to.

So formalizing this, we would like to deploy some policy \(p\) such that even after our competitors see what it is and try to defeat it, try to minimize the win probability, we should still win. Or we should at least maximize the margin, whatever that is. So we want to deploy the policy that maximizes our pairwise win probability, even against the worst-case distribution our competitors could challenge us with.

That is why we're interested in this max-min problem. We go first and deploy the policy. Then our competitors look at it and get to challenge us with it. And we would like, to the extent possible, that even with that informed challenge from our competitors, they not be able to defeat our policy. That's why we want to maximize the minimum of \(p^\top M q\), because we know our competitors are going to try to minimize this. They're going to try to beat us as frequently as they can, and we would like to mitigate that risk. We'd like to maximize the minimum. Yeah?

8Zero Is the Best Guarantee We Could Hope For

One useful observation, just to set the stage, of course, was that given how we've set things up, one thing our competitors could do is they could just copy our policy. They could deploy exactly the same lottery we did. And if they did that, they would obviously tie us just by symmetry of this competition. We're not going to beat our own distribution, we're going to tie it, right? If we deploy \(p\) and they deploy \(p\), the margin is going to be zero. Obvious if you think about the competition; it also follows from the antisymmetry.

Which means that our hope, of course, cannot be to deploy a policy that strictly defeats the best challenger, because it is in our competitor's strategy space to just copy what we did. So the strongest thing we could hope for, and this is the thing we're shooting for, is to deploy a policy that either beats or ties all possible challengers. They could copy what we do, in which case it would tie, but we do want to prevent them from being able to strictly beat us. We know, of course, that if we deployed a deterministic completion, they would always be able to strictly beat us. So to the extent that this is going to be possible, it's only going to be because of the randomization.

9The Robustness Question Is a Zero-Sum Game

And so we took a digression last class to derive a general, useful theorem about zero-sum games. The reason we did this is because this competition we've set up between policies is a particular zero-sum game, and so understanding zero-sum games is useful.

In a general zero-sum game, this margin matrix is just replaced by some other matrix \(G\). It doesn't have to be antisymmetric anymore. And there's a maximization player and a minimization player, just as there is in the special case of the lottery game. The maximization player chooses a distribution. The minimization player chooses a distribution over their actions. And each of them cares about the expected payoff in the game, which will still be written as this quadratic form. And as their names suggest, the maximization player would like to make this number big, and the minimization player would like to make this number small.

10Commitment Order Defines Two Values

Then we thought about order of play. You could have the maximization player go first, announce the strategy to the world, and then let the minimization player, with knowledge of that strategy, come up with the maximally effective response, the maximally effective challenge, the best response. And if both players were playing optimally, the resulting payoff would be what we call the maximin value of the game. Maximin because the maximization player, who goes first, deploys a policy to maximize the payoff after his opponent's best response by minimizing.

And symmetrically, the minimization player could have gone first. If the minimization player went first, we would obtain, if both players played optimally, the minimax value of the game. Minimax because the minimization player, who goes first, would initially choose her distribution to minimize the reward, and subsequently the maximization player, with knowledge of the minimization player's distribution, would choose his to maximize the reward. Maximin and minimax record in their names the order of play that we're talking about.

And it's intuitively obvious that going first can only be a disadvantage if you're revealing information to your opponent, and the real question is, can it ever be a strict disadvantage, or does it not matter?

12With Randomization, Commitment Order Does Not Matter

And the main thing we aspire to prove — and we got partway through the proof last time, but not all the way — is von Neumann's minimax theorem, which is the meat of it, the other direction relating the maximin value to the minimax value. And what von Neumann's minimax theorem asserts is that generally, in any zero-sum game, not specific to this lottery competition game, order of play does not matter. That is, if Max goes first and obtains the maximin value, that will be the same payoff he gets even if Min is allowed to go first and he's allowed to best respond, and we get the minimax value. In all such games, the maximin value is the same as the minimax value. Order of play doesn't matter.

This isn't true in non-zero-sum games. And it's not true even in zero-sum games without allowing randomness. This is a property that requires both of those things: the zero-sum property and the fact that people are allowed to randomize.

29Antisymmetry Establishes the Value of the Preference Game

It was because of this preference game. Now, what we've just proven is that zero-sum games generally, and in particular this preference game, have some value. We've been calling them the maximin value and the minimax value, but now that we know they're the same, we can just start calling it the value of the game. Different zero-sum games have different values. But for the preference game, because of the antisymmetry, we know what the value of the game is. The value of the game has to be zero.

Let's remind ourselves of why. In the preference game, it's just the zero-sum game where the payoff matrix is this margin matrix. And the margin matrix, because of the way we scaled it, has this antisymmetry property: if we negate it and take the transpose of it, we get the same matrix. And in any such antisymmetric game, if you play any strategy against itself, it gets zero payoff.

And the way we noted this originally was in the max-min order of play. If Max goes first, then one thing that Min can do in response — maybe she can do something better, but one thing she could do in response — is just to copy Max's strategy. The maximin value of the game is at most the payoff she gets for any particular strategy she could use, and so it's at most zero. She can always guarantee that it's at most zero by just stealing Max's strategy.

But the same strategy-copying argument works in the reverse order of play. Suppose Min goes first and has to announce what her challenger is. And then Max gets to best respond. Well, Max can always guarantee that when he best responds, he wins at least half of the time, or the expected margin is at least zero, for exactly the same reason. Because although he may have more clever best responses, if nothing else, he can always just copy Min's strategy. So if he best responds with the most clever thing he can do, it'll only be better than if he copies the strategy, which is one thing he could do. Which establishes the minimax value of the game is at least zero.
