1Bayesian Persuasion

So, some weeks ago we talked about calibration and things like decision calibration as something like decision alignment in a communication setting. If I am not empowered directly to take actions, you're the one empowered to take actions, but I can talk to you. I can give you information that's hopefully relevant to your relevant state, informative information. So if I know your utility function and I want to help you optimize your utility function, I can make forecasts that are, for example, decision calibrated relative to that utility function, maybe also relative to various benchmark policies you could have used. And if you then trust the forecast and act accordingly, you will inherit nice downstream guarantees.

What I want to talk about in this lecture is a similar communication setting. So think about maybe a model that is not empowered to take actions. All it can do is try to inform you, and you get to take actions. But I want to think about a similar model in which a party communicating with you does not necessarily have the same objectives as you do. So it knows that you're the one empowered to take actions, but because it has a different objective function than you, it's trying to convince you to take actions that would ultimately be beneficial for, say, its utility function. And that'll be this classical model of Bayesian persuasion.

3When the Provider Chooses What to Reveal

So just to have maybe a toy scenario in our mind (we'll see this as a more general framework), let's suppose there's some new technology and we're trying to decide whether we want to deploy it or not. And there's some unknown state of the world that is relevant to our deployment decision. Maybe the technology is safe or not. And we don't know what that state of the world is, but we know what our utility function is. If we deploy the model and it's safe, we get one unit of utility. If we deploy the model and it's unsafe, we get minus four units of utility. And either way, we can just hold, we can fail to deploy the model, in which case we don't care if it's safe or not, we just get zero units. Nothing good happens, nothing bad happens.

Now, we don't know what the state of the world is, but there's someone else that does know what the state of the world is. They choose to tell us something about it. But the problem is, their objective function is different than ours. They get a dollar if we deploy the model and zero dollars otherwise. So they are rewarded exactly when we deploy the model, independently of whether it's safe or not.

So the setup makes sense. I don't know the state of the world. I've got my utility function. I'd like to deploy if safe, not deploy if harmful. I can talk to someone who does know the state of the world, but I should be aware when I talk to them that their objective is different than mine. In this case, they would like me to deploy no matter what. They don't care whether the technology is safe or not.

So just to give terminology, let's say that I, the user, I'm the receiver, because I'm the one listening to the message and deciding what to do. The person who is informed we'll call the sender. They can tell me something. They can decide what they want to tell me.

Now, I am not stupid. And so if they decide, for example, to just say, "Oh yeah, it's safe," always, no matter what, and I know that's what they're doing, I'm not going to take them at face value. I'm not just going to trust that it's safe and deploy it. I am going to think about what my belief should be about the state of the world given that they told me that it was safe. And if they always tell me that it's safe, then the fact that they told me that it was safe doesn't tell me anything at all, doesn't cause me to update my prior beliefs at all.

So the sender has this problem: they've got a different objective than I do, but they legitimately have to convince me. I'm not stupid. If they try just always lying to me, for example, I just won't listen to them. I won't update my beliefs as a function of what they say at all. And so it is not a priori obvious how the sender should behave in this situation.

4The User Needs an 80% Safety Probability

So in order to reason about what the sender should do, first we need to think about what the receiver is going to do. Let's think about everything in a Bayesian framework. So there's going to be a prior belief over the state of the world. And the receiver is pretty smart. The receiver is going to be a Bayesian. They are going to, knowing how the world works, condition on the information they have received and form a correct posterior belief on the state of the world.

And so if I receive some information, I'm going to look at what is my expected utility for holding, which is always zero, compared to my expected utility for deploying, in expectation over the true state of the world, given what the information is. And let's say, this is just a one-dimensional setting, we can summarize my posterior belief as the probability that I place on the event that the technology is safe. Let's call that \(x\).

So my posterior expected utility for deploying is the probability that the technology is safe, that's \(x\), times 1 (I get a dollar if it's safe and I deploy), plus the probability that it's not safe, that's \(1-x\), times what I lose if I deploy and it's not safe, that's \(-4\). And I can write out what my posterior expected utility is for deploying, \(5x-4\), and just by solving for \(x\), I can find what my best response function is. I will deploy exactly when I believe that the probability that the technology is safe exceeds \(4/5\). That is when my expected utility for deploying exceeds my outside option, which is to hold. It always gives me zero.

So the sender, knowing this, is going to want to figure out how to share information with me in a way so that, given that I am going to reason as on this slide, I deploy as frequently as possible. Again, the reason that is non-trivial is because if they always tell me "safe," for example, for a Bayesian, that's the same thing as telling me nothing. It's not that I'm going to believe them. I'm going to condition on the meaning of their signal, and if they always say the same thing, independently of what the true state of the world is, the signal is entirely uninformative. [...]

To get rid of corner cases: in this particular example, when my posterior belief that the technology is safe is exactly four-fifths, I'm indifferent between these two. Let's just say I'll break ties in favor of deploying. This is just an example. More generally, whatever the threshold is for taking some action, I'm going to use the variable \(q\). 7Choose the Rule to Meet the User's Threshold

So if you think about it, we started with our two trivial signaling schemes. Share no information at all: that results in the receiver never deploying. That was pretty bad for the sender. The sender only wants the receiver to deploy. Or the other trivial one would share full information. When it's safe, say safe. When it's not safe, say not safe. That one induced a posterior belief in the receiver either of 100% safety or of 0% safety.

And if you think about it from the sender's perspective, those induced posterior beliefs were a little bit wasteful. Because in order to get the receiver to deploy, I only needed to convince them that there was an 80% chance that the technology was safe. And yet, under the full revelation policy, whenever I get them to deploy, I've actually convinced them that there was 100% chance that it was safe. I was being wasteful with the safe probability mass. And so you might think, well, maybe there's some way to smear their beliefs a bit more loosely, so that when they deploy, it's because they believe there's an 80% chance that the technology is safe. And what I might gain by doing that is causing them to have that belief more frequently and therefore getting them to deploy more frequently.

So how should I maximally smear beliefs like that? Well, when I tell them to deploy, I want there to be an 80% chance that the thing is safe. So I want the deploy recommendation to combine both safe and unsafe states in the right proportion. So maybe when the thing is unsafe, I should just tell them for sure it's unsafe. Why not?

This is sort of like gerrymandering. Think about packing and cracking congressional districts. A district that I'm going to lose, I'm happy to lose it by a lot. A district that I'm going to win, I want to win just barely enough. I don't want to waste votes. And so a message that causes the user not to deploy, that's like a district I'm going to lose. I might as well lose it by a lot. So it's okay to induce 0% belief of safety on days when I tell them not to deploy.

So let's say that if the state truly is unsafe, I'll just tell them it's unsafe. Sorry, other way around. If it's truly safe, I'll tell them it's safe. On the other hand, I want to mix some of the unsafe mass in with the safe message. So on days when it's truly unsafe, I will, some fraction of the time, tell them that it's safe. And with the remainder, I'll tell them that it's unsafe. So conditional on seeing "unsafe," they will know that the state really is unsafe. The only way you can get an unsafe message is if the state really is unsafe. This is like having 0% of the vote. If you see unsafe, you're certain it's not safe, you don't deploy.

But now, conditional on seeing "safe," you're no longer certain it's safe. Because you could have gotten that message because the state truly was safe and I told you so with certainty, but you also could have gotten that message because the state was unsafe, and the way the coin flips came out caused me to tell you "safe" anyway. So as I dial up the probability that I tell you that the state is safe when it is really unsafe, starting from zero and then going higher and higher, what will happen is your posterior belief, conditional on seeing "safe," will start at 100% and then go lower and lower. And the idea is to turn the dial so that, conditional on seeing "safe," your belief of safe is just big enough to deploy. So I'm not wasting any votes.

So let's say that I will recommend deployment for every safe proposal. And for an unsafe proposal, I will recommend deployment with some probability \(r\). Otherwise I won't. \(r\) is a variable we're going to solve for. So now if I look, as a function of \(r\), at the probability that the receiver thinks the state is safe conditional on being told to deploy: well, there's the probability that it really is safe. That's two-fifths. Whenever it's safe, I tell them it's safe. But I have to divide by the probability that I tell them it's safe. And that happens with probability \(2/5\), because if it is safe I tell them it is safe, plus \(3/5\), the probability that it's unsafe, times \(r\), the probability that I tell them to deploy even when the state is unsafe.

And I would like to choose \(r\) so that the probability of safe, conditional on telling them to deploy, is just high enough that this will actually get them to deploy the product, which in this case is four-fifths. So if I don't want to waste any votes, if I want to make deployment happen as frequently as possible, I should set \(r\) to cause this belief to be exactly four-fifths, just enough to get them to deploy.

So now it's just an exercise of solving for \(r\). To cause these to be exactly equal is to choose \(r\) so that the deployment probability is exactly one half, which turns out to mean that, conditional on it being unsafe, I should still lie and tell you that it's safe one sixth of the time. That's the maximal frequency with which I can lie so that you'll still listen to me. If I lied to you even a little bit more frequently than that, all of a sudden my claim would no longer have enough power to convince you to deploy. Your posterior belief conditional on my telling you to deploy would have gone below 80%. But I can get you to deploy half the time.

9More Deployment, Less Utility for the User

Just to compare these policies for both parties: if I shared no information, the receiver would deploy never, and the receiver would also get no utility. So both the sender and the receiver would get utility zero.

If I use the full disclosure rule, this is optimal for the receiver. The receiver now gets to do exactly what they want, which is to deploy exactly when it is safe. They get their optimal utility, two-fifths. And I'm doing better than no disclosure, but not optimally. I'm only getting two-fifths.

And under this clever rule that increased my utility, that got the receiver to deploy half the time, well, the way I got them to do that was by really making them indifferent between deploying and not deploying on the days when I tell them it's safe. Because when they weren't indifferent, from my perspective, I was wasting votes. I was wasting probability mass. So the way I maximized the probability of deployment was by making them as incredulous as possible, as indifferent as possible between deploying and not deploying, without crossing over the line. Which means that at that rule, their expected utility for holding is always zero, but I also cause their expected utility for deploying to be zero.

And so in optimizing my own utility, I have caused their utility to drop to zero, as bad as it was if I had just told them nothing. I can't cause their utility to drop lower than that, because they're not stupid, they're Bayesian. One thing they can do is always ignore my signal. There's nothing I can do that will cause their utility to drop below the no-information baseline. But in optimizing my utility, I have pushed their utility down to that.

So because the way I win this game is by causing them to be indifferent between their two actions, our objectives are at odds here. I've made their utility as bad as possible, subject to the unavoidable constraints, but I can't make it worse than had I provided no information.

11A Finite Model of Information Design

So we saw the two naive policies, reveal everything, reveal nothing. Then we saw this one example policy where we could do better: we could get them to deploy half the time. So you might ask, can I do better, or is getting them to deploy half the time, in this instance, the best that I can possibly do?

In order to prove that actually you can't do better than getting them to deploy half the time, we want to understand the structure of this information. The game that the sender is playing is really trying to shape the posterior beliefs of the receiver. The sender has control over what the receiver does (that's what the sender cares about, what action they take) only through being able to shape their posterior beliefs. They can't force them to do anything. They can only convince them of things that will cause them to take the appropriate action. The question I want to ask is, which posterior beliefs can the sender induce in the receiver through any signaling scheme?

So let's ask this in a slightly more general model now. So far we've just had a single example, but now let's say there's a generic set of states. It's no longer binary, not just safe or unsafe. There could be any finite number of states. And it's not just two actions like hold or deploy. There's again any finite collection of actions. And everyone's a Bayesian. So there's a common prior over the set of states. Everyone knows the prior distribution; let's call it \(b_0\). The state is drawn from this prior distribution. And without loss of generality, we can assume that the prior is full support. If there's any state that has zero probability under the prior, there's nothing I can do to convince the receiver that that was the state, since it had zero probability, so I can just ignore that state. I can just throw it away.

And there are two utility functions, one for the sender, one for the receiver, that are both a function of the state and the action that the receiver takes. The sender, again, has no ability to directly take action, only to try to convince the receiver. It says here that the utilities are known to both parties, but actually the receiver doesn't need to know the sender's utility, only the signaling scheme.

12A Signal Has a Probability and a Posterior

So once all of that is fixed, we can talk about the probability of a signal. Let's call that \(\alpha_s\). The probability that a signal is generated is just the sum, over all of the underlying states of the world, of the probability that that state of the world is realized times the probability that the signal is selected given that underlying state. There's different ways a signal can be selected, one for each of the underlying states of the world. We're just going to sum up over all of those different ways. So that's the probability of a signal. And again, we can, without loss of generality, discard signals that have probability zero. If the signal was never sent, it's not really in the set of signals.

A signal will induce some posterior belief in the receiver. Let's think about that as a vector. So given a signal \(s\), the posterior belief that the receiver has I'll think of as a vector, indexed by states. There will be a probability for each state, conditional on the signal. So coordinate \(\theta\) of this posterior belief vector is just the probability of the state being \(\theta\) conditional on \(s\), which we can just write out using Bayes' rule. It's just the prior probability on the state \(\theta\) times the probability of the signal \(s\) given the state \(\theta\) (the first term is determined by the prior, the second term is determined by the signaling scheme), divided by the probability of the signal, that's \(\alpha_s\), which again is non-zero.

So once we have all of these moving parts, we can just apply Bayes' rule and convert the prior into a posterior vector. There's one of these for every signal. Now, the effect of a signaling scheme, from the point of view of what's going to happen, is entirely summarized by the set of posterior beliefs that the signals induce and the probability with which those signals are realized. Nobody cares what the signaling scheme is except insofar as it determines what these two quantities are, because those two quantities determine what the receiver is going to do on every possible signal. They're going to best respond to the posterior, and how frequently they do it is how frequently that signal is going to be sent.

So the signaling scheme is relevant to the utility of both players only insofar as it induces a collection of belief vectors together with a collection of probabilities with which those belief vectors are realized. And so when we want to ask the question, what kind of behaviors can be induced by a signaling scheme, it's enough to understand what kind of belief vectors and probabilities can be induced by a signaling scheme.

13The Mean Posterior Is the Only Restriction

So let's say that a collection of belief vectors together with probability weights over them, \(\alpha_s\), is Bayes plausible if, when I take the weighted average of the posterior belief vectors by their probabilities (I sum up over all of the possible signals the probability of sending that signal times the posterior belief induced by that signal), I just get the prior. So a set of posterior belief vectors together with probabilities associated with them is Bayes plausible if the corresponding weighted sum is just the prior.

So the lemma that is going to make the design problem for the sender in this Bayesian persuasion game tractable for us to think about is the splitting lemma. It says that there is a signaling scheme that will induce a set of signal probabilities and posterior belief vectors if and only if that set of probabilities and vectors is Bayes plausible.

Two directions there. Any signaling scheme will split the prior into posterior pieces, if you like, that average out to the prior. Maybe that's immediate. But the reverse is also true. For any way of chopping up the prior into a bunch of weighted posteriors, so long as they average out to the prior, there actually does exist a signaling scheme that will induce that.

So the reason this is going to make our lives easier is because when we're asking the question of how well a sender can do in this game, this is a question about the supremum over all signaling schemes. A little bit hard to think about. But this lemma is going to reduce that to a question about the supremum over all splits of the prior distribution, which will be more tractable.

Does that lemma make sense? So, two directions. The first is maybe not so surprising; it comes from the definitions. Bayesian posteriors have to average to the prior. The slightly more surprising direction is that this is a complete description: any split can be implemented by a signaling scheme, requiring only Bayes plausibility.

14Necessity: Beliefs Average Back to the Prior

So let's do the forward direction first. Let's fix a state and a signaling scheme. We can compute, just in closed form from Bayes' rule, what is the probability of \(\theta\) given \(s\). Those probabilities collect into this vector \(b_s\), this part of the supposed split of the prior. So by Bayes' rule, it's just the probability of the state under the prior times the probability of the signal given the state under the signaling scheme, divided by the probability of the signal.

Now, what Bayes plausibility means is that when we weight each of these posteriors by the probability of the signal, these should sum up to the prior. So for each of these posteriors, let's weight it by the probability of the signal, \(\alpha_s\). And all that does is cancel out the denominator here in Bayes' rule. And you can already see that both the left-hand side and the right-hand side of this expression are just different ways of specifying the joint probability of seeing a state \(\theta\) and a signal \(s\). I can either look at the probability of the signal times the probability of the state given the signal, or I can look at the probability of the state times the probability of the signal given the state.

Let's just sum over signals. When I sum over signals, well, the prior probability of \(\theta\) does not depend on the signal. That can come outside of the sum. And what's left inside of the sum is the sum over the entire support of a probability distribution, the signaling scheme. It's just one. And so the weighted average of the posteriors is just the prior. So this was the first direction: every signaling scheme indeed induces a split of the prior. We've chopped up the prior into a bunch of weighted posterior parts that fit back together into the prior.

15Construct a Rule from the Desired Posteriors

So how about the other direction? In the other direction, we are given a Bayes plausible split. We've been given, for each signal \(s\), a posterior supposedly induced by that signal and the probability of that signal, and we've checked that indeed these average out to the prior. What we need to demonstrate is that there's actually a signaling scheme, a distribution over signals that we can attach to each state, that will induce this split.

The way we're going to do that: we need to specify explicitly what is the probability of each signal given the state. That's what the signaling scheme is. What we're going to try to do is arrange that the joint probability of the state and the signal is equal to the corresponding part of the split. If we can do that, we're done, because, by the calculation at the end of the last slide, when we sum up over these terms, we get the prior.

So our attempt at doing this will be pretty straightforward. We're going to set the signaling scheme to be what it has to be in order for this to work out, and we just need to verify that these actually define probabilities. So let's set the probability of sending signal \(s\) given \(\theta\) to be \(\alpha_s\) times the posterior probability of \(\theta\) (these are the things that are given to us), divided by the prior probability of \(\theta\). This is what it has to be if this equality is going to be satisfied, because the probability of \(\theta\) and \(s\) happening together is the probability of \(\theta\) under the prior times the probability of \(s\) given \(\theta\). We want it to be the numerator here, and so we have to divide by the prior probability.

So this would make everything work out. If this is a signaling scheme, we're done. But the thing that we might be worried about is that, although we can write down the algebra, this is not in fact a signaling scheme. Maybe these are not valid probabilities. 21A Concave Graph Lies Above Its Chords

That means that, given this posterior split, our hands were tied. There was only one signaling scheme that would induce this pair of posteriors while being Bayes plausible. But it doesn't yet tell us that there's not somehow a more clever split. Maybe I can use three signals and induce some different collection of posteriors that would get higher utility for me. And so I need a way to upper bound what I can get on every possible split.

Let's say I've got distributions over states, \(b\) and \(c\), and I've got some function of these distributions. The function is concave... The way I learned in school to remember the difference between concave and convex: well, concave is the one that looks like a cave. But what does concave mean? It means that if I take any two points and think about linearly interpolating between them, if I evaluate the function at that linear interpolant, that should only be bigger than if I average the function's values at the two points. The function lies above the chord joining any two points on its graph. It's the one that looks like a cave. So it just means if I average points and evaluate the function, I'm going to get a bigger value than if I evaluate the function and average the values.

So maybe I have another function \(v\) that's not concave, but I can talk about a concave upper bound on the function. A concave upper bound on a function \(v\) is just some function \(g\) that, on the one hand, upper bounds \(v\): for every belief \(b\), \(g(b)\) is at least \(v(b)\). And yet \(g\) is concave. That's what it sounds like. It's an upper bound, but it's concave. And I can talk about the smallest possible concave upper bound, and we'll call that the concave envelope of \(v\): the smallest concave function that upper bounds \(v\).

22The Best Disclosure Value Is the Concave Envelope

Let's define the function that we care about here. I'll call this capital \(V(b)\), which is just the optimal obtainable value on the part of the sender. Capital \(V(b)\) is the best expected utility the sender can get, as optimized over all possible signaling schemes or, equivalently because of the splitting lemma, all possible finite splits of the prior that maintain Bayes plausibility. It's the concave envelope of little \(v\). The way we're going to show this is we're going to say every concave function that upper bounds little \(v\) actually also upper bounds big \(V\). Then we're going to show that big \(V\) is itself concave. Which means that since big \(V\) upper bounds little \(v\), is itself upper bounded by any concave function that upper bounds little \(v\), and is itself concave, it must be the minimal concave upper bound.

30Summary

Good. So to summarize, we've now studied this communication game in which the informed party, whom we call the sender, doesn't have the ability to act on their own, but has control over what information they share with the receiver and how they share it. The receiver has the ability to act and has their own incentives. They're not stupid. They're going to correctly condition on the information they receive. And the power the sender therefore has is the power to rationally convince them of things. It's the power to shape their posterior beliefs.

And we've seen that when parties are misaligned, and the sender wants different things than the receiver, they can sometimes exploit the receiver, in the sense that they can strategically withhold information in a way that leads to good outcomes for the sender and bad outcomes for the receiver, although never worse than if the receiver had no information at all.

What we're going to see in a few lectures is how the story changes if the receiver has some choice about who to listen to. Suppose there are senders, our LLM providers say, and there's more than one of them. It's not just Anthropic; there's OpenAI and xAI, and users have the ability to choose which one they use, where they get their information. What we'll see is that the story can change in important ways. In particular, even if all of the parties remain misaligned, meaning none of the senders have the utility function of the receiver, the competition for the receiver's attention can lead to similarly good outcomes as if there had been perfect alignment. We'll talk about that next week.
