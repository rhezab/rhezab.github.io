3Pairwise Robustness and Welfare Ask Different Questions

Okay. So just to refresh a bit from last class, because it's going to play a leading role today. The thing we studied last class, and this was our best idea so far for what to do with pairwise comparison data, is to compute a maximal lottery, which was like a winning strategy in this zero-sum game where we pit our proposed completion distribution against any challenger. A maximal lottery is just a strategy in that game that always wins or at least ties. A maximal lottery was something that says, okay, if we employ this distribution, there's nobody who can come up to us after the fact and say, look, here's a different distribution over completions.

Okay, so a maximal lottery, and we'll call that \(p^N\) in this lecture, N for Nash, because it's the Nash equilibrium of this game, was a distribution over completions such that for all challenger distributions, the fraction of users who think that a randomly drawn completion from our distribution is preferred to a randomly drawn one from the challenger distribution is at least one half. We cannot be defeated in this game.

So this was a nice, satisfying idea for what you might do with pairwise preference data. And it's maybe commercially attractive. It says, okay, there should be nobody who can deploy a competitor that will attract away at least half of my customers. But it's really still this statement about ordinal preferences and has nothing to say at all about the strength of preferences. It could be, for example, that although at least half of the users prefer my product to yours, the people who prefer my product to yours are kind of on the fence. They prefer my product by a little bit on the margin, whereas maybe your customers are true believers. They really prefer yours to mine. This is just saying which way the comparisons come out. It has nothing to do with the strength of those preferences.

And as a result, it doesn't tell us directly about things like social welfare. It doesn't say that, for example, my lottery obtains higher social welfare than yours, because social welfare is about not just orderings of preferences, but strengths of preferences. So today we care about social welfare. We care about strengths of preferences, but annoyingly, we don't have data about that. If we knew everyone's utility function, we could set up an optimization problem. The tricky thing for us is that the only thing we've collected is the pairwise comparison data. And we need to figure out how well we can optimize preferences with that alone.

4A Welfare Comparison Requires a Common Cardinal Scale

Okay. So the model we have is a model of von Neumann–Morgenstern utilities that we saw in the second lecture. Every user in our population of users has some utility function that we'll think of as a vector. It just attaches a number, their cardinal utility for each particular completion, to every completion. So if we have some set \(C\) of possible completions, every user values each completion as some number between 0 and 1; the vector of those numbers is their utility function. And we're going to imagine that there's a population of users that we'll model as a distribution, each with a potentially different utility function. And so the distribution is also a distribution over utility functions.

And the thing we care about is social welfare, or population welfare. What we want to do is make people as happy as possible in the aggregate. Okay, so without knowing which user we're facing today, if we serve some completion \(a\), we'll say that the welfare, the population welfare or the social welfare of that completion, is just the average value, the expected value of the utility that the user has for this completion, where the randomness in the expectation is over the choice of users. The idea is we have a pool of users. We don't know exactly who we're serving today, and so we'll evaluate the welfare of the completion as the average utility that a random person from our population of users will experience for serving that.

5Policy Welfare Averages Over Users and Outputs

And expectations are linear. And so if we ask what is the welfare of a lottery that we deploy, a distribution over completions, there's two sources of randomness here. There's the random choice of completion that we will draw from our lottery, and there's also the random choice of user that will be drawn from our population, and these two sources are independent. And expectations are linear, and so just writing out the welfare of a lottery, which is the expectation jointly over the two independent sources of randomness, over the random user and over the randomly chosen completion drawn from the lottery, we find that this is just an average, weighted by the probability that our lottery places on each completion, of the welfare of each completion on its own. It's linearity of expectation.

And the thing that we would really like to be able to serve to our user population, but is unobtainable to us because we don't know what their utility functions are, is a lottery that maximizes social welfare. If we knew what their utility functions were, then the optimization problem we would want to solve is to find the distribution over completions, the lottery, that amongst all of them maximizes the population welfare of that lottery. The solution to this optimization problem would be always to put all of your weight, all of your mass, on a single completion that had maximum welfare. 6We Observe Noisy Pairwise Choices

So in order to make this practical, we are going to make one important modeling assumption about how these preferences come to us. We've seen this modeling before, but last time we saw it, it was in a more innocuous way. What we're going to assume is that when users are asked to express preferences between completion \(a\) or completion \(b\), they are going to answer randomly according to a Bradley–Terry model.

We saw the Bradley–Terry model before when we talked about training reward models. But there, we weren't assuming that the Bradley–Terry model actually had any bearing on how people answered these comparison questions. We made no assumptions at all there. We just took comparison data as an arbitrary input matrix. We were just trying to fit a Bradley–Terry model to the population of preferences. Here we are doing something different. We're actually going to assume, perhaps wrongly, that when we ask users to express preferences, they actually do answer in a random fashion according to a Bradley–Terry model. We're not assuming that the population preferences follow a Bradley–Terry model. We're not assuming that we can't get these cyclic kinds of examples that we've seen when you aggregate preferences. We're assuming just that individual users, first of all, have utility functions and answer according to a Bradley–Terry model. So the population data of course might have cycles, might still not fit the Bradley–Terry model, but this is the key assumption about the world that is going to go into the theorem we prove in this lecture. If you're going to object to something about the theorem, it's probably this. You might not like this assumption, but this is what it is.

So just to remind, what does it mean to assume that people's choices follow a Bradley–Terry distribution? Well, we're going to assume that users have utility functions that give cardinal utilities for every completion \(a\) and \(b\). And when we ask someone, what do you prefer, \(a\) or \(b\), they sample their response from a distribution, and what that distribution is: the probability that they answer \(a\) is equal to the sigmoid function, this S-shaped function that's exponential in the middle, applied to some scaling \(\beta\) — \(\beta\) is the Bradley–Terry parameter — times the difference in utilities they have for these two options.

So they're more likely to choose the option they have higher utility for, and they are exponentially more likely to choose it in proportion to the gap between those utilities. If the gap becomes very big, they'll choose it with probability approaching one. If the gap becomes very negative, they'll choose it with probability approaching zero. And how sharp this transition is between always choosing \(a\) versus always choosing \(b\) depends on the scale of this parameter \(\beta\). If \(\beta\) is very high, this approaches just a sharp max function; as \(\beta\) is very small, approaching zero, they'll just pick randomly. This is the Bradley–Terry model we saw before, but again, now we're actually going to assume that users behave this way. Okay, so this is the main suspect assumption in the lecture. But does it make sense? Okay.

But again, the presumption here is that individuals follow Bradley–Terry models. It's not that the population does. So if I ask what is the population probability that someone picks \(a\) over \(b\), that doesn't follow a Bradley–Terry model, but we have an expression for it now. It is the expectation, when I pick a random user from the population, that they will pick \(a\) over \(b\), and they will pick it according to a Bradley–Terry model. Okay, so it's the expectation of this sigmoid function applied to the utility difference between \(a\) and \(b\), where the utility difference is the thing that is the random variable here. That's what's being drawn from the population. And the reason why we're going to have to work a little bit is because of the non-linearity of the sigmoid function. The random variable here is inside this sigmoid function and the expectation is outside of it.

Okay. If I wanted to dot all the i's and cross all the t's, the distribution would be this object that exists in the ether and all you have are samples from it. Okay, so the starting point for what we want to do, the data that we have for the problem we want to solve, as in previous lectures, is directly this comparison matrix \(P\). 7Distortion Measures Welfare Lost by the Rule

Okay. And so, again, if we knew people's utility functions, we would just say, all right, what I'm going to do is maximize social welfare. I'm going to find the lottery that maximizes social welfare. But because the comparison data doesn't identify utility functions, we can't do that. And so the best we can do is to hope for some kind of approximation. So we will evaluate policies \(p\) according to their distortion. This is like an approximation ratio.

The distortion of a policy is a ratio. In the numerator, it is the welfare of the welfare-optimal policy tailored to that population. The thing in the numerator is the thing we would really like to achieve. We don't know what the utility functions are, so we can't achieve that. But the maximum value of the social welfare, if only you knew everything, is this thing in the numerator. It's the thing that optimizes social welfare. On the other hand, our policy achieves the thing in the denominator. So this distortion factor is going to be bigger than one, because our welfare is only going to be smaller than the optimum. But we would like to make the distortion factor as small as possible.

What's going on is, we've got this population data that doesn't determine what the utility functions are, but it does constrain them. It rules out some of them. There are some collections of utility functions that are not consistent with the comparison data we've gathered. And so the way we're going to evaluate a policy that we come up with is going to be its worst-case distortion: in the worst case over distributions that are consistent with the data we have, of the optimal welfare for that distribution in the numerator, divided by the welfare of our lottery in the denominator.

Okay, so we'll say that, for example, an algorithm for taking preference data and computing a lottery has a distortion factor of, say, 10, if we can promise that in the worst case over what the distributions are, we never do more than a factor of 10 worse on the utility functions that we've been given compared to what would have been welfare optimal for them. The smaller the distortion factor, the better. Does the measure make sense? A worst-case approximation ratio. Yeah?

8A Linear Rule Would Reveal Welfare Differences

So in some sense, you might wonder: is it really the case that the data doesn't identify the utility functions? If we assumed nothing about how the preference data was generated, or if we assumed that people deterministically chose their favorite of the two outcomes, then you could give these examples where I say, well, two people who both prefer \(a\) over \(b\) give me the same response, but person one likes \(a\) way more than \(b\), person two likes \(a\) only a little bit more than \(b\), so there's no way to tell them apart. But now I've told you that people answer according to this Bradley–Terry model. So it's a little bit less clear that the data doesn't identify the utility functions. If I could ask a single person over and over again which they prefer, \(a\) or \(b\), then I would identify at least the utility differences for all of the pairs, which would, up to some affine transform, identify the utility functions.

The reason why our data doesn't identify the utility functions is a little bit more subtle than that. It's because I don't get to ask the same person over and over again. I get basically to ask each person one question, or maybe I get to ask people multiple questions, but the comparisons that I get are not labeled by the annotator who provided them. They're just comparisons, right? I only have samples from a larger population. That's the reason.

Now, even that wouldn't be a problem if the comparison model I used was linear rather than going through the sigmoid function, because expectations are linear. Okay, so just to drive that point home, suppose my comparison model didn't have a sigmoid function. Suppose it was linear. Then, when I looked at the fraction of people who preferred \(a\) over \(b\), which would just be the expectation of this linear utility difference now, an expectation over a random draw from the population, well, I can distribute expectations across the terms of a linear function. 9The Sigmoid Can Hide Welfare Differences

The reason why we have to work a bit, and the reason why the distortion factor is not going to be just one, is exactly because the Bradley–Terry model is not linear. The probability that the population prefers \(a\) over \(b\) is the expectation over the population of the sigmoid of their utility differences. The expectation's outside the sigmoid, the utility functions are inside the sigmoid, and this does not equal, in general, the sigmoid applied to the population difference. I can always invert the sigmoid. The sigmoid's an invertible function, but inverting the sigmoid does not give me the welfare, because there's a difference between taking the expectation over utilities outside the sigmoid function and inside the sigmoid function.

Okay, and this failure can be pretty severe. We'll see this when we prove a lower bound: this doesn't even tell me the sign of the population-wide utility difference. The sign of the population-wide utility difference can be different from what the fraction of people who prefer \(a\) over \(b\) suggests. And we'll exploit this when we prove limits on what is possible for this problem of minimizing distortion.

But still, thinking about how easy things would be if only the preference model was linear gives us some idea of what we want to do. Expectations play well with linear functions. And so what we're going to do is bound the actual preference function by the tightest linear upper and lower bounds that we can. We're going to use the fact that expectations distribute over linear functions, and we'll chug through the algebra to end up getting a tight approximation. This is the basic idea.

21One Comparison Gives the Entire Welfare Guarantee

And it wins against any other lottery, so we can choose to compare it to any one of them. In the analysis, the lottery that we will compare it to is the lottery that maximizes social welfare for the underlying population. We don't know what that is, of course. And we don't need to know what that is to compute the maximal lottery. This is entering only in the analysis.

Okay, the maximal lottery wins against all other challenger lotteries, in particular whatever happens to be the welfare-optimal one. The maximal lottery condition says that its expected margin, when battled against the welfare-optimal lottery, is non-negative.
