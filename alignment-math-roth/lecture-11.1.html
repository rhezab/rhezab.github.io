3Overview

Okay. And so there's two results that I want to talk about in this lecture, or maybe this lecture and the next, depending on how far we get. The first is a classical result in statistics and economics called Aumann's agreement theorem, which speaks to what perfect Bayesian agents with different observations can agree on just by exchanging predictions, not directly exchanging their observations. We'll get a simple, very stylized theorem, but it will say that just from conversations about beliefs, not conversations about observations, two parties can come to accuracy-improving agreement about any prediction problem.

But ultimately, I don't want to talk about perfect Bayesians. It's difficult to be a perfect Bayesian. You need to have a prior over all states of the world. We'll see here you need a joint prior over observations that you and the model might make, together with how they correlate with the outcome. And even if I had that information, this prior belief, the computational problem of correctly updating your beliefs as a Bayesian is generally quite difficult.

So we're going to try to get the nice conclusion of Aumann's agreement theorem, that two parties can agree on the probability of any event just by having a conversation about their beliefs, but we're going to try to recover a stronger, tractable version of that theorem where we don't need to make Bayesian assumptions. We don't need to assume, for example, that there's any prior at all, let alone a common prior. We don't need to assume any computationally difficult task like Bayesian updating. And we'd nevertheless like to conclude that conversations about beliefs converge, not just in the limit, but in fact very quickly, in a way that only improves accuracy. And we'll see how to do that with a variant of the kinds of calibration definitions we've been talking about.

And maybe unsurprisingly, we'll see that rather than needing to engage in the implausible computational feats that a Bayesian would engage in when updating, the kind of conditions we need to recover this fast agreement can be obtained tractably just using what's becoming our favorite tool, Blackwell approachability. Okay, so that's the game plan for today.

6Knowledge and Common Knowledge

Okay, so we need to talk about knowledge and common knowledge. Common knowledge is kind of confusing. Aumann's theorem is about common knowledge. When we get to the efficient dynamics, we'll realize we actually didn't need to have common knowledge, which seems like this really strong thing. We didn't even need to have common priors. But let me, just to walk you through everything in the historically correct order, tell you about knowledge and common knowledge.

So, what does it mean to know that an event \(F\), say, occurs when the state is \(\omega\)? Remember, if I knew what the state was, I could just check if \(\omega\) was an element of \(F\) or not. But I don't observe the state directly. I only observe the element of the information partition that contains the state. Even if \(\omega\) is in \(F\), I still don't necessarily know that the event \(F\) occurred, because the information partition element that contains the state \(\omega\) could contain some states like \(\omega\) that are elements of the event, but it could also contain other states that are not. And if it does, then I'd be uncertain about whether the event occurs. But if every state in the information partition element containing \(\omega\) is contained within the event, causes the event to be true, then at state \(\omega\), I actually do know that the event occurs. And that's what we mean when we say that at a state \(\omega\), an agent knows that the event has occurred: \(I_i(\omega) \subseteq F\).

Okay, does that make sense? It's just that your posterior belief has to be 100% if the prior was correct, which requires that not only is the true state an element of the event, but all of the states that you cannot distinguish from the true state because of the information partition also cause the event to be true. Does that make sense?

Okay, now I might know that the event is true, you might know that the event is true. That means we both have knowledge of the event, but it doesn't yet mean that we have common knowledge of the event. Common knowledge means not only that I have knowledge of the event and you have knowledge of the event, but that you know that I have knowledge of the event, and I know that you have knowledge of the event. And not just that, but you know that I know that you know that I have knowledge of the event, and I know that you know that I know that you have knowledge of the event. And not just that, but that you know that I know that you know that I know that you know that I know that you have knowledge of the event, and so on and so forth. So common knowledge requires this infinite recursion of me knowing that you know that I know that you know, and so forth.

Yeah. So how do you reason about this? Neither of us know what the state is. You know that the state is an element of your information partition, from your observations. And you also know the structure of my information partition. You know what I would observe at every state. Okay, so how do we formally define common knowledge, and why does it correspond to this infinite chain of you know that I know that you know that I know?

Okay, so first of all, let's think about how I would know that you know something. Forget about the infinite recursion. What does it mean that I know that you know something? Well, first of all, I don't know what the state is. If I knew what the state was, I would know whether or not you knew something, because I would know that you know that the state is in the element of your information partition containing the state. And then I could just check: does every state in your part of the information partition satisfy the event? Now, I don't know what the state is. What I know is that the state is one of the states in my part of the information partition. So I have a collection of possibilities for what the state is. For each of those possibilities, I know what part of your information partition this possibility is in. And if all of your parts of the information partition that are consistent with my part of the information partition cause the event to be true, then I know that you know it. And to say that I know that you know that I know, we have to iterate this chain of reasoning.

So here's a concise way to express that iterated reasoning. Let's imagine building a graph. The vertices of the graph are the states of the world. And I will draw an edge between two states in this graph if either party is unable to distinguish these states. If states \(\omega_1\) and \(\omega_2\) are part of the same element of the information partition for me, I'll draw an edge between them. And I'll also draw an edge between them if they're part of the same element of the information partition for you.

Okay, so let's say that the edges corresponding to my information partition are blue and the edges corresponding to your information partition are red. Okay, so the real state's \(\omega\). I look at everything that is connected to it with blue edges. That's the set of states that are possible according to my knowledge. Now I look at everything that is connected to those with red edges. Those are the set of states that, as far as I know, might be possible beliefs, non-zero beliefs, for you. And more generally, as we iterate this chain of reasoning, what do I know that you know that I know, we have to extend that out one further level. Look at all of the blue edges one level out. And if we turn this crank an infinite number of times, expanding out one level at a time, what we get to is the connected component in this graph that the true state of the world, \(\omega\), is a member of.

So, right, we're going to build this graph: connect two states in the graph whenever either agent, the human being or the AI, cannot distinguish them. And let's say that \(D(\omega)\) is the connected component in this graph that contains \(\omega\). Then an event \(F\) is common knowledge if the whole connected component \(D(\omega)\) is contained in \(F\), if every state in this connected component causes the event to be true. And the reason why this is the correct technical definition for this notion that I know that you know that I know that you know, is because each alternation of "I know that you know" [means] I'm looking at what are the possible states that you could believe in. And I have to look at all of the states that I believe in and then all of the states that are indistinguishable to you from those states, which is expanding out one additional layer in this graph. And the connected component is just what this converges to when we expand out through everything. Does that make sense?

Student: And after expanding out, there could be some cases that we know.

Roth: That's right. So the graph doesn't have to be connected. There's different connected components. In the trivial case, all that is common knowledge is what we knew before making any observations at all. But we don't have to be in the trivial case, because this knowledge graph doesn't have to be connected. Makes sense?

So this is the formal definition of common knowledge: that the connected component in this knowledge graph is contained within the event, \(D(\omega) \subseteq F\).

8Aumann's Agreement Theorem

Okay. So what is Aumann's agreement theorem? I started off talking about how we could come to agreement by talking about our beliefs. Aumann's agreement theorem on its own doesn't have any dynamics in it. We'll bring the talking back in shortly. But Aumann's agreement theorem is about common knowledge.

It says: suppose we've got two Bayesian agents, and they start with a common prior. But they have different observations. They have different information partitions. Then a state is realized, and each party observes the element of the information partition it belongs to. If things stop there, they can have different posterior beliefs about the event, because they have different observations. But suppose that they actually had common knowledge of each other's beliefs. That is, you have common knowledge that the AI believes the event \(E\) has some probability \(\alpha\) of occurring, and that the human being believes that there's some probability \(\beta\) of the event occurring. Suppose these posterior probabilities are common knowledge. Then Aumann's agreement theorem concludes that the beliefs have to be the same: \(\alpha = \beta\).

So it's saying that disagreement cannot survive common knowledge of each other's beliefs. Sometimes the tagline for this theorem is that Bayesians cannot agree to disagree. Of course, a priori, I have different beliefs than you, because I have different observations than you. But the only way we can sustain that disagreement is if somehow we don't fully understand each other's beliefs. If we had common knowledge of each other's beliefs, then our beliefs would have to be the same. That's what Aumann's agreement theorem is saying. It's actually a very short proof. We'll prove it in one slide. But I want to make sure we understand. Yes?

Student: This is basically saying, and I know that this isn't the exact application, but if you had an AI and a human and they were talking about their beliefs for an infinite amount of time, then eventually they would come [to agreement].

Roth: Yeah, good. So we'll prove this, and then we'll quickly connect it back to conversations. But how would you come to common knowledge of each other's beliefs? Well, one way would be to have a conversation. I could say, well, here's what I believe. And then you could think about that, and you could come back and say, well, here's what I believe. And I could think about that and tell you, here's what I believe.

The reason why this isn't totally trivial is because as soon as I tell you what I believe, it's now common knowledge, because I just said it, what I believed last round. But in telling you that, since you're a Bayesian, you might now believe something different. You tell that to me, which would cause me to update my beliefs. We would now have common knowledge about what you believed last round and what I believed two rounds ago. But that's not necessarily common knowledge about what we believe at the moment. But in the infinite limit of this conversation, we reach common knowledge. And in fact, because we've assumed the state space is finite, you don't really even need the infinite limit. We'll reach common knowledge of our current beliefs, we'll see, after a number of iterations at most the number of states, which could be really big, but finite.

So the first way we'll connect this back to conversations is by observing that really, really long conversations, in which we just pass back and forth our current beliefs, will lead to this common knowledge. For perfect Bayesians, at least, if we have honest conversations about our beliefs, where we never share evidence, only beliefs, we will eventually come to agreement. Good. 9Which Event Is Common Knowledge?

So remember, through these information partitions, and we wrote down what the expression was for conditional probability, for any state of the world we can write down what is the AI's belief that the event occurs when that's the state of the world that is realized, and what is the human's belief when that's the state of the world that's realized. It comes from conditioning, for alternately the human or the AI, on the information partition element that contains the realized state of the world. So for every state of the world, there's a number associated with it, which is the AI's posterior belief, and the human's posterior belief.

And so the event that we are assuming in the hypothesis of the theorem to be common knowledge is that whatever the state of the world is, it is in this set that causes the AI to believe that the probability of the event is \(\alpha\) and the human to believe that the probability of the event is \(\beta\): $\(F := \{\omega' \in \Omega : p_A(\omega') = \alpha,\ p_H(\omega') = \beta\}.\)$ Okay, so the premise of this theorem is that this well-defined set is common knowledge. So remember, what's the definition of common knowledge? Whatever the state actually is, it has produced some connected component in this indistinguishability graph, and the assumption is that this connected component is a subset of \(F\). That's what it means to know, through common knowledge, that \(F\) has occurred. [We] know that every state in the common-knowledge connected component causes the AI's posterior belief to be \(\alpha\) and the human's posterior belief to be \(\beta\).

10Both Probabilities Average to the Same Number

And now we're just going to write out a simple conditional probability in two different ways. So remember we observed that this common knowledge cell \(D\) can be written alternately as a disjoint union of some of the AI's information cells, or as a disjoint union of some of the human's information cells. And so we're just going to write out the law of iterated expectations, basically, in both of those two ways.

For each of these information cells, the AI has some posterior belief, the probability of the event given the information cell. And because we know that this common knowledge event is contained in \(F\), we know that for all of these information cells, the AI's posterior belief is \(\alpha\). The law of total probability says: all right, what is the probability of the event \(E\), given that the true state is in this common knowledge connected component \(D\)? Well, \(D\) is just a disjoint union of some of the AI's information cells. So let's just sum, over all of the AI's information cells that are contained in \(D\), the probability of that information cell given \(D\) times the probability of the event given the information cell: $\(\mu(E \mid D) = \sum_{I \in \mathcal{I}_A :\, I \subseteq D} \mu(I \mid D)\,\mu(E \mid I).\)$ It's just a way to write out the probability of the event given \(D\), since \(D\) is a disjoint union of these things. And I don't know specifically what the probability of any of these information cells is, conditional on \(D\), but I do know that they sum to 1, because \(D\) is a disjoint union of all of them, and so one of them has to be realized. And I know that, because of the common knowledge assumption, the probability of the event given each of these information cells is exactly \(\alpha\). And so that means that this just sums up to \(\alpha\). The probability of \(E\), the event that we care about, given the common knowledge connected component, is \(\alpha\).

But the roles of the AI and the human being here are entirely symmetric. I could have performed this calculation by instead decomposing the probability of the event as a function of the human being's information cells. And then I would have concluded that the probability of the event given \(D\) is \(\beta\). But the probability of the event given \(D\) is just some number defined by the prior. And so if it's equal to both \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\), the only way that can be the case is if \(\alpha\) equals \(\beta\).

Okay, so that's it. That's Aumann's agreement theorem. The proof is extremely simple, but it is saying something interesting, and we'll pull on the thread of why it is interesting by thinking about the dynamics of how you get to common knowledge. But it's saying: Bayesians who really understand each other's beliefs (they haven't seen each other's evidence, they just really understand each other's beliefs, in the sense that each other's beliefs are common knowledge) must agree. They cannot disagree. Make sense?

Okay. So I think if you think about it enough, Aumann's theorem is kind of cool, but it certainly leaves quite a bit to be desired. First of all, it's just talking about common knowledge. It's not talking about dynamics at all. So why would you have common knowledge? How do you get to common knowledge? We'll address that in a moment. But maybe more fundamentally, you might say, okay, fine, but this is just some reductio ad absurdum for imagining that people are Bayesians. You're really abusing the premise of being a Bayesian when you start talking about common knowledge, which somehow requires this infinite recursion of Bayesian updates.

The other, maybe more fundamental, criticism you might have of Aumann's agreement theorem is that it speaks only to perfect Bayesians, which you might consider to be both epistemically and computationally implausible. Epistemically, I mean, where are you even getting the prior from? It's a complicated object. It's a prior over my observations, the AI's observations, and the outcome. And even if I had that, how could I perform these complicated Bayesian updates? Those are criticisms of this whole premise of modeling computational agents as Bayesians, and if Aumann's theorem spoke only to perfect Bayesians, that would be a major limitation. These are the ways in which we would like to strengthen this story. Does that make sense?

11Can Conversation Create Common Knowledge?

And so first, I just want to think about how you get to common knowledge. We'll strip away the Bayesian part later. But what is a dynamic story for how you even get to common knowledge? And as we hinted at earlier, the dynamic story will be about simple but possibly long conversations.

Okay, so I make my observations, the AI makes its observations, and at the moment, we don't have common knowledge of anything, and we have different posteriors because we have different observations. But what's going to happen is we are going to engage in a conversation, a boring, long conversation. I will simply, at every round, express my posterior belief to the AI. And then the AI will express their posterior belief to me. And we'll go on in this way. And the reason why the conversation doesn't just immediately end is because [when you] tell me your posterior belief, this causes me, potentially, to update my belief, because I could think about all of the things you could have seen that would have caused you to have the sequence of beliefs you have expressed to me over the course of this conversation.

So don't worry for now about how you'd actually compute these posterior updates. In principle they're well defined for a perfect Bayesian, but they'd be horrendously complicated to compute. We'll do away with that horrendous computation in a minute. But let's just think about this conversation, which in principle could happen. Does the conversation make sense? 14We Want Fast Approximate Agreement

Okay. So, right. Aumann's theorem initially is about common knowledge. Now we see, okay, it's about the limiting behavior of conversations about beliefs. But there's still quite a bit to be desired. First of all, the conversations are not short. All this argument established is that the length of the conversation before agreement is no longer than the cardinality of the state space, but in a rich state space that could be really, really big. And that's even if everyone's a perfect Bayesian. Of course, we can't actually expect that of any person, and we can't implement that on a machine. And it's not just a computational problem, it's an information problem. We don't have correct prior beliefs over complicated state spaces. We might not even think that it made sense to think about a prior. Maybe we think about operating in a dynamic world in which there is no distribution, right? We want to figure out how to have a version of Aumann's agreement theorem without making any of these assumptions, and we'd like to have very short conversations. And so that's what we're going to try to do next.

And so towards this, we will relax a little bit what we mean by agreement. Okay, so Aumann's theorem, when it says agreement, it means agreement to decimal precision. If I think there's a 93.21659% chance of rain, and you think there's a 93.21658% chance of rain, that doesn't count. We don't agree. I want that to count. So I'm going to talk about \(\varepsilon\)-approximate agreement. And that just means we're going to high-five and call it a day if our beliefs are within \(\varepsilon\) of one another. Okay, so a 91.2% chance of rain will count as agreeing with a 91.3% chance of rain if \(\varepsilon\) is greater than 0.1.

And we're still going to think about the same kind of communication pattern. I'll tell you my belief, you'll tell me your belief, I'll tell you my belief, and so on and so forth, until we report numbers in adjacent rounds that are within \(\varepsilon\) of one another. But what it means to tell you my belief will be something much weaker than that it's a correct Bayesian posterior. It just means that it is some number that satisfies some weak conditions I will tell you about, that would be satisfied by a Bayesian. So if you were a Bayesian, you would satisfy these calibration conditions that I'm going to tell you about, but there are many other ways to satisfy them that don't require doing anything computationally impossible. So it's a relaxation or generalization of Bayesian rationality, in that if you believe everyone's a Bayesian, we're going to recover a stronger, quantitative version of Aumann's agreement theorem, but the theorem will speak to a much more general and tractable category of behaviors than just correct Bayesian reasoning.
