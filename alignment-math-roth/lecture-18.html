2Overview

So last class we were talking about AI oversight, and one of the morals that we saw from that lecture was that combining models can help. If different reviewers have complementary objectives, then they can help us steer the ensemble towards not-harmful outcomes. And we're going to take this moral and deliver a little bit of bad news today.

So the question that we're going to be asking is: can we expect these helpful ensembles? Can we expect commercially available models to supply this kind of helpful diversity? For instance, if I'm a potential model provider and I see an opportunity for arbitrage on a market, say I see the opportunity to ensemble together three different models and get a better-performing service, then I might reasonably expect that I, as this potential provider, am going to package this combination, sell the service, and make money. So the idea is that competition can therefore absorb these cheap improvements into the menu of services that we already have available on the market. So this is a hypothesis about what happens in real markets.

And then our question is going to be: when accurate models can't be improved by simple ensembles (for this lecture, we're going to be looking at a simple kind of voting), how must these errors concentrate? And specifically, we're going to show that these errors concentrate on a common set of questions, which means that adding more models might not necessarily supply these helpful complementary judgments.

3When Does Combining Models Improve Accuracy?

So we're going to call any procedure that combines several models' answers into one an ensemble. Ensembles can be a lot of different kinds of things. They can be things like voting rules, which we're going to look at for this lecture. But they can also be things like routing. Routing is another kind of ensemble, where we look at the query and then we select from our pool of models.

So the thing we're going to focus on for this lecture is: if simple combinations actually can't outperform the best service at the same total cost, what does that imply about the pattern of the models' mistakes?

4Independent Errors Make Voting More Reliable

This is a toy example to get some intuition. Suppose we have really good complementarity. So suppose we have five models, and for this setting let's consider five binary predictors. So the right answer is just plus one or minus one, and each model decides which answer it wants to predict. Let's suppose that we have five models and they're all wrong on 10% of the questions from the same population.

If we suppose that their errors are mutually independent, so they're basically generated by, say, rolling a ten-sided die every time to decide whether they get the question wrong, then they're maximally uncorrelated, which means that we can actually ensemble these models together to get a much better-performing model just by simply taking the majority vote. We can just combinatorially compute what the new accuracy is going to be. And this is just because, in order for this majority-vote model to get a question wrong, it needs to be the case that at least three of the models out of these five individually got it wrong.

5Identical Errors Leave Voting No Better

On the other end of the spectrum, if their errors are maximally correlated, so if they make exactly the same errors, then of course we would expect that taking a majority vote on these five models is not going to help, because every time any of the models gets a question wrong, all the models get the question wrong, and therefore a majority vote adds nothing.

9Model: Binary Answers on a Fixed Population

Okay, cool. So we're going to jump into the model that we're going to look at for today. The Arena score was nice in that it convinces us that this Pareto frontier is something that actual benchmarks are thinking about. But for simplicity, we're going to look at binary predictors. So we're going to be drawing our questions \(x\) from some finite set \(\mathcal{X}\). And each of these questions \(x\) is going to have a fixed correct binary label that's either minus one or plus one. And our models are just going to be functions that take input questions and output a label deterministically. And for each model, we're going to write its population error as the probability that it gets the question wrong, \(\mathrm{err}(h) = P(h(X) \neq Y)\).

Yeah, and just a small note about AI oversight, just to ground our intuition a little bit: we can think about each question as a proposed action, and its true label is whether or not it's going to harm the principal, and each model is maybe either an inaccurate or a slightly misaligned model that predicts whether or not it's going to cause harm.

10What Accuracy Is Available at Budget B?

We're also going to define \(\alpha(B)\) to be the minimum error, or the best possible accuracy, that we can achieve at budget \(B\). It's defined exactly how you would think it is: it's just the minimum, over all models that are affordable at this budget \(B\), of their error. And since we're in this binary prediction case, we're also going to include for free a class of models, the two constant predictors. One of them always predicts plus one regardless of what the input question is, the other one always predicts minus one, just because these are free models that anyone can always use. And because of this, we know that our \(\alpha\) is always going to be at most one-half, because if we're above one-half, then one of the constant predictors could do better just by predicting a constant.

11Threshold Votes Count Positive Answers

So we're going to be looking at a specific class of votes, and these are going to be threshold votes. So suppose we have \(k\) models, and suppose that if we add up all of their costs, they're jointly affordable at our budget \(B\). If they satisfy that condition, we're just going to call them jointly affordable. And let's let \(A(x)\) count how many models answer plus one. So that's pretty straightforward. For any threshold \(q\), we're going to define the vote \(v_q(x)\): we're going to vote plus one if at least \(q\) of the models say plus one, and otherwise we're going to say minus one.

So again, to ground intuition: if there are \(k\) models, there are \(k\) such possible threshold votes. The most generous voting rule is \(v_1\), which only requires one positive answer. So it says: if any of you guys think that the answer should be plus one, we're also going to vote plus one. And the strictest rule is going to be \(v_k\), which requires that every single model in our collection votes plus one in order to vote plus one. And this also includes things like strict majority, which just uses the value \(q = \lfloor k/2 \rfloor + 1\). Yeah, and all of these votes just cost the sum of the costs of all of the models individually.

And as a concrete example, say we have five models. This top table here is showing us, for each question in the column, what their response is. The bottom rows are going to be the responses of each of our five thresholds. And maybe one observation to keep in our back pocket: if we look at each of the questions, like question \(x_2\) for instance, on question \(x_2\) three of our models said plus one. And that also means that on question \(x_2\), exactly three of our voting rules are also going to say plus one. And this should probably make some intuitive sense, because if three models say plus one, then \(v_1\) is happy, \(v_2\) is happy, and \(v_3\) is also happy.

14Assume No Threshold Vote Beats the Budget Benchmark

Okay, awesome. So this is where we're going to bring in our efficient-market hypothesis assumption. In broad strokes, the assumption that we're going to make is that none of these voting rules is going to allow us to beat the Pareto frontier at this budget. So what this is saying is that the population error of every one of these \(k\) possible voting rules is going to be at least \(\alpha\). So it's saying that no matter how you pool these models and aggregate their votes using one of these \(k\) threshold votes, you're not going to be able to achieve less than \(\alpha\) error.

15How Often Can Any Model Be Wrong?

And okay, so to think about this, the object we're going to be interested in is the union: the set of questions on which any of the models makes a mistake. So you can think of it as: each model has its set of error questions \(E_j\), and we're just going to be interested in a set \(U\), which is just the union of all of these. We're also going to define \(\delta_j\) to be the excess error that each model makes. So this is just going to be the population error of model \(j\) minus the, quote unquote, frontier error that we're trying to beat, \(\alpha\).

16Theorem: Errors Concentrate

And then what we're going to prove, based on this assumption that none of these threshold votes is able to beat \(\alpha\) error, is a bound on the size of this union. So what we're going to say is that if every threshold vote of jointly affordable models has error that's at least \(\alpha\), then the size of this union of errors is going to be less than or equal to \(2\alpha\) plus this sum of the excess errors, the residual errors of all of the models in your collection: \(P(U) \le 2\alpha + \sum_j \delta_j\).

And just to ground our intuition a little bit: this theorem is most meaningful when our bound is tight, when the size of the union of errors is small. And this is intuitive because it's saying that the set of questions on which any model makes a mistake is pretty small. So outside of this set of questions, no model makes a mistake. So for instance, if we have five models, each with error \(\alpha = 10\%\), and let's say there's no excess error, then our bound is 20%. So all five of these models err on the same 20% of the benchmark.

17Threshold Votes Preserve the Error Count

So one of the observations that we're going to be making, which we saw visually a couple of slides ago, is just this observation that the threshold votes also preserve the error count. So to think about this a little bit: fixing one question, if we have \(k\) models and \(r\) out of these \(k\) models give positive answers, then that also means that \(r\) out of these \(k\) threshold votes are going to give positive answers. Which implies that if we take the population error of the models and sum these together across the different models, the population error summed over the threshold votes is also going to be the same: \(\sum_q \mathrm{err}(v_q) = \sum_j \mathrm{err}(h_j)\).

This is just saying algebraically what we observed with the plot: for each column, the number of pluses and minuses is the same. And therefore, for the entire benchmark, the number of errors that the threshold votes make is collectively the same as the number of errors that the models make.

18The Two Extreme Thresholds Detect Every Error

So to put our proof through, we're going to consider these two extreme threshold rules. This is again the most permissive rule, which only needs one plus one to vote plus one, and also the most strict rule, which needs unanimity in order to vote plus one. And we're going to say that whenever one of these models is wrong, at least one of these two votes is wrong.

So we can just step through this logic a little bit. If we have a question whose true label is plus one, and at least one of the models gets it wrong, then we don't have unanimity. So the most strict rule, \(v_k\), is going to answer negative one, and \(v_k\) is going to be wrong. On the other hand, symmetrically, if we have a question whose true label is minus one and some model is wrong and answers plus one, that means that the most permissive rule is going to say, "Hey, one of these models answered plus one, therefore I'm going to vote plus one also." And therefore this most permissive vote, \(v_1\), is going to get this question wrong.

So in set notation, this just means that whenever a question is in the set \(U\), whenever it's a question that at least one model gets wrong, at least one of these two votes is wrong. So just by the simple union bound, this gives us that the size of the union is going to be no more than the population error of our most permissive rule plus the population error of our unanimous rule.

25Weighted Votes Generalize Threshold Votes

Yeah, so this is going back to [the student's] question about weighted votes and generalizing the notion of threshold votes. So as I was saying, one of the critiques that you might have of the error concentration theorem is that there's this annoying term, \(2\alpha + \sum_j \delta_j\). And this term grows a lot with the models' excess errors. And at a certain point, it doesn't really tell you anything meaningful about the distribution of errors anymore. It will just tell you, "Oh, all the errors lie inside the benchmark," which is not a very interesting statement. So we might ask: can we make a stronger assumption and thus get rid of that term? And it turns out that we can.

So we're going to generalize our class of votes from just these threshold votes to the broader class of affine weighted votes. And the way each of these votes works is we're going to assign a weight \(a_j\) to each model \(j\). \(a_j\) is a real number. We don't require that the \(a\)'s sum up to one or anything. It can also be negative. So we assign a weight \(a_j\) to each of these models, sum them all up, and we also have an intercept \(b\) that we just choose. So once we aggregate this and get our score for a question, \(g(x)\), we're just going to predict whatever the sign of this \(g(x)\) is. So if \(g(x)\) is five, we're going to predict plus one. If it's negative 0.4, we're going to predict minus one. If \(g(x)\) is zero, then that's indicating that we are unable to come to consensus for whatever reason; we're indecisive between plus one and minus one. [We count that] as an error as well.

So the corresponding assumption we're going to make here, this is our stronger assumption, is that every weighted vote of any collection of jointly affordable models is going to have error that's at least \(\alpha\). And this is strictly stronger than the assumption that we made for the error concentration bound, because the threshold voting rules that we used for that theorem are a special case of these general weighted votes. [If you set the weights to one and the offset to \(b = k - 2q + 1\),] then you recover a threshold vote \(v_q\).

26A Hard Core: Where Every Model Is at Chance

And then in our error concentration bound, the object of interest that we were looking at was the union of the sets of errors that each model has. Here our object of interest is going to be this thing called a hard core. What that means is: you can think of a hard core as a weighting \(w\) across the entire benchmark. So \(w\) assigns a weight between 0 and 1 to every question in the benchmark. So for instance, one special type of weighting is just a subset of the benchmark: every question in the subset has weight one, and every [other question has weight zero].

27Theorem: A Common Hard Core of Size 2α

Okay, cool. So the theorem that we're going to try to prove is that if every weighted vote of a collection of jointly affordable models has error that's at least \(\alpha\), then these models also have a hard core of size \(2\alpha\).

And you'll notice that previously, for the error concentration bound, we were trying to get a small set of errors. It was more interesting if the union of the errors was a small part of the benchmark. Here, we want the hard core size to be large, because that's a more interesting statement. It's a more interesting statement to say that on a larger portion of the benchmark, every one of these binary classifiers is exactly at chance.

You'll also notice that the lower bound that we provide here, the size of \(2\alpha\), does not include the perhaps irritating term of the summation of the excess errors of all the individual models. So this is a result that holds regardless of how bad your models are going to be. And it's uniform for every possible jointly affordable collection.

28Proof Sketch, Step 1: Cores Are Bounded by Hinge Loss

Okay, cool. So to get some intuition for how we're going to proceed, we're just going to observe that the size of the hard core is bounded by the minimum hinge loss.

So for notation purposes, we're just going to write the signed score of a weighted vote as \(m(x)\). It's just going to be the true label times the score that we assign to it. So if we assign a score \(g(x)\) of 0.6 to a question, and it turns out that the question's true label is plus one, that's great: the signed score is 0.6. On the other hand, if it turns out that the question's true label is minus one, then that's not so great. We got the question wrong, and our signed score is going to be minus 0.6. So in other words, our vote is going to get a question right exactly when this signed score is strictly positive.

So those two conditions that I mentioned on the last slide, the label balance condition and also the condition that every single model is at chance, we can write as follows. The first is that the expectation of \(w\) times \(Y\) is equal to zero. Let's just consider the case where \(w\) is an actual subset of the benchmark, so all the weights on questions are zero or one. Then \(E[wY] = 0\) is just saying that if you take all of the questions that are included in our subset and you sum their true labels, they'll cancel out. So we have the same number of questions that have true label plus one as we have questions that have true label minus one.

And the second condition that we want is that every model is at chance. We can write that similarly, as the expectation of \(wYh_j\) is equal to zero. So consider the term \(Y\) times \(h_j\): that's just the true label times the model's response of plus or minus one. If the model gets it right, then \(Y h_j\) is one. If the model gets it wrong, then \(Y h_j\) is minus one. So if we do this for every question and weight by \(w\), then we require that on average we get zero.

So these are a couple of moment conditions that we have. And then additionally, because I like linearity of expectation, we can also [say] that the expectation of \(w\) times \(Yg\), where \(g\) is again [the score of any weighted vote], is zero. And then since that's zero, [we can write \(E[w] = E[w(1 - Yg)]\), which is at most the hinge loss of \(g\)]. So the size of the hard core is upper bounded by the hinge loss that any score achieves. But we actually want to prove equality. And we can prove equality by writing our problem down as an LP.

29Proof Sketch: The Core Is a Linear Program

So our primal is going to be this problem of maximizing the size of the hard core. We want to maximize \(E[w]\) subject to a couple of constraints. [The first] are the constraints on the weights being valid. The second constraint is just label balance. The third constraint is the constraint holding every model at chance. And we don't have to go through the algebra, but if we then derive the dual using these multipliers, then it turns out that this dual is this problem of finding the smallest hinge loss. And at the optimum, this \(\xi\) is going to be [the larger of] \(1 - yg\) and zero, because if you want to satisfy both of these conditions at once, that's what you want to do.

And because this LP is feasible (one valid solution is just to assign zero weight to everything), and it's also bounded in the sense that the weights can only go from zero to one, we can wave our hands, and by the magic of strong duality these two optima are equal. Which means that we have this result: given a collection of models, the size of the largest hard core of these models is going to be exactly equal to the best possible hinge loss on the benchmark.

Okay, cool. So up until this point, we've proven this identity between hard core size and hinge loss. So in other words, we've proven that every weighted vote has hinge loss at least \(2\alpha\) if and only if there is a hard core with size at least \(2\alpha\). But none of this quite ties it to our premise, which is our assumption that no weighted vote achieves an error of less than \(\alpha\). So there's this distinction between zero-one error, which is what our assumption is about, and hinge loss, which is what our identity with the hard core gives us.

So in order to close this gap, we're going to fix a score \(g\) that minimizes hinge loss. This score \(g\) can be whatever weights you want, as long as it's the global minimizer on the entire benchmark. And by what we proved on the last slide, this means that the largest hard core has size equal to the hinge loss of this best \(g\). So all that we have left to show is just that this hinge loss that \(g\) achieves is at least \(2\alpha\). So basically what we want to get is a weighted vote that has error at most the hinge loss of \(g\) over 2. Because if we have a weighted vote with error at most \(\mathrm{hinge}(g)/2\), then, since its error is at least \(\alpha\), the hinge loss of \(g\) is at least \(2\alpha\), which implies that we get the hard core that we want.

Yeah, if we try to take a first stab at it just by using \(g\) itself, we don't really get this bound, because if we plot zero-one loss versus hinge loss, there's this interval where they're flush against each other, so we don't get the factor of two that we need. So in order to get this factor of two, we're going to mutate our optimal score \(g\) a little bit, by moving the threshold at which \(g\) predicts plus or minus one. So we're going to draw some threshold \(\tau\) uniformly from the interval from \(-1\) to \(1\). Our new mutated rule is going to be that we take \(g\), then we subtract our threshold \(\tau\), and then we just predict the sign of whatever that new number is.

Yeah, so if we work out the algebra: if we fix one question, and originally it has some margin, some signed score \(m\), which again is equal to the question's true label times the original score \(g\), then our new mutated threshold rule \(f_\tau\) is going to be wrong precisely when \(y\tau\) is at least \(m\). And because \(y\) is fixed, \(y\tau\) is formally uniform on the interval from \(-1\) to \(1\), and therefore this event of our mutated rule \(f_\tau\) being wrong is going to occur with probability at most half the non-negative part of \(1 - m\), \(\frac{1}{2}(1-m)_+\).

So by drawing \(\tau\) uniformly from \([-1, 1]\)... there's this black line that shows the error from before, and by averaging over \(\tau\) we're able to get this orange line, and this orange line satisfies the condition that, for this specific question, at every possible \(m\), it's at most half as large as the hinge loss. So this is for a fixed question. If we average over questions as well as \(\tau\), then we get that the error is at most the hinge loss of \(g\) over 2. So the average population error over possible thresholds \(\tau\) is at most \(\mathrm{hinge}(g)/2\), which means that, because this is an average, there exists some fixed threshold \(\tau_0\) such that \(\alpha\) is less than or equal to the error of this corresponding \(f_{\tau_0}\), and that error is going to be bounded above by half the hinge loss of \(g\). The second inequality is by what we just did here with the random sampling. The first inequality is by our no-arbitrage assumption, because \(f_{\tau_0}\) is also itself a valid weighted vote.

Okay, cool. So now we've completed the chain of implications. And from the chain of implications, we get that the largest hard core has size that's equal to the hinge loss of the optimal score, and this hinge loss is going to be at least \(2\alpha\). [You just need] to scale down to exactly \(2\alpha\) if you want to recover the exact statement of the theorem.

32The Hard Core Recovers Error Concentration

Awesome. So that finishes proving the hard core theorem. And just as a final note, this hard core theorem does recover the error concentration. I referred to this earlier, but the hard core is not a weaker notion than the error concentration. It also implies the bound. It's just that if you have a hard core on which all of these models are exactly at chance, then you already know that within this part of the benchmark, which is of size exactly \(2\alpha\), each of these models has already put \(\alpha\) of its errors inside this set. Which means that, just by a basic union bound over the entire benchmark, the size of the error union is also bounded by the same error concentration bound. And actually, for multi-class classification, we can recover the error concentration result by this route: getting a hard core for multi-class, and then having that imply error concentration.

33Summary

Okay, cool. So as a recap of what we've talked about today: the premise was just that we have this hypothesis about what a market should reflect, which is that because people compete with each other, because people want to get these opportunities to beat the Pareto frontier and make some money, this competition gives us a market-based explanation for why simple ensemble improvements might already be available as services. Or, said another way, you can't really beat the frontier using these cheap, simple improvements.

And then we said: if we take this hypothesis to be true, so if it is in fact the case that accurate models can't improve on this benchmark by simple ensemble methods like threshold voting, then their errors concentrate on a small (well, small depending on your conditions, but let's say small) common set of questions. But this error concentration bound depended on the sum of the excess errors of each of these models. And we said, okay, this seems like it can get a little bit out of hand; it can get a little bit vacuous if your models make a lot of mistakes. So we strengthened our assumption and expanded our scope to include all affine weighted votes, instead of just the threshold votes that we were looking at earlier. And under this stronger assumption, we proved that the models actually share a hard core of size \(2\alpha\), on which every one of these models is exactly 50% accurate.

So overall, the moral of the story is essentially just that buying more models does not automatically buy useful complementarity, just because if you're in a mature market, then you might expect that if it were possible to do this, other people would already have taken advantage of that complementarity.
