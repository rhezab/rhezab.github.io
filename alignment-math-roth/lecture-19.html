1Can AI Competitors Cooperate on Safety?

What I want to talk about today is maybe a little bit of a departure from the technical details of learning systems that we've spent much of the class on. I want to think about the incentives of AI providers themselves, and whether they can cooperate on joint safety goals, or whether the competitive pressures of the market stop that coordination, even when it would be mutually beneficial.

2Overview

The underlying picture you should have in your mind are these calls to pace the frontier. The premise is that maybe by developing superintelligent AI as fast as we can, we're kind of hurtling towards civilizational disaster. We don't know exactly when it's going to happen, but we get these models that are smarter than we know how to control, and at some point the world ends. And that is bad, not just for us, but also for the profits of the AI companies.

And it might even be that the AI companies and their employees kind of agree on this picture. We've seen, for example, high-profile resignations of highly placed employees at OpenAI making exactly this case. They say, oh, what are we doing? We are working towards the end of the world. So maybe the people in charge of these companies are even on board with this premise. I think you could make the case that this is maybe true of Anthropic, at least.

Still, there's a coordination problem. Suppose everyone believes in this premise: that while we are working to develop superintelligent AI, we are at risk of destroying the world. Does that mean it is rational for any of the parties to stop? Or are we, through market competition, locked into some kind of race dynamic where we can't stop?

What we're going to study today is a toy model from the recent economics literature that tries to formalize this, and give conditions under which it is rational to stop and conditions under which maybe it is irrational to stop. And what we'll see comes out as the crux in this model is monitoring speed.

It might be that it would be an equilibrium for us to both agree to pause AI development at the current state. Say there's only two providers in the market, Anthropic and OpenAI. Let's forget about all the other providers that maybe complicate the story. It might be in both of their interests to just stop development now and divide up the world and make lots and lots of money selling Astra and Fable to companies right now, and stop racing ahead. This would have various desirable business consequences, one of which is you could stop investing money in training the next models. The other of which is maybe you would stop the chance of the next model ending the world, which would also be bad for business.

Maybe that's an equilibrium, but the problem is there's lots of difficulties with explicitly coordinating on that equilibrium. In particular, I think it would be an obvious antitrust violation if two parties said, hey, why don't we stop making our product better and just save a lot of money and make much more profit. So a different and maybe more relevant question is whether, absent this kind of coordination, it is rational for either party to stop development first.

What we'll see coming out of this model is that in a two-provider world, once one provider stops, it is rational for the other provider to stop. But what's going to be the relevant quantity is monitoring speed: how long after I stop development do you observe that and become confident that I really have stopped development? And what we'll see is that if monitoring speed is sufficiently fast, then indeed stopping can be supported in equilibrium. But if monitoring speed isn't sufficiently fast, then racing to the end of the world is also an equilibrium.

We're going to write down, as you'll see, a very toy model of two-provider competition. The basic parameters of the model are that the amount of money that I am able to make is a function of the relative capabilities of my model and your model. So the amount of money that I'm able to make with GPT-6 competing with Fable 5 is some amount of money. That's the same amount of money I would be able to make with GPT-7 competing with Fable 6. The fact that we've both grown equally more capable does not allow us to extract more money from the market, because we're competing down to the price of compute. But as soon as my capability starts to exceed yours, I very quickly eat up all of the money, and vice versa.

4Firms Can Stop Developing and Keep Selling

As you will see, I'm going to write down a hilariously simple model. There's lots of things to criticize about it. There are going to be parameters in the model; I'm just going to plug in simple values for those parameters that I'm not going to worry too much about.

Here's the model. It's going to be a continuous-time model, so time \(t\) just advances continuously. And there's only two firms. Let's call them OpenAI and Anthropic; we can call them 1 and 2. Everyone's capability starts at zero, and it increases at a linear rate until you decide to stop. Once you stop, in this toy model, that's it, you're not going to start up again. So say firm \(i\) decides to stop at some time \(\tau_i\). Then their capability at time \(t\) is just whichever is smaller, \(t\) or \(\tau_i\): \(x_i(t) = \min\{t, \tau_i\}\). It's just this piecewise linear function where your capability increases linearly at rate one until the stopping time, and if you decide to stop, it just stays the same forever. So this capability is just linearly increasing and then capped at your stopping time, if you ever decide to stop developing capabilities.

Stopping does not mean that you go out of business and you stop making money. It just means you stop developing the model. If OpenAI stops now, they can continue to keep selling access to Astra, and how much money that will make them will depend on the performance of the Claude models as they develop.

5Falling Behind Reduces a Firm's Profits

So there's three moving parts to how you're going to calculate your long-run payoff. It's a continuous-time model, so these are all instantaneous quantities. The first is: instantaneously at time \(t\), how much money can you make? This will be a function of the relative abilities of your model versus your competitor's model.

For all three moving parts we're going to use exponential functions, not because there's any deep reason to believe that that's the right function, but just because this is going to be convenient for calculations: if I want to compute what's the right thing to do at some time, all that matters is the state at that time. I don't need to worry about the history. So seller \(i\)'s instantaneous profit at time \(t\) is just the exponential of the difference between seller \(i\)'s capability at time \(t\) minus their opponent's capability at time \(t\): \(\pi_i(t) = e^{x_i(t) - x_j(t)}\). Just \(i\) and \(j\). As you can imagine, coordination is only going to become harder when there's more players.

Student: [comment that the total profit is not constant across the two firms]

Roth: That's right. Yeah, it's not constant. It's not that they're splitting a fixed pot of money in this model. The exponential function is chosen mainly for the memoryless property, not for its faithfulness in modeling the market, so I won't try to defend it too much. But as capabilities race ahead, maybe the pot widens, so maybe it captures something of Burke's claim that as we get more powerful models, you can make more money with them, but only if you have some kind of relative monopoly power. If you have an equally good competitor, you just make a dollar, no matter how powerful the model is. I'm not going to defend that too strongly as a faithful representation of the market. That's just the function we're going to use. Everything we do here, you should think about as a case study of some model where we've plugged in particular instantiations of these things. You could, of course, ask all of the same questions in a more [general model].

So this will be our profit model. Just to develop some intuition: both parties develop at the same speed. So if neither of them stops, then at all times the capability difference will be zero, and they're just going to make one dollar per unit time. And what's going to happen is, if one of them stops and the other doesn't, this gap will keep increasing until the other one stops. And the gap is going to be a severe bonus to the party that stops second and a severe penalty for the one that stops first, because profit is shaped as the exponential of the gap.

Which I think is actually not crazy. These are fast-moving markets. I think if, rather than having Astra right now, OpenAI was still selling GPT-4, they would be making much less money against the current crop of Claude models, even though in absolute timescales, believe it or not, GPT-4 wasn't that long ago.

Good. So just to put numbers to that: they were both marching along towards disaster, and firm \(i\) decides to stop. Then their rival, firm \(j\), eventually decides to stop as well, but they wait another \(s\) units of time before stopping. After the other firm stops and we're out of harm's way, firm \(i\) will be making instantaneous profit that is now only \(e^{-s}\), because that's the capability gap. \(j\) will be making instantaneous profit \(e^{s}\). So this is giving a big advantage to even small relative differences in capabilities.

6Firms Discount Future Profits

The second moving part is a discount factor. In any game where potentially the game is going to continue forever, I can't just add up my instantaneous profit, or, in a continuous model, integrate my instantaneous profit over all days, because it will be a divergent quantity. It'll go to infinity. And so the way you normally think about this is by discounting the future. There's a discount factor. The idea is, a dollar tomorrow is worth less to you than a dollar today, and a dollar two days from now is worth even less.

The way we'll represent this, and this part is very standard modeling, is that there will be some discount rate \(\rho\), which just for simplicity we'll take to be one. If you're not worried about the end of the world, the way you would calculate your lifetime future returns would be by discounting exponentially with time. So your lifetime future returns, if you're not worried about the world ending, would be the integral from zero to infinity of your instantaneous profit at each time, discounted by how far it is into the future. Even if the world doesn't end, that makes your future profits a finite number, so we can compare them with things. And if the world does end at time \(T\), then the integral goes up to time \(T\), because after the world ends, no more profits. Discounted future returns.

7Development Also Risks Losing All Future Profits

And finally, part of the model is that there is this background rate of catastrophe. And the only way to stop this background rate of catastrophe is for both providers to stop developing their technology. If only one of them stops, it doesn't affect the rate of catastrophe at all. That's going to be driving a lot of the results of the model.

In principle, this could be a parameter, but we're just going to fix it. There's some background instantaneous probability per unit time that the world ends. Parametrically, that would be a parameter \(\lambda\), but we're just going to take it to be 2. It's a continuous model, but roughly what that means is that over a short period of time of length \(h\), the probability of the world ending scales roughly as \(2h\). There's a rate 2 of the world ending at any particular time. In the continuous model, what that means is that the probability that the world doesn't end over the next \(s\) units of time, so the probability that you can keep making money \(s\) steps from now, is going to be \(e^{-\lambda s}\). And since we're going to take \(\lambda\) to be 2, it's just \(e^{-2s}\). That's the probability that the world hasn't ended in \(s\) time steps if at least one of us is continuing to develop.

Once we both stop, the risk of the world ending goes away. That's the important modeling step here: one of us stopping unilaterally does not remove the risk of the world ending. We both need to stop to remove the risk of the world ending. But if I unilaterally stop, that does immediately start harming my profits.

And all of the quantities here, the profit, the discount factor, and the instantaneous rate at which the world might end, have all been conveniently written in exponential form, so they'll still be exponentials when we multiply them together. It means that if I want to figure out what is my expected long-term profit over, say, the next \(s\) rounds, if danger hasn't ended, if at least one of us continues to develop over those next \(s\) rounds, I need to multiply together three things and then integrate over them. There's the instantaneous profit rate that I make at each period in time if the world hasn't ended. There's the probability that the world hasn't ended; if the world has ended, that's bad for my IPO filings. And as usual, I discount the future, so I'm less interested in money ten years from now than I am in money today. In principle, all of these things have parameters, like what is the discount factor or what is the instantaneous probability of disaster. We're just plugging in numbers.

8Both Firms Prefer Stopping Together

Good. So we can solve some integrals and figure out what some baseline quantities are. One thing we could try to both coordinate on is we could say, okay, look, it's bad for our children and it is bad for business to destroy the world, so why don't we both just stop now? We just coordinate on a simultaneous stop. In that case, because we both stop at the same time, we have matched capabilities, so the instantaneous profit is just one. The disaster risk goes away. And so we're just integrating from zero to infinity (we get to go to infinity because the world hasn't ended, because we both stopped) our instantaneous profit, which is 1, times our discount factor. And because we've chosen a discount rate of 1, that makes this integral work out to something convenient, which is 1. We can make unit profit if we just somehow coordinated on a simultaneous stop.

Or we could both just race ahead to the end of the world. If we did that, what would be our long-run profit? Well, again, our instantaneous profit at each round would always be one, because we're always capability-matched. The capabilities are uniformly increasing, but the difference is always zero. So the instantaneous profit remains one, and we still discount the future as before. But now there's the probability that the world ends, and we only make money if the world hasn't ended. The probability that the world hasn't ended after \(s\) time steps is \(e^{-2s}\). These multiply together nicely, and we can integrate, and we find that our long-run discounted profit is only one third.

So the end of the world shows up on the balance sheet. There's a reason to want to stop. It's not just so that your kids can live. It's also because you get to make two-thirds more dollars. So this is just to point out that if the firms got to choose an outcome, at least if they were choosing amongst these two, they would choose the outcome in which they both stop. That doesn't end the world, and also it makes them three times as much money. So it's a better outcome for the firms. And so the question is, if they only have unilateral power to affect what they do, is anyone going to have an incentive to stop first?

9A Rival's Stop Is Observed Only After a Delay

So we need to have some part of the model that specifies what your opponent learns if you stop first. Say you use the GPUs you had for training just for inference. You're not burning down the data centers or anything, you're not shutting down as a company, you're still serving your current models. You're just reallocating all of your resources from training the next model to serving current models and building products. It might not be immediately obvious that you had done that in a believable way. The company still exists, you're still hiring people, you still have these big data centers. And so maybe it takes a little bit of time after one firm unilaterally stops for the other firm to gather enough evidence to be sure that they've stopped. The first firm probably issued a big press release, but it takes some time to make sure you believe it.

So let's say that if a firm stops, its rival will receive some conclusive report that certifies that stop at some point in the future, but potentially with some delay. And we'll have control in the model of the distribution over that. The probability that the delay is longer than \(s\) units of time will go down as \(e^{-\omega s}\). So \(\omega\) is the parameter that specifies how quickly another firm comes to believe that you have stopped once you stop. And the way this is parametrized, a larger value of \(\omega\) means that they learn more quickly in expectation, because the probability that they haven't learned that you've stopped after \(s\) units of time goes down exponentially with \(\omega\) times \(s\). So the probability they haven't learned after a long period of time is smaller the bigger \(\omega\) is. And the mean of this distribution is \(1/\omega\). So if \(\omega\) is 2, after you stop, you expect the other firm to notice you've stopped after half a unit of time. Again, obviously this parametric choice doesn't involve any kind of deep economic analysis of the fundamentals of the market. It's a nice exponential.

10Keep Developing Until a Stop Report Arrives

But let's think about different strategies that the firms could undertake in what is now a game. One is that maybe you don't want to be the one put at a disadvantage. If you stop first, even if the other guy eventually stops in response, it will be after some delay, which will allow their capabilities to advance beyond yours. In the resulting safe world in which progress has eventually stopped, you're going to be making a lot less money than the other guy, because you stopped first. You've got the less capable model. So maybe what you want to do is you don't want to stop first. You want to keep developing while the other guy hasn't stopped. But upon learning that the other guy stopped, which is not immediately when he stopped, but just when you receive the signal, maybe then you stop right away.

One observation, of course, is that when we both follow this rule, if neither of us is planning to stop first, then neither of us will stop, and we'll just keep developing until catastrophe, and in expectation we'll make only a third of a dollar.

So one question you might want to ask is: are we just going to race to the end of the world in equilibrium? Here's a candidate strategy that could be an equilibrium for both players. Is this an equilibrium? Suppose we're both playing this strategy. If it's not an equilibrium, it means that one of us has a beneficial deviation, and the game is simple enough that the beneficial deviation is stopping first: I'll develop maybe until some finite time, and then I will stop even if you haven't as well. So let's first check whether, if our opponent is playing this strategy, planning to stop second once they have a convincing report that we've stopped, I should also follow that strategy, or whether it would be better for me to just bite the bullet and stop first.

11Slow Monitoring Can Sustain a Dangerous Race

And what we're going to see is that this hinges on the expected delay of the signal. What's going to happen is that if the signal is too slow, if the expected time at which the other guy gets a convincing signal that I have stopped, sufficiently convincing that it'll cause them to stop too, if that is too slow, then this is an equilibrium. We're both willing to stop second, but neither of us is willing to stop first, and so we're going to race to the end of the world.

On the other hand, if the world is such that a convincing signal can reach the other guy quickly enough (it doesn't have to be instantaneous; it's good enough in this model if \(\omega\) is greater than one half, which means that the expected number of time steps until the other guy gets the signal is less than two), if the signal is sufficiently fast, then this isn't an equilibrium, and someone would actually break out of this and bite the bullet and stop first.

And this is true even though across the whole range of parameters the payoff would be highest for these two firms if they stopped immediately together. The problem is just the coordination. It could still be an equilibrium even though the firms always prefer stopping simultaneously and immediately. So the qualitative message is that if a convincing signal of unilateral disarmament can be sent sufficiently quickly, this kind of racing outcome, racing to the end of the world, is not an equilibrium. But there's a sharp threshold, and if the signal cannot be sent sufficiently quickly, racing to the end of the world is an equilibrium, despite the fact that both parties would agree that it is better to not race to the end of the world. Does that make sense?

And so there's two things we need to verify, because the strategy that we're studying here has two parts. One is that you don't stop if the other guy hasn't stopped. But the other is that as soon as you learn the other guy has stopped, you do stop. We need to verify that after you've received a signal that the other guy has stopped, it is actually a best response for you to stop as well. And to verify also that if you haven't received such a signal, and \(\omega\) is at most one half, if the reporting delay is sufficiently long, then there's no alternative that benefits you; you should just continue racing. But if \(\omega\) is greater than one half, if the reporting delay is sufficiently small, then there is a better alternative. You should stop unilaterally.

12Waiting Raises Profits but Prolongs Disaster Risk

So first let's think about after we've received a report that our competitor has stopped. Is it a good idea for us to stop, or should we continue racing? Let's say that at the moment we've learned that our competitor has stopped, our instantaneous profit at that moment is \(b\). Our instantaneous profit has been evolving over time. It was 1 until our opponent stopped. At some point they stopped, and we realized it at some later time. And at this moment, our instantaneous profit is \(b\). This is relevant for us because if we stop now, our instantaneous profit will continue to be \(b\) forever. We might be tempted, however, to keep going for at least a little bit to increase our instantaneous profit.

So our instantaneous profit is \(b\), and we're just going to think about time from here on forwards. We've got a collection of choices after we see the signal. We can either stop immediately, or we could keep developing until some future time \(h\), and then we could stop. Or we could keep developing forever; that's \(h\) is infinity.

So when we receive the signal, assuming we are still alive, if we stop now, our instantaneous profit forever will be \(b\). But what we will gain is that, assuming we're alive at this moment, we will continue being alive. Our probability of continuing to be alive at some future time \(s\) will be 1, because the other guy stopped; once we stop, we end this probability of disaster.

On the other hand, we could say, okay, well, we're not going to stop. I'll stop eventually, but maybe not just yet. I'm going to squeeze out just a little bit more progress, one more LLM training run. If I'm alive after I stop, then the payoff I'll get for having waited to stop until later is that my instantaneous profit will have increased. At time \(s\), my instantaneous profit will be what it was today, \(b\), larger by a factor of \(e\) to whichever is smaller, time \(s\) or my stopping time: \(be^{\min\{s,h\}}\). Because my capabilities will increase linearly until my stopping time, at which point they will level out, and the other guy stopped already, so the difference in capabilities is frozen. So that's the reason potentially to not stop immediately, to stop only at some future time.

But the reason not to do that is that I continue this disaster-prone period, and my probability of being alive at some future time \(s\) diminishes to \(e^{-2\min\{s,h\}}\), \(e\) to the minus 2 times whichever is smaller, \(s\) or the stopping time. Again, \(e^{-2s}\) is my probability of still being alive after \(s\) time steps if I've been racing that whole time. If \(s\) is bigger than the stopping time, the probability of being alive is frozen at \(e^{-2h}\), because after having survived the first \(h\) units of time, from then on out, I was good.

And the future is discounted, but the future is discounted in the same way for both of these two competing possibilities. The thing to note here is that, as we've parameterized these functions, the rate at which my instantaneous profit is going to increase with time and with my stopping time is slower than the rate at which my probability of death will increase. And when we multiply these two terms together, as we do when we compute my expected future profit, what I'm going to get is \(e\) to the minus whichever is smaller, \(s\) or \(h\).

14Stopping First Leaves a Period of Shared Danger

And so the remaining question is what I should do before the other guy stops. Should I stop first? Let's think about what would happen if I stop first. Index time at the moment that I stop. We've already established that the other guy will stop as soon as he receives the convincing signal. So I can divide what's going to happen into two phases. There's the phase before the other guy receives the signal that I've stopped, and there's the phase after.

Now, before the other guy receives the signal, I'm really quite upset that I decided to stop, because I immediately start falling behind. 15The Payoff From Stopping First Has Two Parts

And so let's think about the payoff that I would get for stopping first, for stopping immediately. Let's call that \(V_{\text{first}}\). It divides into two parts, according to these two parts of the plot we just saw. There's before the report arrives and there's after the report arrives. Before the report arrives, our profit rate is actively falling, and we're still at risk of the world ending. After the report arrives, if I survive that long, which is not guaranteed, my profit rate stops falling, but it is lower forever. Actually forever now, because the world's not going to end.

This is something you can compute. It's just another integral with these terms. It's a more complicated but not especially enlightening integral, so I won't work through it. But the upshot, if you do the calculation, is that my expected utility for stopping now can be written in closed form as \(\frac{1+\omega}{4+\omega}\). Remember, \(\omega\) is the parameter in our model that captures how quickly the signal arrives after stopping. The expected time of the signal is \(1/\omega\). Bigger \(\omega\) means faster monitoring.

Just looking at the limiting behavior of this quantity: as \(\omega\) goes to infinity, as we get arbitrarily fast monitoring, so the other guy notices we've stopped almost immediately, the payoff becomes 1. As \(\omega\) goes to 0, it becomes one quarter. And just to have something to compare this to, the racing strategy, which is the thing that we're trying to show is an equilibrium in some range of these \(\omega\) values, has payoff one third. If I decide to continue racing to disaster, in expectation my payoff's one third. We already see that I can do better: I can get payoff arbitrarily close to one if the monitoring is approaching instantaneous notification. But if the monitoring is becoming increasingly slow, as \(\omega\) goes to zero, this will be one quarter, so it'll be worse.

16Faster Reports Can Make Stopping First Better

So remember, the strategy that everyone would coordinate on, if they could, was to stop immediately. That had payoff one. The payoff of the racing strategy is one third. And the stopping-first deviation from the racing strategy has some payoff that depends on \(\omega\), that begins below one third, at one quarter, and asymptotes to one, but only slowly. And so at some point they cross. Before the crossing point, racing is an equilibrium, and after the crossing point it's not; after the crossing point the payoff is better for stopping first. The crossing point for this parameterization is \(\omega\) is one half.

All we've established so far is that the threshold \(\omega = 1/2\) specifies when it's better to stop immediately than to wait forever. You might still wonder: below one half, is there some other deviation, other than stopping immediately? For example, you could stop after a minute, or you could stop at some randomly selected time. It's not hard to rule out that kind of deviation as well.

17Delaying a Stop Cannot Turn a Loss into a Gain

The reason is kind of straightforward. My proposed clever deviation is to wait some \(h\) units of time and then stop. And I'm comparing this to the strategy that is supposedly an equilibrium, the thing that I'm deviating against, which would never stop. The point is, I'm interested in the difference between the payoff of this deviation and the payoff of the racing strategy. If that difference is positive, that's when the racing strategy is not an equilibrium. But we're doing exactly the same thing in both strategies until we stop. So any contribution to the payoff before the stopping time cancels out; we get it both in the thing that we're supposedly deviating from and in the deviation. And so the difference in payoff begins only at the moment that I stop.

And at that moment, because these exponential functions are memoryless, my difference in payoff is exactly what it would have been had we stopped immediately, because at that moment, I'm stopping immediately. The only difference in the calculation is that I am uniformly down-weighting differences in payoff in the future, because I have a discount factor, and also I might be dead in the future. But that's just some uniform multiplier on my change in payoff at the moment when I stop, which, because of the memoryless property of exponential distributions, is just the same change in payoff we've already analyzed: the change in payoff of stopping immediately. If at time \(h\) it's positive, it's still positive even after I multiply it by the discount factor. So if you're going to stop, you might as well stop immediately. Stopping in the future doesn't help you.

18Randomizing the Stopping Time Cannot Help Either

And stopping randomly doesn't help you either. It's not like you've learned anything before you've stopped, or rather, it's not like you've learned anything before the other guy stops. The other guy gives you a signal when he stops at some point in the future, but if he hasn't stopped, you receive no new information. So if we know, as we do, that it's not beneficial to stop in a minute or in 5 minutes or in 10 minutes, it's not beneficial to randomize over those as well, because our gain from doing so is just some convex combination of our gain from playing any one of them.

19Safe Stopping and Dangerous Racing Can Coexist

So that is not to say that racing ahead is the only equilibrium, even when reporting is slow. Racing ahead is an equilibrium, but there's at least two. We could play the racing strategy: keep developing until the other stops. That's an equilibrium. The payoff is one third, and the probability that we end the world is one. It's definitely going to happen. But if we both stop together, the payoff is one, and we definitely won't end the world. So there is room for two equilibria. It's just that the good equilibrium might be hard to coordinate.

Given that we know that there's multiple equilibria, I might worry that even in the world in which monitoring is sufficiently fast to eliminate the racing equilibrium, all we've ruled out is that particular equilibrium. Now that we know there is a multiplicity of equilibria, even with sufficiently fast monitoring, although there are safe equilibria, there might also be other dangerous equilibria in which the world ends with certainty. So does having sufficiently fast monitoring rule out racing forever in every equilibrium?

20Fast Reports Help Only If Responses Are Credible

To answer this question in the right way, we need to think about equilibrium refinement. We need to think about basically subgame perfect equilibrium, because there is technically a Nash equilibrium in which the world is definitely going to end. The issue with it is it's not a credible equilibrium.

So here's the bad Nash equilibrium that survives even infinitely fast monitoring. It's that we're both going to keep developing forever, even if the other person has already stopped. I'm just going to rip the steering wheel off the car. I commit to having no ability to stop, even if you do. If we're both doing this, this is still an equilibrium. Why would you stop? The reason to stop would be to end the probability of disaster, which would happen eventually if I was going to stop in response, but won't happen if somehow I've committed to not stopping even when you stop. That is a Nash equilibrium. It's just arranging it so that neither of us has the unilateral ability to end disaster even by stopping, removing the only incentive to stop. So that's an actual equilibrium.

But maybe the reason not to be too worried about this Nash equilibrium is that it's not credible. Another way of saying this is it's not subgame perfect. We've already calculated that once one of the participants in this game receives a signal that the other one has stopped, it is a best response for them to stop as well. So to believe that this Nash equilibrium would actually be executed, you would need to believe that once your opponent receives a signal of your stop, they would at that point act against their own interests and not stop, even though at that moment it is better for them to stop. So this is related to ripping the steering wheel off in a game of chicken. You could write down a model where someone could commit to this, but absent commitment power, it's not credible that they would follow through with this strategy.

So maybe we ask this question in a more nuanced way. We can restrict attention to subgame perfect equilibria, or perfect Bayesian equilibria, which require not just that one strategy is a best response to the other, but that it's a best response at every information state. It's a best response in this particular case before I've learned that you stopped, and also after I've learned that you stopped. Which rules out this kind of strategy where I commit to developing forever even if you've stopped, because once you've stopped, it's a best response for me to stop as well.

21Fast Monitoring Rules Out Perpetual Racing

And what we can show is that once we restrict attention to these credible equilibria, these subgame perfect equilibria, then in a world with sufficiently fast reporting, if \(\omega\) is greater than one half, then across all Nash equilibria, even though there could be multiple equilibria, you can promise that the planned stopping time for both firms will be finite with probability one. They can randomize, but we won't have any equilibria that race towards certain demise. It doesn't say that we're necessarily going to stop immediately. There might be some positive probability of demise, but the probability of survival will be greater than zero. The stopping time for both firms will be finite. So maybe it's not quite as reassuring as you'd like. It doesn't say that the only equilibrium is to stop immediately. That is an equilibrium, but maybe not the only one. But it is saying that we have no equilibria in which the probability of dying is 1. It'll be less than 1.

So what we need to rule out is that, if we're in equilibrium, there's positive probability that neither firm stops first. That's the possibility of racing to infinity. It can't be positive probability that neither firm ever stops first. And the only thing we need to remember from our prior analysis to prove this is the gap between our future profit for stopping first and our future profit for racing: \(\Delta = V_{\text{first}} - V_{\text{race}}\). This gap is greater than zero. So we're going to derive this theorem about all Nash equilibria just by remembering that. If I consider my payoff from immediately stopping, and compare that to my future payoff for racing ahead to infinity, that gain is positive.

22Late but Finite Stopping Plans Become Rare

So let's fix any subgame perfect equilibrium: any equilibrium in which at any information state I'm playing a credible response. And what it means that we're playing a credible response is that both of us are going to stop if we learn that the other one has stopped. So what's going to characterize our strategies is the date at which either of us would stop in the absence of such a signal. When do we decide to stop first? These are potentially randomized strategies, so that could be a random variable. Let's say that \(S_i\) is the date that firm \(i\) would stop in the absence of hearing a report that the other guy has stopped, assuming that the world hasn't ended before that. We can stop before this date if we get a report, but this is the date at which I would stop otherwise.

And the thing we want to rule out is that there is positive probability that neither of us would stop first. That is what would be required to have the probability of the world ending be one. Let's say that \(q\) is the probability that neither of us decides to stop first: the stopping-first times are both at infinity. We want to show that \(q\) has to be zero. So the thing that we're worried about, that we want to prove by contradiction cannot happen, is that \(q\) is greater than zero: there's some positive probability that we just keep developing until the world ends.

Now, the thing to notice is that for any firm, if I look at the probability that firm \(i\)'s initial stopping time is finite, it's not infinity, but occurs after time \(t\), that probability has to shrink to zero as \(t\) grows large. How come? You can eliminate from the support of the distribution the choice to never stop. What you're left with is a distribution over finite times. You can write out the CDF of this distribution. Any CDF approaches one, and this quantity is just 1 minus the CDF. So this is just saying that for any probability distribution over numbers, the probability that a draw from that distribution exceeds some threshold \(t\) goes to 0 as \(t\) goes to infinity. In particular, \(\varepsilon_t\), the sum of the two probabilities that player one's stop time is finite but exceeds \(t\) and that player two's stop time is finite but exceeds \(t\), also tends to zero in the limit as \(t\) goes to infinity.

It's important that there's the restriction that stop times are finite in these events. It could be that there's positive probability that we never stop. This is saying the probability that we were planning to stop at some finite time, it's just that our plan was to stop later, goes to zero as I move \(t\) further and further forward.

And so suppose there's some positive probability that we never stop. I'm going to show that this can't have been supported in equilibrium, because there was a better response: a response that would improve on the existing strategy. And the form of that response will be that I'm going to do what I was doing before up until time \(t\), but now I'll stop at time \(t\). So the deviation will be to keep doing what you were doing until some finite cutoff point of my choosing, and at that cutoff point, I'll stop even if the other guy hasn't. That's the form of the deviation. I want to argue that if \(q\) is greater than 0, there's always a beneficial deviation of this form. We're not saying \(t\) is 0. We're not saying you should stop immediately, but you will stop at some finite time.

23Stopping Late Has Only One Possible Downside

There's really only three components of the payoff to consider here. What's happening at time \(t\) when we decide to stop? It could be that the other guy already stopped and we just didn't know about it. We were already going to stop if we got the report that he stopped, but there's this delay. So it could be that the other guy already stopped and we just didn't get the report. In that case, we're lucky that we stopped. Once the other guy has stopped, we've already established that stopping is optimal. So if at time \(t\) the other guy has stopped, then conditional on that event, my deviation is beneficial to me, because stopping is optimal once the other guy has stopped.

It could also be that under the previous strategies, under the realization of our randomness, it turns out that neither of us was going to stop ever. And our assumption for the purpose of contradiction is that this second conditioning event actually does happen, with some probability \(q\). We're trying to show that the second conditioning event doesn't happen. We've assumed, for the purpose of contradiction, that this event, that actually neither of us was planning to stop ever, does happen with probability \(q\). But in this case, conditional on this event, stopping now is also a better response. Because now, at time \(t\), what we're comparing is the utility for racing ahead and never stopping (that's what's happening in this conditioning event, we're going to race ahead and never stop) with the utility of stopping now at time \(t\). And we've already analyzed that. That was the expected gain quantity \(\Delta\) that we showed was positive.

And so the event under which it might actually be a bad idea for us to have stopped, or at least we haven't shown that it was a good idea, is that the other guy is still developing. He hasn't stopped yet, but he's planning to stop. He's not planning to go forever; he's planning to stop in a little bit. Maybe it would be a bummer if I decided to stop at time \(t\) and it turned out he was planning to stop at time \(t + \epsilon\). Under that third conditioning event, maybe we lose by stopping early. But we don't lose too much. We only lose at most one. I showed already that the maximum reward is one.

26Summary

But maybe the takeaway is just that if you're interested in ideas like getting people to unilaterally shift the equilibrium we're in, it is maybe helpful to think about this as a coordination problem, and one in which the market incentives might be making it difficult to coordinate, even though all of the parties might individually prefer to be in the world in which they've all coordinated to stop. And one knob in the market design or the policy design problem here is credible monitoring. Someone can put out a press release saying they've stopped, but that's maybe not enough to convince me to stop until I really understand what that means. Is this marketing, or have you really disbanded your training team? We don't need instantaneous credible monitoring, but making it faster helps the coordination problem.
