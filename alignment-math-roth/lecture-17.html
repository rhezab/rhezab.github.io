1Delegating AI Oversight

So, last class, we studied how we might get aligned behavior even if all of the ML providers in the system are misaligned: LLM providers that wanted different things from the user, and yet, because they were reliant on the user to actually take action, all the LLM providers could do was provide information and advice. They were in the position of needing to compete for the user's attention. And that mechanism of competition, we showed, under this weaker market-level condition called market alignment, was enough to provide aligned outcomes in the equilibrium of this game without needing actually aligned agents.

Now, what was driving that competition was really the fact that the AI providers could not directly act in the world themselves. Only the user could act. This was modeling LLMs that can talk to you. Of course, the situation is quite different if we have agentic AI systems that can actually take actions themselves. They don't necessarily need to convince you of anything. They can just do the thing.

And so what I want to think about today is whether there's some way to build agentic AI systems (we get to design these things) that can get safety guarantees from the same kind of emergent alignment phenomena that we saw. We want to build a system that might be composed of misaligned components, maybe because we don't know how to instruct the aligned components, but we want to arrange them such that we get somehow the same kind of emergent alignment we saw last time, but now in the agentic system.

2Overview

Of course, you've used Codex and Claude Code, and you have a choice about how closely you'd like to oversee them. The most conservative option is that you can require that whenever the agent is going to take some action that could have consequences, like a tool call, sending an email, whatever, it needs to ask you about it. It needs to ask permission before it can do stuff.

And this is, on the one hand, a nice oversight protocol: before it actually does anything that might have consequences, it asks you. So if you're paying attention, it won't do anything bad. On the other hand, it is extremely annoying, and kind of obviates the goal of task automation. The thing that you want to do is offload some task to your agent. You want to be able to step away for a couple of hours and teach a class and come back and find that your agent has done something. In fact, I'm doing that right now. My agent is running a set of experiments, hopefully for the whole two hours of the class, and we'll have results when I get back. I would be very annoyed if, when I got back to my computer, I found that the agent had stopped five minutes in because it wanted to install a new version of NumPy or something and was asking my permission. "Yes, of course you can do that."

So if I want to be super conservative in Codex and Claude Code, I can require that before it does anything, makes any tool call, it asks for my permission. But that really gets rid of a lot of the benefits of these agentic systems, because I always have to be on hand to manually give permission to anything it wants to do. The goal of task automation is that I should be able to step away.

On the other hand, there is a maximally aggressive mode that gives a blank check to the agent. It gives complete control of your computer over to the agent and says it doesn't need to ask you for permission for anything. And the user interface tries to make this look scary. In Codex, it's written in all red. In Claude Code, I think it used to be called "dangerously skip permissions." And indeed, it is dangerous. Sometimes, especially for long-running tasks, the agent kind of gets out of hand and will, for example, delete something irrevocably.

And for the last four or five months maybe, there's been a seemingly much more useful feature called auto-review. In auto-review, the driver agent still asks for permission, but it doesn't ask you for permission. It just spins up another agent and asks that agent for permission. And functionally, this seems much more convenient. It operates essentially in the same way as if you gave it complete control of your computer. It never asks you for anything. So you can step away for hours and come back and it's done work. And now you get to feel virtuous about doing it: the option in Codex isn't written in all red, you don't have to type "dangerously skip permissions" into Claude Code, because after all, now it's asking someone for permission.

But you might wonder about how virtuous you really ought to feel, because it is not asking you for permission. It's just asking another LLM. And if you're concerned (the reason why you didn't want to give full control of your computer to the driver agent), you might reasonably have the same concern about the reviewer agent. Why should I trust the permissions given by the reviewer agent?

And what we're going to try to do in this lecture is give conditions under which that is reasonable: when you can safely delegate this authorization approval to other LLM agents and get some kind of provable safety guarantee, despite the fact that the LLM agents, just like the driver agent, might not be fully aligned with you. And the way we're going to try to do that is by taking advantage of the same kind of diverse misalignment that we took advantage of last lecture when we studied the equilibrium of LLM markets.

3Who Should Approve an AI's Proposed Action?

Here's the idea. There's a human being, we'll call him the principal, and what he's doing is he would like to delegate some long-running task to his agent, Codex, Claude Code. And he doesn't necessarily fully trust the agent who's driving the system. That's why he might like to have someone review the actions before they are executed. The driver agent will propose actions, and then somehow [someone decides] whether to [execute them] or not.

But the reason the human is delegating this task to an agent in the first place is he doesn't have time to review everything. And of course you can delegate to another AI agent, but that doesn't on its own solve the problem. The whole point was that the driver agent's objective was potentially different from the user's. If you have a reviewer agent on hand who is perfectly aligned, maybe you could have just used them as the driver agent. So the only reason why delegated approval makes sense as a problem to think about in the first place is because we don't think we have fully aligned agents. So that doesn't solve the problem on its own.

And so what we're going to try to do is delegate approval not to a single agent but to some panel of agents. And the hope is that although every agent in the panel might be individually misaligned, if they are misaligned in different ways, maybe something like this market alignment condition from last lecture might be enough to guarantee us some kind of safety, even without individual [alignment].

4Two Specialists, Neither With the Human's Objective

Let me give just a toy example to start, to build some intuition. Let's say that the proposal that the driver agent makes is a change to a software artifact. And maybe there's two things that you care about as a human being. Obviously, this is very stylized. But let's say there's two components of the artifact that you care about. You care about its performance in some way: how good is it at predicting things? But you also care about its privacy properties. These are two different things you care about, and a proposed change could have an effect on both of those.

So let's assume that you can quantify these things in some common units. And let's say that what you care about is the sum of them. I equally care about performance and privacy. And so, baseline levels are zero, and a proposed change increases or decreases performance by \(d_1\) and increases or decreases privacy by \(d_2\). My value for that is \(d_1 + d_2\). If it was up to me, I would approve it if \(d_1 + d_2\) is positive. Otherwise, I would [reject it].

Here the choice is to approve the change or else veto it. If I veto it, I'm left with the status quo. Let's say it's just a zero in both components.

Now, if I had a reviewer who had the same objective function as me, who also cared about \(d_1 + d_2\), I could just delegate approval to them. They would always make the same choices that I would. That would be great. But suppose that I don't. I've got two reviewers, and one of them only cares about performance. So their utility is \(d_1\). They will approve if performance is increased and otherwise reject. And the other one only cares about privacy. Their objective is \(d_2\). They will approve if privacy properties are increased. Otherwise they will reject.

Now, let's imagine that nobody's being strategic. They're just going to vote sincerely. We'll talk a little bit about strategic behavior at the end. But the reviewers, let's say, are not trying to manipulate the behavior of the system towards their preferred outcome for now. They're just correctly telling me whether they think that the proposed action should be taken, as evaluated according to their utility.

5Unanimous Approval Guarantees No Harm

And so I've got these two reviewers who are going to, for any proposal, vote, basically, and say, should we execute this proposal or not? And we have some design control over how we aggregate their votes into an actual execution decision. Let's say for now that the aggregation rule we choose is that we require unanimous approval. So if they both vote to approve, then we will execute the proposed action. Otherwise, we will not. So equivalently, we are giving both of the two reviewers veto power. If either of them says not to execute the proposed action, we won't.

So, given how we've set things up in this toy example, the thing we learn from the vote of each reviewer is whether the component that they care about is positive or negative. If the performance component is non-negative, then the first reviewer will vote to approve; otherwise they'll veto. And if the privacy component is non-negative, the second will vote to approve; otherwise they'll veto. So we get a single bit of information from each reviewer's vote, and that bit of information just tells us whether their utility function evaluated on this proposal is positive or negative. In this case, it's just the individual [coordinate].

So, the way we've constructed these utility functions is that although the reviewers are both individually misaligned with the principal (they have different utility functions), the principal's utility is, in particular, a non-negative linear combination of the reviewers' utilities. It's the sum. The utility is reviewer 1's utility plus reviewer 2's utility.

And so I know that for every action that is executed, the reason it is executed is because it had unanimous approval of these reviewers. What I learn from unanimous approval is that both reviewers had non-negative utility. In particular, the first coordinate was non-negative and the second coordinate was non-negative. It means that when we express the principal's utility function as the sum of the reviewers' utility functions, \(d_1 + d_2\), I learn that the principal's utility is also non-negative, because it is the sum of components that are guaranteed to be non-negative because of the fact that the reviewers did not veto the proposal.

The reviewers' approval is a certification that their part of the principal's utility function is non-negative, and thus their non-negative weighted sum must also be non-negative. In particular, although these two reviewers are not aligned with the principal, I know that if they both approve some proposal, it must be that that proposal is non-harmful to the principal. This is the basic property that we want to take advantage of.

6Either Reviewer Alone Can Authorize Harm

So, just to say some other straightforward things to build intuition: in this case, it was quite important that, amongst all of the approval rules we could have constructed from the binary votes, we chose to require unanimous approval. There's only two reviewers. So what are the non-trivial approval systems? By non-trivial, I mean, you could always have a rule that never approves anything, or you could have a rule that always approves everything. Those ignore the reviewers; those are the trivial ones. But otherwise there's not very many non-trivial rules. You could, as we did, require unanimous approval, or the only other thing you could do is require that approval by one or the other of the reviewers themselves was sufficient.

In this particular case, we want the safety guarantee; we had to use unanimous approval. Requiring approval only from one or the other of the reviewers would not have been good enough. As an example, suppose we said, okay, instead of giving veto power to the reviewers, instead of saying any reviewer can unilaterally reject a proposal, we give unilateral approval authorization to each of the reviewers. If either party approves, then we will execute the proposal.

That wouldn't be good, because if we saw a proposal like this, that gives a unit improvement to performance but makes privacy worse by two units, then reviewer 1 would approve, since it's positive; reviewer 2 would reject, since its utility is negative, and its rejection signal doesn't give us any information about the magnitude of how negative its utility is. In which case the approval would be authorized, but the principal would get negative utility, because it turned out that the negative utility from the privacy loss outweighed the positive utility from the efficiency gains. And that just wasn't evident; that information wasn't present in the binary signals that we get from the approval votes.

7Unanimity Can Reject Beneficial Proposals

Maybe just to, again, hammer home the intuition with the picture. In this case, proposals are represented as points in two dimensions, and the principal cares about the sum of them. The principal, if they had the time to authorize or reject every proposal, would authorize exactly those proposals whose sum, \(d_1 + d_2\), is non-negative. Their indifference line is therefore this diagonal. They would authorize any proposal that lies above the diagonal, on the right.

On the other hand, the reviewers just care about the axes, right? Reviewer 1 approves anything above the x-axis, reviewer 2 everything to the right of the y-axis. And unanimous approval therefore approves the things that are in the intersection of the approval regions of the two reviewers, which is the top-right quadrant. And all of those proposals are safe, in the sense that they give non-negative utility to the principal.

This approval rule is sound, it's safe, but it's not complete. There are things that, had the principal been able to devote their attention to them, they would have approved. A proposal that has efficiency and privacy gains \((2, -1)\) does give positive utility to the principal. That should be approved, but it's rejected by this rule. And that is kind of the cost of requiring that the rule reject everything that is unsafe. It correctly rejected, for example, the one that has utility \((1, -2)\).

And note that all we get to look at are the binary approval votes of the reviewers. If we're going to reject this harmful proposal, we have to reject this beneficial proposal as well, because both of those lead to exactly the same approval vote pattern. Both of them result in the first reviewer approving and the second reviewer rejecting. So the votes themselves don't give us enough information to be able to distinguish this beneficial proposal from this harmful proposal. And so to be safe, to be conservative, to make sure that we don't approve anything harmful, we need to reject both of them. We're able to accept some things, to have a guarantee: a region in which we do approve, and everything in that region is guaranteed to be safe. But the rule is not complete, and it can't be. Make sense?

9Which Groups Are Allowed to Authorize?

Now, in designing the oversight system, the thing we get to design is which coalitions of approving voters are sufficient to authorize execution of the proposal. So, for example, the unanimous approval rule requires that everyone vote to approve, but there are other approval rules we could use. And more generally, a rule will specify some collection of winning coalitions. A winning coalition is just some arbitrary subset of the reviewers. And what it means for something to be a winning coalition is that if every member of any of the winning coalitions votes to approve, then we will execute the proposal. So the approval rule is exactly that we execute a proposal if and only if the set of reviewers who sincerely vote to approve makes up one of the winning coalitions.

So a set of winning coalitions defines the approval rule. If it is the unanimous approval rule, that says there's only one winning coalition, and it's the set of all of the reviewers. We could imagine other things. If we are just going to go with majority votes, then the sets of \(n/2 + 1\) reviewers each form a winning coalition. Any majority would be sufficient. You can imagine plenty of others; that's the design we have.

And it's natural to consider monotone authorization rules. So in principle, you could have a non-monotone rule where there's some set of five reviewers, and if they all vote to approve, the proposal is executed. But then, if another reviewer who previously voted to reject flips their vote and votes to approve, all of a sudden we would go from executing the proposal to not executing it. That would be kind of weird, because the proposal only got more votes. So why would it cause us to go from implementing the proposal to rejecting it? But that kind of thing is allowed at the level of generality at which we've defined the mechanism. And so it'll be natural sometimes to restrict attention to monotone authorization rules, which just forbid that from happening.

A monotone authorization rule is just one such that if I have a winning coalition, adding people to the winning coalition keeps it a winning coalition. Whenever I have a set of votes that is enough for me to execute the proposal, having more votes to execute the proposal only helps. Formally, that just means for any winning coalition \(S\) that is a subset of a larger coalition \(T\), the larger coalition is also a winning coalition. You can't have this weird behavior where additional votes cause you to reject when you wouldn't have otherwise.

And trying to rule out the trivial rule, we're going to require that if everybody votes to implement the proposal, then we implement it. The only monotone approval rule that doesn't have the complete set of reviewers as a winning coalition is the one that just ignores the reviewers entirely and doesn't do anything. That's not a useful system, so we will just require in our definition of a monotone approval rule that it's not trivial: if everyone votes to accept, we'll execute the proposal.

10The Goal: Never Authorize a Harmful Proposal

We want a safety guarantee. Our object of study will be a safety guarantee. It says something pretty simple. A sound approval rule only executes proposals that the principal themselves would have executed had they been given the choice. It might be more conservative than the principal. It might reject stuff that the principal would have executed, but it cannot execute stuff that the principal would have rejected. That's why it's a safety guarantee. It's potentially less aggressive at approval than the principal would have been, but the fact that the approval rule executes some proposal is a certificate that it is safe, in the sense that the principal would have made the same decision.

Formally, an authorization rule is sound if, for every proposal, if the set of reviewers that sincerely approve of that proposal, the people who vote to approve, forms a winning coalition, meaning our system will execute the proposal, it must be that the proposal was not harmful, in the sense that the principal gets non-negative utility for that proposal. They would have executed it too. Execution never lowers the principal's utility relative to the baseline. Definition makes sense?

This is not all you would want of an authorization scheme. This thing could be too conservative. It could reject too much stuff. But at least it's not going to do stuff that you wouldn't have done. Definition makes sense? I require this in the worst case: not on average over some distribution of proposals, but for all proposals.

12Exactly Which Authorization Rules Are Sound?

Okay. So the theorem is quite simple and should remind you very much of the theorem we proved last class for aligned outcomes in the equilibria of the competition of these LLM markets, which is that this coalitional coverage condition is the thing that characterizes safety. In the utility-theoretic model we've written down, give me a monotone authorization rule, which is just defined by its set of winning coalitions. The question of whether this monotone authorization rule is safe is exactly the same question as whether every single one of the winning coalitions that defines the monotone authorization rule satisfies the coalitional coverage condition.

Coalitional coverage exactly characterizes soundness, in that if for every winning coalition in your monotone authorization rule I have coalitional coverage, meaning it is possible to write the principal's utility as a non-negative sum of the utilities of the reviewers in that coalition, then the rule is sound. And if there is even one winning coalition for which it is not possible to do this, then the rule is not sound. Okay, so this is a two-way characterization.

13Sufficiency: Coverage Makes Approval Safe

So the forward direction is very simple, quite similar to what we saw last time. And the backwards direction is our Harsanyi aggregation theorem, which we proved in lecture three.

So, let's consider the forward direction. Suppose every winning coalition covers the principal. We need to establish safety. What that means is, for any proposal that is authorized, we need to show it has non-negative utility for the principal. Let's take any proposal \(d\). Because we assume this is a proposal that is authorized, we know that the set of sincerely approving reviewers is one of the winning coalitions. That's what it means for the proposal to be authorized.

Now, because we're trying to prove the forward direction, we know that every winning coalition satisfies coalitional coverage. In particular, the set of sincerely approving reviewers must satisfy coalitional coverage, which means there exists a set of non-negative weights so that I can write the principal's utility as the weighted sum of the utilities of the sincerely approving reviewers. But what it means that a reviewer is sincerely approving is that their utility for the proposal is non-negative. And so representing the principal's utility as this non-negative linear combination of the utilities of the sincerely approving reviewers lets me write it as a non-negative weighted linear combination of terms, each of which I know is non-negative.

The fact that each reviewer is sincerely approving certifies to me that each of these constituent terms is non-negative, and it stays non-negative when I multiply it by a non-negative weight. And so I immediately get that the principal's utility is also non-negative, which establishes soundness of the rule. If all of the reviewers approve, then it must be that the [principal's] utility is non-negative. This is true for all proposals. We get soundness from this coalitional coverage.

14An Uncovered Coalition Can Approve Harm

The reverse direction is really just our Harsanyi aggregation theorem. Just remembering what the theorem said: suppose I've got a utility function that satisfies the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, which means I can represent it as maximizing an expectation. Then suppose it satisfies the Pareto property, which just means that for any distribution on outcomes that every external party agrees is beneficial, that every external party has non-negative utility for, the social aggregate utility is also non-negative. That was what we viewed as our minimal desirable property of a social aggregation function: at the very least, if everybody agreed that something was beneficial, the social aggregation function should also agree that it's beneficial. Then what Harsanyi's aggregation theorem said was that the form of the social aggregation function had to be that of a weighted social welfare function. It had to be some non-negative sum of individual utilities.

Okay. We just say, okay, the constituent parties are the reviewers, and the thing that we are thinking of as the aggregate utility function is the utility of the principal. And Harsanyi's aggregation theorem directly says, if we want the safety property that approval by all of the reviewers implies non-negative utility for the principal, it's got to be that the set of reviewers satisfies the coalitional coverage condition: that we can write the utility function as the non-negative weighted sum of the utility functions of the reviewers.

It's been a little while since we proved this, but just to remind you of what the proof looked like: this followed from linear programming duality. What we did is we wrote down the linear program that had constraints, one for each reviewer, that the proposal had to be approved. The proposal had to give non-negative utility. And then the linear program was: find the proposal, subject to the constraints that all of the reviewers would approve it, that minimizes the principal's utility. And the safety condition means that the optimal value of that linear program cannot be negative. It's got to be zero. The safety condition means there does not exist a proposal that all of the reviewers would approve that the principal would have negative utility for. That would be a violation of safety. So the linear program is just directly trying to find a violation of safety. And if there is none, that means the optimal value of that linear program is zero. And then the dual of the linear program constructively gives you the weights that certify coalitional [coverage]. The weights are the dual variables of the linear program.

I won't go through that again; that was the proof of Harsanyi's aggregation theorem that we did in lecture three. But this is the same phenomenon. This is very much an application of Harsanyi's aggregation theorem. The forward direction is kind of easy; the backward direction is where something interesting is happening, but we've already proven the backwards direction. That was lecture three. Make sense?

And for us, if there's some winning coalition that doesn't cover the principal, that means that the implication that is the premise of Harsanyi's aggregation theorem fails. So there must be a proposal \(d\) that is beneficial for all of the reviewers, hence approved by all of the reviewers sincerely, and yet is harmful for the principal.

15Necessity: The Harmful Proposal Must Execute

So, the coalitional alignment condition is one that can be applied to any coalition. So in the reverse direction, suppose we did have some winning coalition that did not cover the principal. By the failure of Harsanyi's aggregation theorem, that means that there is a proposal that would cause everyone in this winning coalition to vote to approve, and yet would be harmful to the principal.

Now, there might be other people who approve as well, right? That doesn't mean that this winning coalition is the entire set of people who vote to approve. But it does mean that the winning coalition is a subset of the set of reviewers who sincerely vote to approve. And because we have a monotone authorization rule, that subset is enough to guarantee that we approve. We're using monotonicity in the reverse direction. The forward direction didn't require monotonicity.

But this constructively gives a proposal that certifies a failure of safety: a proposal that would be approved by a winning coalition and yet would be harmful. When I say constructively, I mean, if you show me the winning coalition that doesn't satisfy coalitional coverage, there's just a linear program I can write down that'll give me the harmful proposal. 17Which Approval Thresholds Are Sound?

Okay, so the most conservative approval rule that I could use is one that requires unanimous approval. With unanimous approval, there's only one winning coalition. It's the set of all of the reviewers. And what characterizes safety then is the weakest of all of the conditions we're talking about, which is just coverage by the full collection of reviewers, the full panel of reviewers.

But that might be an overly conservative rule, in the sense that if I require unanimous approval, I'm going to reject lots of stuff. For example, there's a natural family of monotone approval rules that are just thresholded votes. I could say, look, I'm going to approve if the majority votes to approve. That's thresholding at [a majority]. Or I could consider other thresholds. I could say, okay, if anyone votes to approve, I'm going to approve. That's thresholding at one. Or I could say, nobody has veto power, but if any two people disapprove, I won't do it. That's thresholding at \(q = n - 2\).

So for any such rule, the characterization of safety is exactly that every subset of \(q\) of the reviewers must cover the principal. Or said another way, the coverage by the full panel must be robust enough that I still have coverage even if I go and delete an arbitrary subset of \(n - q\) of the reviewers. So safety under a more permissive rule requires a stronger condition. It requires coverage in some more robust way, robust to deletions. If I'm going to allow any two people to vote to reject and still approve, I need that I should still have coverage even after I remove any two of the reviewers. That's, again, the if-and-only-if characterization of safety under that kind of approval rule.

18Why Would Reviewers Vote Sincerely?

Okay. So, maybe just to briefly talk about strategic reviewers. On the one hand, we're modeling these reviewer agents as if they have preferences. We're saying, look, they've got utility functions; they're evaluating proposals under these utility functions. But on the other hand, we've been talking about sincere voting. They're not acting so as to maximize their utility necessarily under the underlying [utility functions]. So what happens if they do? Suppose we don't want to assume sincere voting anymore.

Well, mostly things are going to be okay still, because by restricting reviewers to being able to issue only binary preferences, approve or disapprove, rather than expressing a numeric-valued preference, we've kind of aligned (if we have a monotone authorization rule at least; a lack of monotonicity would mess this up), under a monotone authorization rule, the incentives of the reviewers with the behavior of the system.

Because under monotonicity, if you change your vote from disapproval to approval, there's only two things that can happen. Either your vote wasn't pivotal, and nothing changes. Or, if it was pivotal, it can only be pivotal in the direction of the change in vote. By changing your vote from disapproving to approving, you can cause the system to change from rejecting a proposal to executing it, which will change your utility as a reviewer from zero, the utility that you get if nothing was executed, to whatever your utility for the proposal is.

Which means that sincere voting is a weakly dominant strategy. Meaning, fixing whatever everyone else does, it is never worse for you to vote sincerely than it is to vote insincerely. Because voting sincerely corresponds to voting to execute exactly when this quantity is positive. And the only effect that your vote has is either nothing, or to change your utility by exactly the amount of your utility for the proposal. So you'd want to execute that change when the change was positive, and otherwise not.

And one implication of this is that sincere voting is always a Nash equilibrium. But it's not the only one. You could ask if every Nash equilibrium needs to be safe.

19Majority Can Be Unsafe Even With Aligned Reviewers

And it's pretty easy to see that, at the generality [at which we've defined] things, it's not. And the reason is just because, for general approval rules, we can come up with situations in which all of the reviewers are unhappy with what's happening, but none of them are pivotal. None of them can do anything about it.

So just as a simple example, suppose we've got three reviewers, and in fact, this is an easy case: they're all perfectly aligned. There's no misalignment. We could have delegated approval to any one of them. They would have done exactly what the principal would have done. But that's not what we did. We instead decided to subject them to majority vote. So we'll do whatever any two of them say.

Suppose we've got a harmful proposal: the principal's, and therefore all of the reviewers', utility is negative one. But let's say that all three vote to approve. Everybody's unhappy with this. But this is still a Nash equilibrium, because nobody is pivotal. Nobody has the power to stop this bad thing from happening through their unilateral deviation. So everyone's utility is negative one independently of what they do, and in particular, everyone voting to approve is also a Nash equilibrium of this game.

20Unanimity Prevents Harm at Every Equilibrium

But this kind of artificially bad Nash equilibrium [goes away] if everybody always has the power to be pivotal in the direction of rejecting a proposal. So if our approval rule is the maximally conservative one, the one that requires unanimous approval, equivalently, if we endow everybody with the power to veto a proposal, anyone can unilaterally cause the proposal not to execute by voting against it. Then the same condition that guarantees that we get safety under sincere voting, that the full panel covers the principal, is also sufficient to get safety at every Nash equilibrium of the strategic game. Sincere voting is always a Nash equilibrium, no matter what the approval rule is, but under the unanimous approval rule, it's also the only one. Sincere voting is the only Nash equilibrium.

21Why Veto Power Prevents Harm

And this is pretty simple to see, but let's just walk through it. Let's say we've got some particular proposal \(d\). And suppose we have voting, perhaps not sincere, under some Nash equilibrium. People are allowed to randomize. So let's say that \(\rho\) is the probability that the proposal is executed, and because people are potentially randomizing, \(\rho\) could be some probability strictly between 0 and 1. And so at this equilibrium, I can write reviewer \(i\)'s expected utility as \(\rho\), the probability the proposal executes, times their utility for the proposal in the event that it executes.

Now, what it means to be in this Nash equilibrium is that no reviewer has the power to unilaterally change their action in a way that would improve [their utility]. Now, under the unanimous approval rule, every reviewer has veto power. So every reviewer has the ability to unilaterally change their action to reject, which causes the proposal not to execute and would cause them to have utility zero. So the Nash condition is exactly that their utility in equilibrium, which is \(\rho\) times their utility for the proposal if it executes, must be non-negative. Because if it was negative, there would be a beneficial deviation: veto the proposal.

And [under] coverage, I can write the utility of the principal, which is similarly the probability that the proposal executes times the principal's utility if it executes, as a non-negative combination of the reviewers' utilities. That's the coalitional alignment condition. And once again, each of these terms is non-negative, which means the [principal's] expected utility must also be non-negative. The [principal's] expected utility is the product of two terms. One is the probability that the proposal executes, and the other is the utility of the proposal if it executes. And the only way it can be non-negative, if the utility of the proposal if it executes is negative, is if the probability it executes is zero, which is exactly the safety condition: that every harmful proposal must be rejected with certainty.

So, to summarize: we analyzed safety under this premise of sincere voting, but in fact sincere voting is always a Nash equilibrium, even if you think of the reviewers as strategic, and in fact the only Nash equilibrium when you use the unanimous approval rule.

22Can We Approve Every Proposal the Human Would?

Good. So far we've been talking only about safety, or soundness. We want to reject everything that is harmful to the principal. But we've allowed that our rule could be extremely conservative. One trivial way to get soundness would be to reject everything. We didn't allow that in our definitions, because we said unanimous approval always needs to execute the proposal. But if we hadn't written that in our definitions, there would be a trivial way to obtain safety, which would be to reject everything. So safety on its own is not valuable unless it is paired with some kind of utility guarantee. You want to say you're safe, and also that sometimes you actually do useful stuff. And at the extreme, you'd want to do all of the useful stuff.

So just as you can define soundness or safety, you can define something you'd really like to have, which is completeness. It's kind of the reverse condition. Safety says that harmful proposals are never executed; completeness says that beneficial proposals are always [executed]. If you had safety and completeness, you would just replicate the decision-making process of the principal. So completeness requires that for any proposal that is beneficial to the principal, the set of reviewers that sincerely approve of that proposal forms a winning coalition, so we actually execute.

So if you had soundness and completeness together, you would perfectly replicate the decision-making process of the principal. The difficulty is that if you want to do this in the setting we've defined, somehow you need to figure out how to do this from an extremely impoverished kind of feedback you're getting from the reviewers. Because you're not seeing what their utilities are. You're only seeing these binary votes. You're getting this indicator for whether their utility is positive or negative.

23The Same Votes Can Hide Opposite Principal Gains

So to see that this is probably an obstacle, that the fact that you're only getting this binary signal is probably an obstacle to getting soundness and completeness together, let's just go back to our initial toy example, where the principal cares about the sum of two components and the reviewers care about one or the other.

The difficulty is we can come up with two proposals that have opposite properties for the principal. The first proposal has utility one for the principal, so the first proposal is beneficial for the principal. The second proposal has utility minus one for the principal. The second proposal is harmful to the principal. And yet, both of these proposals appear to be identical as viewed through the sincere votes of the reviewers. For both of these proposals, the first reviewer votes to approve, since the first coefficient is positive, and the second reviewer votes to reject, since the second coefficient is negative.

So we just do not have enough information to distinguish between these two proposals. They look exactly the same through the lens of the set of sincerely approving reviewers. And so we have to make the same decision for both of them, despite the fact that one of them is harmful for the principal and the other one is beneficial. So there's no rule observing only the set of approving votes that will accept the beneficial one and reject the harmful one, because we don't have the information to distinguish them. So already you see, at least for this example, that we can't get perfect soundness and completeness if we insist on using only these binary votes. If we want soundness, we have to give something up.

24Exact Approval Requires an Aligned Reviewer

And in fact, it's not just a property of this one example. This is kind of a robust obstruction. Let's say that a reviewer is individually aligned if their utility is just some non-negative scalar multiple of the principal's. If you had an individually aligned reviewer, you could just delegate entirely to them. They would be the coalition, and we would be sound and complete. As the reviewer has, up to scale, the same utility function we do, they would just make exactly the same decisions we would.

And what you can show is that for any utility function of the principal and any set of utility functions for the reviewers, this obstruction that we saw in our two-dimensional example wasn't specific to that example. It is fully general. Which is to say, for a set of utility functions, there is an aggregation rule that from the votes alone implements a perfectly sound and efficient rule if and only if one of the reviewers is perfectly aligned. In one direction, if there's a perfectly aligned reviewer, you can just make them the unique coalition. In the other direction, if there's not, there's a construction that will produce a pair of proposals like the one we just saw in the example, one of which is harmful, the other of which is beneficial, and yet that receive the same pattern of votes.

25Numerical Reports Can Recover the Lost Information

So, how can you get around this? How can you generalize the set of approval rules in a way that broadens your power? One way you could do it is if you said, well, what we're going to learn is actually the numerical-valued gain of the reviewer. We're going to see not just their approval vote; they're going to tell us a number, how helpful or beneficial they think this proposal is.

Of course, if we had that, then under coalitional alignment, in fact under the weaker condition that the utility function was just in the span of the reviewer utility functions, we could, from these numerical reports, if we knew the weights, reconstruct the utility function of the principal and threshold that. So a lot of the barriers that we're facing, and the nature of the characterization we saw, rely on this binary approval model. If you allowed numeric-valued approvals, then of course you could reconstruct the utility of the principal, in principle, from the numeric votes, even under the weaker condition that the principal's utility was in the span of the reviewers' utilities.

But this is requiring a lot more. First of all, it's requiring the reviewers to be able to enunciate their numeric-valued utility. So we're requiring that they adhere in a much more fine-grained way to a utility-theoretic model. It's requiring that the approval rule actually know these weights. The binary-vote approval rules we were talking about were things like "approve if everyone approves." The characterization depended on these weights existing, but nobody needed to know them to execute the rule. To execute this rule, you'd actually need to know the weights. And in this setting, you would lose truthfulness. You wouldn't necessarily get sincere voting in equilibrium.
