2Overview

So, just to review a little bit: the premise when we train our reward model is that we're going to take a bunch of pairwise comparisons that might exhibit all of the difficulties that we've been talking about over the last few lectures, for example, and maybe no clean way to organize them, to aggregate them into a total order. And yet, we're going to try to map them to a scalar reward, which will, of course, explicitly give some aggregation of them into a total order.

If we try to do that, maybe one question we would immediately ask is, what is the ordering that we're learning? We're not learning something that is maximizing social welfare. We haven't even gathered the information necessary to identify that problem. We're not somehow respecting pairwise majority votes, because we've seen that there's no total ordering that can do that. What are we doing? And what we're going to see is that the standard implementation is directly learning a social choice rule that we briefly saw before and that has a name: it's learning the Borda ordering.

And once you learn the reward model, you're not done. You train the language model to preferentially prefer high-reward completions. And typically, you're going to have a base model that comes from pre-training, and you don't want to entirely abandon everything you learned in the base model. So what you're going to optimize for in the second stage of RLHF, once you've trained the reward model, is a regularized objective. You want to optimize the reward of the model on the one hand, but on the other hand, you don't want to stray too far from the base model, so you'll add a penalty that is typically the KL divergence between the policy that you're learning and the base policy. And solving that optimization problem, once you've learned a reward model that embeds the Borda ordering, will do something, and we'll see what that is.

And finally, we'll note that choices you make early on in this optimization pipeline, for example, applying simple post-processing to the probabilities that a random user prefers completion A over completion B, can have important effects on what you end up learning through this pipeline. We'll see how a simple change can result in what you learn not being the Borda ordering, but what's called the Copeland ordering.

4The Pipeline has two parts

So here's a schematic of the RLHF pipeline. We're going to study two parts of this today. So first we gather a bunch of comparison data. We select random labelers, we select random completions, and we ask them, do you prefer completion \(i\) to completion \(j\)? And once we've done that, we try to train a reward model to fit this comparison data. It's not going to be, obviously, a perfect fit. This will be what's called a non-realizable learning problem. There will be no parameters of the reward model in general that can perfectly recover these probabilities. Again, because the reward model will need to embed a total ordering, whereas the population comparisons do not. But that's what we do initially, to train a reward model.

Then, once we have a reward model, we use that to optimize a policy. And the policy is not simply the one that puts all of its weight on the highest-reward completion, but rather is sort of balancing that, wanting to reward completions that are scored well by the reward model while making sure not to stray too far from some reference policy, like the policy that came out of pre-training. And the actual optimization problem there will solve sort of a KL-regularized version of the reward problem, and we'll also think about that and see what policy comes out.

So the standard way in which people try to do reward modeling is to fit what's called a Bradley–Terry reward model, which is this very simple thing. So remember the logistic function, which we've called a sigmoid function if you're a machine learning person. Pretty simple function. The sigmoid of \(z\) is just \(1/(1+e^{-z})\). So maybe it's helpful to think about what happens as \(z\) goes to negative infinity and \(z\) goes to infinity. As \(z\) goes to infinity, \(e^{-z}\) goes to zero, and so this is just one. As \(z\) goes to negative infinity, \(e^{-z}\) goes to infinity, and this is zero. Okay, so in the limits, we get zero and one, and in between, we get something like an S-shaped curve. It's zero on one end, one on the other, and it transitions between them in some S-shaped curve.

And here's how the Bradley–Terry model will make predictions about how a person or a population will evaluate which is better, \(i\) or \(j\). So imagine that for every completion, there is a number that's a parameter of the Bradley–Terry model. So the Bradley–Terry model is going to be parametrized, if there's \(n\) completions, by \(n\) numbers, which you should think of as being some proxy for the quality of that completion. These are the rewards, ultimately, that will be represented by the reward model.

So the Bradley–Terry model posits that there's some reward for each completion, but that the labels that we observe are not deterministic. If completion \(i\) has slightly higher reward than completion \(j\), the Bradley–Terry model doesn't say we're always going to see labelers prefer \(i\) to \(j\). What it says is we are going to see labelers prefer \(i\) to \(j\) in some proportion that scales as sigmoid of the difference between reward \(i\) and reward \(j\). Okay, so look at the difference in reward. If that difference is big in favor of \(i\), the reward for \(i\) is much bigger than the reward for \(j\), it says we're going to almost always see \(i\) preferred over \(j\). If the difference is big in the other direction, if the argument of the sigmoid function is negative, it says we're almost always going to see \(j\) preferred over \(i\). But if they're equal, we're going to see the comparison exactly tied. It should be 50–50. And it'll bias to either 0 or 1 in exponential proportion to how large the reward gap is. Yeah.

9A Comparison Label Has a Bernoulli Likelihood

Okay. So there's two completions out there, let's say, \(i\) and \(j\). Let's say that I have a label \(y\) that we'll think of as one if, when we ask a random labeler which is better, \(i\) or \(j\), they come back to us and say \(i\). And if they come back to us and say \(j\), we'll view that as the label \(y\) being zero. Then when I ask, in the population, what is the probability that \(y\) is one? That's \(p\); \(P_{ij}\) equals \(p\). Then \(y\) is just distributed as a Bernoulli random variable with probability \(p\).

Now on the other hand, I've got some Bradley–Terry parameters, and there's \(n\) parameters, the predicted reward for \(i\) and the predicted reward for \(j\), but there's \(n^2\) comparisons, for any pair \(i\) and \(j\). That's sort of why we're not going to be able to represent everything properly. What matters in the Bradley–Terry model for a comparison between \(i\) and \(j\) is the difference between the reward for \(i\) and the reward for \(j\), the difference between these parameters; let's call that \(z\). So the Bradley–Terry model predicts that the probability that \(y\) is going to be 1, in this experiment where we're comparing \(i\) and \(j\), will be \(\sigma(z)\), this score difference passed through the sigmoid function. And similarly, it predicts that the label's going to be 0, \(j\) will be preferred to \(i\), with probability \(\sigma(-z)\). By the way, since these are probabilities, this lets you read off the useful fact that the sigmoid function is odd, meaning these two probabilities have to sum to one, so \(\sigma(-z)\) had better be \(1 - \sigma(z)\).

And so I could ask, how likely is the comparison data I have seen under the Bradley–Terry model? How likely or unlikely is it that I would see the sort of comparisons that I've observed if these comparisons really were governed by some underlying Bradley–Terry model? So, given a particular parameter \(z\) governing the difference in predicted reward between \(i\) and \(j\), the likelihood under the Bradley–Terry model of observing some outcome \(y\) is the probability of that outcome \(y\) under the Bradley–Terry model, which we can write as \(\sigma(z)^y \, \sigma(-z)^{1-y}\). Because if \(y\) is 1, then this exponent is 0, and this is just \(\sigma(z)\). If \(y\) is zero, this term is one, and we just get \(\sigma(-z)\). So this is just a way of writing algebraically that the likelihood of seeing \(y = 1\) is \(\sigma(z)\), and the likelihood of seeing \(y = 0\) is \(\sigma(-z)\). It's a compact way of writing that. That's the likelihood function.

Now, we're going to try to fit parameters to maximize the likelihood of the data. That's the optimization problem that's going to be solved. Maximizing some function like the likelihood is the same optimization problem as maximizing any monotone transformation of it. If I apply a monotone transformation, that doesn't change where the optimizer is. And so often, including in this case,

24KL Penalizes Departure from the Reference Policy

So the way we penalize departure from the base policy is generally through KL divergence. So if you haven't seen it before, here's what it is. Say I have two distributions. Say \(\pi\) is the policy I'm trying to learn; \(\pi_0\) is my base policy. These are just distributions over completions. The KL divergence between these two distributions, \(\pi\) and \(\pi_0\), is just the expectation, when I sample a random completion from \(\pi\), of the log likelihood ratio of the probability of that completion under my policy \(\pi\) and the base policy.

So if the probability is the same under my policy and the base policy, then the ratio is one, and the log of the ratio is zero. If it's more likely under my policy, the log odds ratio is positive; if it's less likely under my policy, it's negative. And the KL divergence is just asking: what is the expectation, over the randomness of a randomly sampled completion from my policy, of this log odds ratio? KL divergence is always non-negative. It's a couple of lines of algebra. It's not obvious that it's always non-negative, but it is. And it is equal to zero only when the two policies are identical. If the two policies are identical, the odds ratio is always one, so the log odds ratio is always zero, so the expected log odds ratio is zero.

25Policy Optimization Trades Reward Against Policy Change

And the optimization problem that follows reward modeling is generally this. We want to find a policy that maximizes, on the one hand, the expected reward as predicted by the reward model, the equation that we outlined, but on the other hand, doesn't stray too far from the base model. And the way we combine those two desiderata is by combining them in the same objective. We want to optimize some linear combination of, on the one hand, the reward objective, and on the other hand, the penalty for straying too far in KL divergence from the base policy. Okay.

By Lagrangian duality, this kind of regularized or penalized objective is really the same thing as constrained optimization. This is the same thing as saying, I would like to find the policy that maximizes reward, but I'm not optimizing over all policies. I'm optimizing only over some KL ball around the base policy, and how wide that ball is that I'm optimizing over is controlled by \(\tau\), the weight that I put on the regularization term. If \(\tau\) is zero, I'm just going to optimize only for reward, and I'll find the Borda winner. If \(\tau\) is infinity, I will output the base policy unmodified, and in between, I'll do something in between.
