1Blackwell Approachability

Last class we talked about both calibration and decision calibration, and why it was a desirable property we might want to have that provided a nice trustworthy interface between prediction and decision making for a variety of downstream decision makers. And we talked about it more generally than we needed to. We didn't talk about it as a statistical property of a model evaluated on a distribution. We talked about it as a property of an arbitrary sequence of predictions and outcomes, which of course generalizes a statistical model, but it makes sense to talk about even in dynamically, adversarially changing environments in which there's no distribution.

But we didn't actually give an algorithm for obtaining these guarantees. And so that's what I want to do this class, and in so doing, introduce to you a very useful tool called Blackwell approachability that we'll also be able to make use of in other settings.

2Overview

So you should think of Blackwell approachability as providing a general method for satisfying not just one constraint, like the desire to get low regret, for example, but for simultaneously satisfying a lot of constraints at the same time in a difficult online adversarial environment, an environment where you need to make choices sequentially and outcomes are chosen by an adversary.

One way to think about it is as the natural generalization of the minimax problem from one dimension. We talked about the minimax theorem for zero-sum games that were defined with a scalar objective. Someone wants to minimize a number, someone else wants to maximize the same number. Blackwell approachability is kind of the analog of the minimax theorem when you generalize from numbers to vectors. That's why it's going to give us a multi-objective kind of flavor.

And we will see that among the many things you can do with Blackwell approachability, one of them is to obtain things like decision calibration online, because we can encode the guarantees that we wanted for decision calibration, essentially that our forecasts should be unbiased conditional on the action they induce for any downstream decision maker. We can encode those as coordinates in a vector that we want to control. And that's exactly going to be what Blackwell approachability lets us do.

And so as our first application of Blackwell approachability, and there will be more of them, we'll see how to get decision-calibrated forecasts, not just for one downstream decision maker, but simultaneously for a whole bunch of them, even when outcomes are chosen adversarially. That lets us give this aligned interface between prediction and decision making simultaneously for different downstream users who might be consuming our forecasts and might want different things; we'll be able to provide for all of them at the same time.

3Recall: Decision Calibration Uses Action Cells

Let me start with a quick recap of decision calibration, just so that we can have one concrete application in mind. Then I'm going to want to generalize to Blackwell approachability. And finally, once we give an algorithm for Blackwell approachability, I'll specialize once more and show how to apply it to decision calibration.

But just to recall, we are thinking about an online, sequential forecasting setting where the forecaster produces a probability distribution over some set of outcomes. There could be a lot of these outcomes, but the forecast is a distribution. It assigns a probability between 0 and 1 to each of the outcomes. And then downstream decision makers, and there might be multiple of them, will consume our forecasts and decide to take some action. The premise here is that the thing we are forecasting is relevant to them. And if we are able to produce decision-calibrated forecasts, they will have strong decision-theoretic justification for trusting our forecasts and acting as if they are correct, which is to say, taking the action that is the best response to our forecast, the action that would maximize their expected utility if our forecast was in fact correct.

So say that the downstream decision maker, who for now we'll imagine has a fixed utility function \(u\), takes action \(a_t\) at each round that is just the best response to our forecast. Now, after this happens, we observe what the actual outcome was. One of the outcomes, \(y_t\), is observed. Let's just encode that as the vector encoding the trivial probability distribution that puts all of its weight on the outcome that actually occurred.

And to define decision calibration, we thought about the following quantities, one for each action the decision maker might take. Call this \(B_a\), the bias for action \(a\), and it's the sum over all of the rounds for which our forecast induced the decision maker to take that action. Our forecasts affect what action is taken. So we're summing up only over the rounds for which our forecast induced some fixed action \(a\). And we're just looking at the residual of our forecasts, the vector-valued bias of our forecasts: the difference between the empirical histogram for the sequence of outcomes that actually occurred and the sum of the probability forecasts that we made. If our forecasts were correct, then these two quantities should converge to the same thing.

And decision calibration was just the condition that our forecasts should have no bias, even conditional on the action they induce, for each of the actions. So perfect decision calibration is the constraint that the action-cell bias should be exactly zero for every action. Of course, even if we were in a stochastic setting and knew the distribution, we'd expect sampling error. So we wouldn't expect to be able to get this to be exactly zero. And so the way we quantified approximation error is by summing up the norm of each of these biases. The biases are vectors, so we can look at their norm, their Euclidean norm, for example, and we measured our decision calibration error just by summing up the norm of each of these bias cells.

4The Forecast Determines Which Bias Is Updated

What we did is we used a bound on decision calibration to control swap regret. So what we're going to think about today is: algorithmically, how can we make forecasts that control swap regret via controlling decision calibration, even against an adaptive adversary?

And you can think about this a little bit as a control problem, where the forecast we make affects two things. First of all, the forecast induces the action the downstream decision maker takes, and therefore controls which of these bins will be updated, which of these bins will accumulate an additional bias iterate, if you like. But it also controls what that bias iterate is, because the bias iterate is the difference between the outcome, which we don't control, and the forecast we just made.

And so in trying to think about how to solve this problem, there's this self-referential aspect that we need to untangle, which is that the forecast has two roles in how we update the score. It controls the one-round bias increment that is added to one of these buckets, but it also controls which of the buckets is going to be updated.

5The Setting

So just to lay out the setting, the online prediction setting here: there's \(d\) possible outcomes, let's say, and we are going to forecast a distribution over them. For reasons that will become clear when we actually solve the problem, essentially because at some key moment we're going to want to apply the minimax theorem, we need to restrict the forecaster to a finite set of possible forecasts. So although there's continuously many probability distributions that we could predict, we are going to restrict the forecaster a priori to predicting on some finite grid. That's what this \(P_\eta\) thing is. It'll be an \(\eta\)-net. But for now, the only thing to keep in mind is this is some discretization of the set of probability forecasts that we could in principle make, and there's only finitely many.

And we'll make randomized forecasts. So what happens is, in rounds, first the forecaster announces a distribution over forecasts, some probability distribution over this grid. And then the environment, or if you like an adversary, gets to see the forecast distribution that we propose, although not the realization of the randomness that we'll ultimately sample from it. And as a function of both the history and the forecast distribution, it chooses an outcome. The outcomes can be adversarially chosen as a function of everything that is known up through round \(t\).

Then we actually produce a forecast by sampling from the distribution that we committed to upfront, which we broadcast and reveal to downstream users. And any particular downstream user who has, say, utility function \(u\) will best respond to that forecast, which induces some action they take. And then the outcome is revealed. We know what the residual increment is that's going to be added to one of the bias cells, and we add it to the cell indexed by the action of the downstream decision maker.

So this is the environment our algorithm is going to be working in, and the goal is to find a way to produce forecasts so that even in the worst case, the decision calibration error is bounded, and hence the swap regret of the decision maker.

6Action-Cell Biases Form One Joint Vector

So just to pin down some accounting details, we're going to keep track of a vector. For each action, we are accumulating these residual vectors: what is the difference between the outcomes that actually occurred and our forecasts on the days when we made a prediction that induced that action? What we're going to do here is we're just going to concatenate those vectors together and keep track of them all as one. And we'll call that big concatenated vector that's keeping track of our accumulated residuals for each downstream action \(g(p, y)\). So \(g(p, y)\) is a single increment of this residual vector when the learner forecasts \(p\) and the adversary chooses outcome \(y\).

So as I said, forecasts are \(d\)-dimensional vectors. So if I look at the residual, that's the difference between my forecast and the outcome. That's a \(d\)-dimensional vector. We have one of those \(d\)-dimensional vectors for each possible action the downstream decision maker might play. And having concatenated those all together, we have a vector whose dimension is \(d\), the dimension of the residual vector, times the cardinality of the action space, because we have one for each action.

And what does each block of that vector look like, each block corresponding to a single action \(a\)? Well, it will be zeroed out if the forecast does not induce action \(a\). That's what the indicator function is doing. This is one if the downstream decision maker takes action \(a\); otherwise it's zero. So that's just saying the block corresponding to an action will be nonzero only if the downstream decision maker takes that action. And if it's nonzero, it's going to be the residual, the thing that we're summing up, the difference between the actual outcome and the forecast.

So this is just the one-round increment of our action-block biases concatenated together. Because of this indicator, and because the downstream decision maker takes one particular action and all of the other blocks are zeroed out, at any given moment only one of these action blocks is nonzero. And if we look at the average of these vectors summed over time, where we're looking at the increment vector at each time step \(t\) induced by the forecast \(p_t\) made by the learner and the outcome \(y_t\) chosen by the adversary, we've constructed things exactly so that we get the average value of the action-conditional bias in each block, […].

And so you can view the goal of making forecasts that are decision calibrated, that have good decision calibration error, as a vector control problem. There's a vector that's going to arise from averaging together these increments. We have some control over what these increments look like. The increments depend on an action we take, \(p\), as well as an action the adversary takes, \(y\). And what we need to do is make predictions that guide the average value of this vector to some small neighborhood of zero in the worst case, no matter what the adversary does. And that's the thing that Blackwell approachability is going to let us do.

7Why Controlling the Joint Vector Is Enough

So just to, again, remember what we proved last time and motivate why we want to make decision calibration errors small: we showed last time that the downstream decision maker will have swap regret bounded by at most the decision calibration error, the sum of the norms of the action-conditional biases.

Now, there's a slight mismatch between the decision calibration error and what we get by controlling the norm of this big concatenated vector, but it's only a very small mismatch. The swap regret was controlled by the sum of the norms of the individual action-conditional bias vectors. Whereas if we just control the norm of this vector that concatenates all of those things together, we might have overshot by a little bit. But by Cauchy–Schwarz, we won't have overshot by more than a factor of root of the number of actions.

So all of this is just to say that if we can come up with a technology that lets us make predictions that control the norm of this big concatenated vector, we will have guaranteed that the downstream decision maker has low swap regret. And that's what we're going to try to do using this Blackwell approachability machinery.

8Predict within a Finite Grid

So maybe one more note about this thing that I mentioned at the beginning, that we're going to have to predict within a finite grid. This machinery will require that the action space of the learner is finite, for the same reason that the minimax theorem, the version we saw, was about finite games. But there's continuously many probability forecasts we can make. That's okay. We can take some finite net of those forecasts, an \(\eta\)-net, that has the property that for each of the continuous forecasts, there's something in our finite net that's pretty close, within \(\eta\). And we can make \(\eta\) as small as we want.

So, going back to maybe a moral point I made earlier in the course, things being finite isn't a very big limitation if you allow them to be very, very big. So we can take a very big finite set and we can make \(\eta\) as small as we want. And there's still some cost to being able to make forecasts only on some grid of granularity \(\eta\). It means that no matter what happens, we're not going to be able to drive the forecast bias down to zero, because merely by being unable to reproduce the actual outcome distribution, we will suffer some unavoidable loss, \(\eta\). But what we'll see is that we are able to control this vector, to drive the action-cell bias vector into this set of vectors that have magnitude at most \(\eta\). It's pretty close to zero, especially if we make \(\eta\) very small. So this is some detail worth keeping in mind, that we are dealing with some finite information.

But now I want to forget about decision calibration for a moment. We'll come back to it again at the end of the lecture. And now I just want to think about how to algorithmically solve this more general problem of playing some online vector control game, where we would like the aggregate of the vectors to get arbitrarily close to some convex target set \(C\). That's a generalization of the problem that we need to study here, but let's solve the more general version of the problem and then see what it does for us here.

9Abstract to a Finite Vector Game

So here's the more general version of the problem, and I think the right way to think about this is as some vector-valued generalization of a zero-sum game. So it's going to be a two-player game. We're going to have actions of the learner, let's call those \(x\); we're going to have actions of the environment, let's call those \(y\); and we'll now have some arbitrary function called \(g\) that maps a pair of actions, one for the learner and one for the adversary, to an \(m\)-dimensional vector. For a two-player zero-sum game, it would map actions of the learner and the adversary to just a number, and one of them would want to make the number big and the other one would want to make it small. Now it's mapping these actions to a vector, and we have to specify what the players want.

And the way this game is going to be played is iteratively, and the learner is forced to go first. So at the beginning of round \(t\), the learner must announce a mixed strategy, a distribution over their action set. The environment, as a function of that mixed strategy, can then choose a response, an action \(y_t\). But it can't depend on the realized randomness. You can choose what you're going to do after I tell you I'm going to randomize between rock and paper and scissors, but not after I draw a rock from the distribution. And after this happens, this fixes everyone's samples from their randomness, fixes an action of the learner and an action of the environment; plug those into this function \(g\), and that fixes an outcome vector.

Now, this is just a more general problem setup than decision calibration. For decision calibration, the action set of the learner is just this \(\eta\)-net over the set of probability forecasts. The \(g\) function is the concatenated action biases. But this can model lots of other stuff too. And so now, let's think about what the goals are of the two players in this game.

10Approachability Controls an Average Vector

So in the one-dimensional case, where this \(g\) function just outputs a number, in a zero-sum game one player would like to make the number small, and the other player would like to make the number big. Or another way of saying that is, if we have a value of the game \(v\), one player would like to make the average payoff be less than or equal to \(v\), to fall into the interval from negative infinity to \(v\). And the other player would like to make it big, or said another way, would like it to fall into the interval from \(v\) to infinity.

So in the one-dimensional case, there are these particular convex sets, the half interval to the left or to the right of \(v\), that the two players would like to get the average to fall into. It was simpler to think about the one-dimensional case as just a player who wants to make the number small and a player who wants to make the number big. But towards this generalization, it's maybe helpful to note that players are trying to make the average outcome converge to these convex sets.

So that's the perspective that we're going to take in this generalization. There's going to be some closed convex set \(C\), which now lives in some \(m\)-dimensional space and doesn't have to be something simple like an interval, and one of the players would like to cause the average outcome to converge to this set \(C\), and the other one would like to stop them from being able to do that. So that's the sense in which it's generalizing a zero-sum game. It's this adversarial thing; they want opposite things. One player wants the average outcome to converge to \(C\). The other player wants the average outcome not to converge to \(C\). \(C\) is going to be some convex set.

Now, what do we even mean when we talk about the distance of a point to a set? Well, we just mean: what is the distance from the closest point in the set to our point? And we'll measure distance in the usual Euclidean norm. And we'll say that an algorithm, or a randomized strategy, is able to approach this convex set \(C\) in this game if against all possible strategies that your opponent might take, you are able to make forecasts that guarantee that, at least in the limit, as time grows large, when I look at the average of the outcome vectors you induce through your play, the distance between that time average of the outcome vectors and the set that you're trying to approach converges to zero.

Does this make sense? We're playing this game every day. What is realized is some outcome vector. What we want is that over time, the average of these outcome vectors should get closer and closer to this set \(C\). We win the game against our opponent if we're able to achieve that, and otherwise we lose. We need to be able to achieve it for all strategies of our opponent. Yes?

11Response Satisfiability Is a One-Round Condition

Okay. There's going to be something that is very minimax-y going on here, in that we're going to think about reversal of order of play. So first, this vector-valued function that takes as input actions of the learner and the adversary and outputs a vector: let's extend that function to mixed strategies of the learner and the adversary. If the learner is going to play some distribution \(\rho\), and the adversary is going to play some distribution \(Q\), if we plug these mixed strategies into this function \(g\) that outputs a vector, let's evaluate that in expectation, by just sampling an action from the two distributions and asking what is the expected value of the vector that is realized.

So now here is a condition that you can evaluate that reverses order of play. It's the minimax-style step here. Let's say that some target set \(C\), the set that the learner would like to approach, is response satisfiable if for every distribution the adversary could fix over their actions, where the adversary is going first, there would exist some distribution for the learner, that could now be chosen as a function of the adversary's distribution (the learner is now going second), such that the expected value of the vector that results when the learner randomizes according to \(\rho\) and the adversary randomizes according to \(Q\) lies within the target set \(C\).

So response satisfiability means that if the learner got to go second, if the learner got to see what the adversary was going to do, then they can always guarantee that, at least in expectation, day by day, the realized outcome vector would lie within the convex set \(C\). This is kind of what the learner wants to do, except response satisfiability says they can do it if they have the advantageous position of going second. Now, of course, this is a condition that perhaps we can evaluate, but it is in the reverse order of play from what's actually going on. In the actual game, the learner needs to go first and the adversary gets the benefit of going second.

What we're going to show is a minimax-like statement that says: if we have response satisfiability, if we have this reversed-order-of-play feasibility, then there is an algorithm, a very simple algorithm that we'll see in a moment, that will give something a little bit weaker than the minimax theorem does. It doesn't just let us reverse the order of quantifiers and have a single strategy that will guarantee that the expected outcome is in the set no matter what the adversary does. That's what the minimax theorem would give us. But it gives us something almost as good, which is to say it will give us a dynamic strategy that we can play in this repeated interaction that will cause the time average to approach the set.

So it's morally an analog of the minimax theorem, but now for \(d\) dimensions instead of one. The thing it gives you is a little bit weaker, in that it now actually requires this time-average play, rather than being a statement about the ability to reverse order of play in a single-shot game.

12Minimax Is One-Dimensional Approachability

So just to note, we talked about this a little bit, but the minimax theorem is kind of the one-dimensional, one-shot version of Blackwell approachability. For a regular zero-sum game, which is the one-dimensional version, the target is always this half line: the minimization player would like the average payoff to be between minus infinity and \(v\), which is some convex set. Response satisfiability in the one-dimensional case just says they can always guarantee that the cost is at most \(v\) if they get to go second. And the minimax theorem, which we proved earlier in this class, says: aha, they can always guarantee this when they go second; reverse the order of quantifiers and establish that they can also guarantee exactly the same thing when they go first.

And here, we never needed to talk about time or approachability. We could just reverse the order of quantifiers. This was something that held true at one round if they go second. This is something that held true at one round if they go first. The corresponding statement is unfortunately not true in \(d\) dimensions. That's a special property of one-dimensional games. But something almost as good is true, which is what we're going to show: in reversing the order of quantifiers, we can still approach the target over time.

13Blackwell's Guarantee

So here's the theorem. Let's parse it first, and then we'll prove it. So fix any Blackwell approachability instance. There can be an arbitrary action set for the learner and the adversary, \(X\) and \(Y\). Let's say they're finite for now. And let's have a set that we would like to approach that lives in this ambient \(m\)-dimensional space, and it should be a convex closed set.

And we need to talk about some scale parameter. When we talk about the rate of approachability, it will matter for us if these vectors that we're adding up have components that have scale on the order of 1 or on the order of a billion. Once we have to talk about rates, of course we care about scale. If the convex set included 0, then this scale \(G\) that we care about would just be the maximum norm of any vector that this thing puts out at us. We're just going to prove something slightly more general that allows us to translate things. What really matters is not the absolute scale of these vectors but rather the scale of the vectors we're adding up in relationship to the set that we're trying to approach. So the way we're going to get at that is we'll define some anchor point inside the convex set that we can use to define the scale. And the scale constant \(G\) is just the maximum norm of the vector increment that can be produced minus the anchor point. If the set contains zero, you can just pick the anchor point to be zero, and it would just be the maximum norm of the vector increment. That's just a scale constant.

And the theorem says: look, if the set is response satisfiable, if we could arrange to be inside the set in expectation if we knew what the adversary was going to do ahead of time, then even in the order of play in which we don't, in which we have to go first but can play this game repeatedly, if I look after \(T\) rounds at the squared distance between the average vector and the set that I'm trying to approach, in expectation that squared distance will go down at a rate of \(1/T\). So we will approach the set: the squared distance goes down at a rate of \(1/T\), or if you don't square the distance, the Euclidean distance to the target set goes down as \(1/\sqrt{T}\). \(T\) is how long we play the game for. That gives a regret-like guarantee, with rates that maybe seem familiar, like \(\sqrt{T}\) rates. But now what we're satisfying is really about some vector-valued [quantity's distance] to the set. Its distance to the set is a function of this \(m\)-dimensional vector. Makes sense?

15Projection Identifies a Current Direction of Failure

Good. So here's what we're going to do. There's this convex set that we're trying to approach. And there's the progress we've made so far: we're paying attention to the average of these vector increments over time. At round \(t\), we've seen \(t-1\) of them. So we have the average of the \(t-1\) vectors we've induced so far. And if it's in the set, great, we're doing wonderfully. So our only concern is that it might not be in the set.

And if it's not in the set, then we're going to look at this picture. What's this picture of? Well, first, we take the projection of our vector average so far onto the set. That's \(c_{t-1}\). The projection is just the point in the set that is closest to our time average in Euclidean norm. And we will draw the normal vector, \(s_{t-1}\), which is just the difference between our vector average and its projection on the set. Draw that vector, call it \(s_{t-1}\); that'll be an important vector, and that will be the normal vector to a separating hyperplane.

16Projection Creates a Supporting Halfspace

So let's figure out some facts about why the normal vector that we get from this projection gives us a separating hyperplane in the first place. There's \(c_{t-1}\); that's our projection onto the convex set. The set is convex, which means we can take any two points in the set and draw the line segment between them, and it remains in the set as well. So in particular I can pick any other point \(z\) in the set \(C\) other than the projection. And let's define \(z_\alpha\) to be the point we get to by just linearly interpolating between \(z\) and the point we've projected onto there, \(c_{t-1}\). Convexity says for every value of \(\alpha\) between zero and one, \(z_\alpha\) lies in the set.

Now, we can look at this function \(f(\alpha)\), which tells us how close our time average so far is to this point \(z_\alpha\) in the set, in squared Euclidean distance. Write that out. And because \(c_{t-1}\) is a projection, it is the closest point in the set, so we know that this function has got to be minimized at \(\alpha = 0\). When \(\alpha = 0\), we just get \(c_{t-1}\). And by definition, since that's a projection, that has to be the point in the set that minimizes distance to the point we projected.

It's the minimizer of this continuous function, and so we know that if we just take the derivative at this point, or at least the right derivative, because at \(\alpha = 0\) we do have the power, while staying within the set, to increase \(\alpha\), although we don't have the power to decrease \(\alpha\), we know that the right derivative at zero had better be nonnegative. Because otherwise, by increasing \(\alpha\) by a little bit, we could get something that was even closer to \(\bar g_{t-1}\), which would violate the premise that we're starting with.

And the derivative is just this inner product where on the left we have the difference between our time average so far, the point that we are projecting, minus the projection itself, \(c_{t-1}\). This is the vector that we called the normal vector, \(s_{t-1}\). And on the right, we've got the difference between \(z\), some arbitrary point in the convex set, and \(c_{t-1}\), the projection. We know that inner product has to be nonpositive. And so that's the property that the separating hyperplane has: if we look at any point within the set, and we look at the difference between that point and the projection \(c_{t-1}\), a displacement from this projection in any direction into the set, it's got to have nonpositive inner product with this normal vector. 17Projection Replaces the Target by One Halfspace

Okay, so there's that normal vector, and then there's the half space that we drew on the picture. What is the half space? The half space is all of the points such that when you plug them into this inner product, the inner product of the normal vector \(s_{t-1}\) with \(v\) minus the projection, they have nonpositive inner product. So what we just observed on the previous slide, the projection inequality, says that everything in the target set is also in this half space. It's just like in the picture. You draw the half space and the convex set that we want to approach, and it's all on one side of it. Everything in the convex set is also in this half space. The half space is bigger. There's stuff under the half space that is outside of the convex set, but in particular it contains the convex set. And just definitionally, a vector lies in the half space exactly when plugging it into this inner product gives a nonpositive value.

So I've already foreshadowed a bit what the algorithm is going to do. But basically, at every round, it's going to do this projection. It's going to construct this half space. And then it's going to invoke the minimax theorem to assert that we always have some strategy that we can play that puts the expected value of the next vector-valued increment on the correct side of this half space, on the side of the half space containing the convex set. The reason we can use the minimax theorem to do that is because although approachability concerns vectors, the half space is defined by a scalar quantity. This inner product is a number. 19Response Satisfiability Gives a Reply to Every Q

So the first step, as I said, is to define a zero-sum game to which we can apply the minimax theorem. Let's define the scalar-valued payoff that will define our game. The strategy spaces for the learner and the adversary are the same as before. The learner plays \(x\), the adversary plays \(y\), and the payoff is then just the inner product between this round's normal vector, \(s_{t-1}\), and the difference between the vector increment that is realized when the learner and the adversary play \(x\) and \(y\) and the value of the projection at that round onto the convex set.

Now, remember that we've assumed as a premise of the theorem that the set that we're trying to approach is response satisfiable. What that means is that for every strategy that the adversary might play, if it is revealed to us, there is a strategy that the learner can play in response that lands the expected value of this vector within the convex set. And the convex set is entirely contained within the half plane. So if we land the vector within the convex set, we've also landed it within the half space.

So just putting those pieces together: for any distribution the adversary might choose, the fact that the thing is response satisfiable says that there is some strategy the learner can choose as a function of \(Q\), \(\rho_Q\), such that the expected value of the vector induced by that play lies inside the convex set. And because everything in the convex set lies inside the half space, this means that the expected value of this zero-sum game that we've defined, when the learner plays in this way, is at most zero. The inner product is less than or equal to zero when the point lies on the correct side of the half space.

And so response satisfiability tells us a bound on the maximin value of the game. The adversary goes first, and the learner gets to best respond. They can guarantee that they're on the correct side of this half space, or that the payoff defined in this particular zero-sum game is at most zero. Now we need to change the order of quantifiers. This is still a world where the adversary goes first and the learner responds. We need this statement in the other direction. But because this is now a scalar zero-sum game, we can use the minimax theorem to reverse the order of quantifiers.

21Blackwell's Approachability Algorithm

So we can now define what the algorithm is. Here's the algorithm. We're not done with the analysis; we still need to argue that this will approach the target set. But at least now we have enough to state the algorithm. This is the classical Blackwell approachability algorithm. The only state we're going to maintain is the accumulated average of these different vectors, initially zero. And at every round, we will just do a Euclidean projection of this average onto the target convex set. That's this point, \(c_{t-1}\). The normal vector, that's just the difference between our current average and the projection. We use that to define the scalar game, which just measures your [displacement] in the direction of this vector. It's negative if you're on the right side of the half space; it's positive if you're on the wrong side of the half space.

And in particular, it gives us this zero-sum game. We then solve that zero-sum game; we solve for a minimax strategy. And because we've analyzed the game through the presumption of response satisfiability, we know that the minimax strategy will guarantee that the next-round increment is on the right side of this half space every day. And that's it. Then we announce the minimax strategy, the adversary can look at it and come up with their response \(y_t\), we sample from it, which gives us the next-round increment, we update our bookkeeping, and we continue. That's the whole algorithm.

We've established the existence of a minimax strategy that gets you to the correct side of the half space at every round. What remains to show is that in doing so you must converge to the convex set. Does that make sense?
