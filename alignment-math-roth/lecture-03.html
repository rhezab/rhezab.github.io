5Rankings of Completions Do Not Rank Every Policy

Okay. And so just to reflect on the lack of information that we really get if all we have are comparisons between individual completions, the kind of objects we started with last class. Suppose I know that one of my users ranks \(a\) better than \(b\), better than \(c\). So that tells us, among the three pure lotteries, always answer with \(a\), always answer with \(b\), or always answer with \(c\), which they prefer. But it doesn't provide us enough information to be able to understand whether they would prefer that we always answer with \(b\), or that we randomize maybe between \(a\) and \(c\).

It might be that \(b\) and \(c\) are roughly equivalent to one another, and \(a\) is much, much better than either of them, in which case this lottery is better than always answering with \(b\). Or it might be that \(a\) and \(b\) are roughly equivalent to one another, both much better than \(c\), in which case the first thing is preferred. And all we have is this ranking. We just don't have the information necessary to distinguish those two cases, and it might be relevant information.

So to even be able to answer questions like this, which of these things holds, we need richer information in some way: either we should be able to ask people questions about lotteries directly, or we should be able to model people as having strengths of preferences, these ratings between 1 and 10. And kind of the point of the first half of this lecture is that, subject to a set of reasonable axioms, those two are kind of the same condition.

6Weak Preferences Allow Indifference

Okay. So we're going to imagine that people have weak preferences now, not just over individual completions, but over distributions, over lotteries. So notationally, \(p \succeq q\) means that a user weakly prefers \(p\) to \(q\). They might strictly prefer \(p\) to \(q\), but they also might be indifferent. The tilde will denote indifference. So if the user is indifferent between \(p\) and \(q\), that means that they weakly prefer \(p\) to \(q\), and also they weakly prefer \(q\) to \(p\). Both of these weak relations hold. And a user might also have strict preferences. They might strictly prefer \(p\) to \(q\). That means they weakly prefer \(p\) to \(q\), but they don't weakly prefer \(q\) to \(p\). These are just the usual weak and strong preference relations, maybe like you're used to with the real numbers.

I'm going to tell you four axioms. These are what are called the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, and they are going to characterize when a user who is able to express preferences over distributions can be modeled as just having a utility function that maps completions to numbers, and just trying to maximize their expected utility.

The first two axioms really just require that this preference relation actually be an order. What that means is, first, I'm able to ask them about any two distributions and they are able to express a preference about them. They actually do have preferences they can express for any two distributions. So if I show them two lotteries, \(p\) and \(q\), they're able to express that either they weakly prefer \(p\) to \(q\), or they weakly prefer \(q\) to \(p\), or maybe both. They could be indifferent. It's just saying that they're not allowed to say "I don't know." They're able to express preferences for any pair of lotteries.

And the second axiom is just that this is a legitimate ordering. This preference relation is transitive. So if you express that you weakly prefer \(p\) to \(q\), and then later you express that you weakly prefer \(q\) to \(r\), transitivity obligates you to prefer \(p\) to \(r\). You're not allowed individually to have one of these cycles that we've observed that populations can have. So the first two axioms are pretty benign. It's just that your preference relation behaves as a real preference relation.

7Continuity Interpolates via Randomization

And now we have two more axioms, which are kind of going to be the meat of the matter, but I think are also somewhat reasonable. The first one is continuity, which kind of says that your preferences have to smoothly, or continuously, interpolate over randomness. So I say, look, if you've expressed to me that you prefer \(p\) to \(q\) to \(r\), \(p\) is the best and \(r\) is the worst, then there should be some way for me to randomize between \(p\) and \(r\), the best thing and the worst thing. I'll look at the lottery that is \(\alpha\) probability mass on \(p\) and \(1-\alpha\) probability mass on \(r\). And there should be some such \(\alpha\) such that you are indifferent between this lottery and \(q\).

Remember, \(p\) is the best and \(r\) is the worst. So if I put all of my weight on \(p\), you weakly prefer that to \(q\). If I put all of my weight on \(r\), you weakly prefer \(q\) to \(r\). And this is just saying, as I smoothly interpolate between putting all of my weight on the best thing and putting all of my weight on the worst thing, your preference shouldn't discontinuously jump from strictly preferring the lottery to \(q\) to strictly preferring \(q\) to the lottery. There should be some intermediate point at which you're indifferent.

So we start with a thing you like at least as much as \(q\), we smoothly interpolate, by mixing between them randomly, to a thing that you like weakly less than \(q\). And the axiom here is that we should pass by a point of indifference. We shouldn't discontinuously jump from preferring \(q\) to the lottery to preferring the lottery to \(q\). Makes sense?

8Independence Makes Common Mixtures Cancel

Then there's one more axiom, which is independence. So fix any three lotteries \(p\), \(q\), and \(r\), and any mixing probability \(\alpha\) between 0 and 1. If you tell me that you weakly prefer \(p\) to \(q\), then if I say, okay, well, I'm going to give you \(p\) with probability \(\alpha\), otherwise I'll give you \(r\) with probability \(1-\alpha\), you should weakly prefer that to the equivalent lottery where I give you \(q\), also with probability \(\alpha\), and \(r\) with probability \(1-\alpha\).

But in both of these lotteries I'm giving you this third option with the same probability, \(1-\alpha\). So the only difference between the two lotteries is: in the probability-\(\alpha\) event, do I give you \(p\) or do I give you \(q\)? And this is saying, if you express separately that you prefer \(p\) to \(q\), then you should also prefer the lottery where in the \(\alpha\) event you get \(p\) instead of \(q\). You should be able to essentially cancel out the identical other branch of the lottery when figuring out this preference.

It's just saying, if you prefer \(p\) to \(q\), and I now offer you a lottery where, when the coin comes up heads, in the one world you get \(p\) and in the other world you get \(q\), and when it comes up tails the same thing happens in both worlds, you should have the preference for this lottery that's the same as the preference you have for the only part of the world that differs. Axiom makes sense?

And it's saying, algebraically, yeah, that you can cancel the left-hand side with the right-hand side, maybe the same way you're used to when dealing with the real numbers. [There's the] same component of the lottery on the left and on the right; I can cancel those and figure out [the preference from] this piece. So these are all of them. These are the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms. Does that make sense?

9VNM Characterizes Expected-Utility Maximization

Okay. And so here's the characterization, due to [von Neumann and Morgenstern]. So it says there's two ways I could maybe model someone as having preferences. There's the way we've talked about so far: they just explicitly have some preference relation that they can express over lotteries. I can show them any pair of lotteries and they say, I prefer \(p\) to \(q\), or vice versa. Or there's this maybe standard way that you've seen before, if you've taken a game theory class. It sort of corresponds to asking people to rate completions with numbers between 1 and 10. It's saying, well, look, a person has a utility function that maps every completion to a number, and the way they evaluate their utility for a distribution, for a lottery, is they just ask what is their expected utility. And that is how their preference relations are formed. They prefer things that have higher expected utility.

What the characterization says is that those are exactly the same thing when the preferences satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms. A preference relation on lotteries satisfies all four of the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms if and only if there is a utility function such that the agent prefers \(p\) to \(q\) exactly when their expected utility for \(p\), the inner product between their utility vector and the probabilities \(p\), exceeds their expected utility for \(q\).

And moreover, the utility function is essentially unique up to affine transformations. I can multiply all of your utilities by 100; that doesn't change anything, you just start valuing things in cents instead of dollars. I can add 100 to all of your utilities, if you just decide that instead of valuing things between 0 and 1, you value things between a million and a million and one. That doesn't change anything. But up to affine transformations, your preferences over lotteries in this case will also uniquely specify your utility. Does the statement make sense?

11Expected Utility =⇒ the VNM Axioms

So let's first observe the easy direction. Let's say that you do have a utility function. So there's some utility function \(u\), and you're going to say that you prefer lottery \(p\) over lottery \(q\) exactly when your expected utility for \(p\) is higher than your expected utility for \(q\). Completeness and transitivity are kind of immediate, just because the usual inequalities over the real numbers are complete and transitive. And so it's really just the other two axioms that require any verification at all.

So let's suppose that \(p\) is weakly preferred to \(q\), which is weakly preferred to \(r\). And let's just give notation for your expected utility for these three lotteries. Your expected utility for lottery \(p\) is \(U_p\), your expected utility for lottery \(q\) is \(U_q\), your expected utility for lottery \(r\) is \(U_r\). Okay. We want to verify the continuity axiom.

So if your utilities for your favorite and least favorite of these three lotteries are equal, your utility for all three of the lotteries is the same, and we can just take \(\alpha\) to be zero: your utility for \(r\) is equal to your utility for \(q\), which is what the continuity axiom requires we show. And so, in the only interesting case, your utility for \(p\) is actually strictly bigger than your utility for \(r\). And because these are just real numbers, we can write down the indifference equation: what is the value of \(\alpha\) that makes your expected utility for the mixture between \(p\) and \(r\) equal to the expected utility for \(q\)? [We] solve for what \(\alpha\) is. It's a number between 0 and 1 because of the ordering we assumed. And because you're a utility maximizer, you just care about expected utility, so for this value of \(\alpha\), because your expected utility for the lottery on the left is equal to your expected utility for \(q\), you're indifferent. And this checks off that if you really do just care about maximizing some expectation, then you satisfy the continuity axiom.

12Expected Utility Implies Independence

And similarly, if you are an expected utility maximizer, then checking independence is really just an algebraic exercise, because of the properties of the real numbers. By the linearity of expectation, your expected utility for the mixture of two lotteries, \(\alpha p + (1-\alpha) r\), minus your expected utility for the same lottery where I've swapped out \(p\) for \(q\), leaving fixed the \(r\) component for both the lottery on the left and the lottery on the right, lets us cancel the \(r\) component from the left and the right and get your expected utility just for the difference between \(p\) and \(q\), times \(\alpha\). And so if you prefer \(p\) to \(q\), the right-hand side is positive. Well, then the left-hand side is also positive. You prefer the lottery that mixes in \(p\) to the lottery that mixes in \(q\).

This is the easy direction. This is just saying, okay, look, the axioms we wrote down are all satisfied by maximizing expected utility. The interesting direction is the reverse: to show that anyone who has preference relations satisfying the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms actually can basically be modeled as a utility maximizer.

13The VNM Axioms =⇒ Expected Utility

And so now we [turn to] the more interesting direction. The starting point is that we've got some agent who can express preferences over lotteries, and we're going to assume that their preferences do satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms. And it's going to be a constructive proof. Our goal is actually to extract from these preference relations an actual utility function, and establish that their preferences are explained entirely by maximizing this expected utility.

And here's kind of the roadmap of the proof. I'm going to think about the independence axiom, and basically it will let us repeatedly perform the following essentially algebraic operation. If I have figured out that you're indifferent between some completion \(j\) and some other lottery, then in any lottery that involves completion \(j\), I can swap out any realization that gives you completion \(j\) for instead a realization that samples from this other lottery you were indifferent to. And I haven't changed your preferences for the lottery. So the first thing we're going to do is establish that that's a legitimate algebraic maneuver.

Then we're going to study these lotteries that are just mixing between two outcomes: your very favorite outcome and your very least favorite outcome. And we're going to show that, at least if you're not indifferent to everything, if you have any preferences at all, then these lotteries are strictly ordered. If I put even a little more weight on your favorite outcome, then you strictly prefer that to the lottery that put a little bit less weight on it.

And finally, we're going to read off our utility function by taking every completion, every outcome that you might care about, and we'll figure out the extent to which you care about it by lining it up with exactly which of these lotteries between your favorite outcome and your least favorite outcome establishes indifference. And together this will give us a unique utility function, again up to affine transformations, that perfectly explains all of your preferences. That's the game plan.

14Independence Preserves Strict Preference and Indifference

So first I want to just note a simple consequence of the independence lemma: although as stated it's about weak preferences, it also applies to strict preferences. Okay, so suppose you actually strictly prefer \(p\) to \(q\). That means you weakly prefer \(p\) to \(q\), and you don't weakly prefer \(q\) to \(p\). As stated, the independence axiom just says, okay, well, if you're comparing equivalent lotteries that mix some fraction of \(r\) with an equal fraction of either \(p\) or \(q\), you weakly prefer the one that mixes with \(p\). In fact, a consequence of this independence axiom is that you strictly prefer the lottery on the left that mixes with \(p\) instead of \(q\).

And the reason is, if this was not the case, if this relation wasn't strict, if I reverse the left-hand side and the right-hand side and you also weakly prefer the right-hand side to the left-hand side, then I could cancel out the component of the lottery that depended on \(r\). And I would find that you weakly prefer \(q\) to \(p\), which is a contradiction, because we assumed you strongly prefer \(p\) to \(q\). Okay, so there's nothing much going on here. It's just observing that the independence axiom actually gives us something useful which is slightly stronger than what was stated: mixing with some identical third option actually preserves strict preferences, not just weak preferences. First observation.

And so now, suppose I've identified two lotteries, \(s\) and \(s'\), that you are indifferent between. It means you weakly prefer \(s\) to \(s'\), and also you weakly prefer \(s'\) to \(s\). Then I can apply the independence axiom [to each comparison], and I'll find on the one hand that you weakly prefer mixing between \(s\) and \(r\) to mixing between \(s'\) and \(r\), and also vice versa. So similarly, if you're indifferent between two base options, then mixing with some third option preserves that indifference as well. Okay, so I can always mix with some third option, and that will preserve strict preferences and will also preserve indifference.

38Summary

So to conclude, now that we've finished [our run] of just classical social choice, what have we learned? Number one, if we are collecting only ordinal preferences over completions, like \(a\) is better than \(b\) or vice versa, we're kind of limited. We're not able to figure out what people's relative preferences are, what the magnitudes of their preferences are for different completions. That's kind of what's driving Arrow's impossibility theorem. It's difficult for us to pin down what a reasonable aggregation rule is.

We'd have a lot more information about people's preferences if we could think about them as maximizing some utility function in expectation. That would give us not just ordering information, but information about the relative strength of their preferences. It turns out that if their preferences satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, we are justified in modeling people as having utility functions. In fact, it's an if-and-only-if relationship. So if you want to model people as having utility functions, you'd better be committing to them satisfying the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms.

And if we have utility functions, then it's a lot clearer; we have a lot more guidance as to what a reasonable social goal is. The Pareto axiom seems like a very minimal thing: you shouldn't reverse unanimous comparisons. And if you buy into that, Harsanyi's theorem has told us essentially what our goal must be, which is to maximize sums of agent utilities. We have choices, in that we can weight them differently, but we can't deviate beyond that. If we're not maximizing some non-negative weighted sum of user utilities, then we're going to violate the Pareto axiom.

And so this is going to set us up for the next few lectures to think about what the standard RLHF pipeline actually optimizes for.
