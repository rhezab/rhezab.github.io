1Can Competition Produce Alignment?

All right. So last class we studied this Bayesian persuasion model where there's one informed party, maybe we're thinking of it as the LLM provider, who had preferences over the actions taken by some downstream decision maker, maybe the user. Maybe they care how the user votes or what they buy, but they don't have direct control over what they do. They try to convince them to take actions, but they can't compel actions. So you can think of this as maybe some sensible model of what a present-day LLM can do. It can inform you, and it can try to influence your behavior, perhaps, by making judgment calls about what to inform you about and what not to inform you about, but it can't take control of your body and force you to vote for it.

And what we saw is that even without this ability to compel action, just through being able to try to convince by selectively sharing information, the informed party could nevertheless, by strategically revealing information, substantially improve their own payoff compared to what would happen if they shared all of their information, and substantially harm the receiver's payoff compared to if the sender had shared all of their information. And so that's maybe a little bit of a bummer if you're the receiver, if you're the user of the LLM.

And what I want to ask in this class is: well, in that model from last class, there was only one sender. The receiver kind of had no choice, or their choice was limited to either using the information provided by that one sender, or else going off on their own and receiving no information at all. So the baseline for them was how well they could do without talking to anybody. But real LLM markets are somewhat different than that, in that there are multiple providers. There's not just one monolithic provider. And what I want to ask this class is whether that substantially changes the story. Even if none of the LLM providers are aligned with the user, might the fact that the user has choice amongst them, and therefore induces competition between them, somehow save the day? Can we recover the outcomes that the receiver would have gotten to enjoy had the sender been fully aligned, without requiring that the sender be fully aligned, just through the presence of market competition among different misaligned senders?

2Overview

What we're going to see is a model that's very similar to what we saw last time. We've got a user who is the one who is empowered to take actions. The user has actions they can take, and some unknown payoff-relevant state, and a utility. LLM providers also have utility functions of the same form. They care about the actions the user takes in different states. For example, they might want to manipulate who she votes for or what she buys, if they're taking advertising money. And they're misaligned, meaning the providers have utility functions that are different. They want her to do different things in different states compared to what she would choose to do on her own if she were fully informed.

But the key thing is that the user can decide which of the LLMs to interact with. And an LLM provider of course has no ability to influence a user's behavior unless the user is actually interacting with it. That is going to induce a game: what are the LLMs that the providers actually deploy, given that the user chooses to interact with the one that provides her with the highest utility, the one that is best for her own goals? So when there's multiple providers and the user chooses amongst them, there is this game induced among the providers, and our hope is that there is a condition that will certify that in the equilibrium of this competitive market game the user will do as well as if she had a fully aligned provider on her side, under a substantially weaker condition. I guess we'll characterize it, if and only if.

3Choosing the Best Offer Is Not Enough

So what we're going to talk about today is some generalization of what you would call a multi-sender Bayesian persuasion game. This is the important modeling assumption, the same modeling assumption as last class in the single-sender game: the model providers commit to their models. They act first, and then, having committed to their models, the user gets to decide which one to use. And of course it's not literally the case that once a model provider deploys 5.6 or something they can never update it to GPT-6. But the assumption is intended to model the spirit that the cadence at which we can provide major model updates is slower than the cadence at which users can decide which models to use, can change their behavior. And so you can maybe think about what happens as a repeated commitment game, where every once in a while the model providers update their models, but then the users rearrange to use the best one.

And one thing that was maybe a little bit unrealistic about the Bayesian persuasion model last class was that, in the traditional economic style, we assumed that the receiver, the user, was an optimal Bayesian. They would condition on all information available to them. They would choose the utility-maximizing action. And we're going to generalize that a bit. We're going to say, okay, the user may be a Bayesian, but more generally they just have some behavioral rule. Maybe they're stupider than a Bayesian. We're just going to use that behavioral rule to define what the optimal outcome for them means. If they're not a Bayesian, maybe even a perfectly aligned provider who's trying to help them out couldn't help them out as much as you'd like. But whatever is the best that someone who was trying to help them out could do for them, that's what we're going to define to be the optimal outcome, for the aligned provider. And what we're going to try to prove is that under some weaker condition, under market alignment, they will do that well.

And so there's no presumption here that the user is doing anything hard. The user can behave in some arbitrary behavioral way, and we are going to prove that they will do well in equilibrium, where "well" means as good as they could have possibly done given the possibly stupid way in which they behave. And if you want to pretend they're Bayesians, that's fine too.

Did the setup make sense? What's new compared to last time is the existence of a second provider. And both providers are misaligned with the user, but the misalignment is different. 12Equilibrium Means No Profitable Unilateral Change

And so what I want to do is think about what the equilibria of this game are. I want to come up with a condition, ultimately, that says under some condition the equilibria of this game will be good in some sense for the user. Let's think about, first of all, what do we even mean by equilibrium? A Nash equilibrium is a collection of deployed LLMs that is robust to unilateral deviation. There is no provider that could have made their own utility higher by unilaterally changing what model they deploy. They can't affect what other players deploy. We're just asking whether there are local improvements that could have been obtained by any of the providers.

So let's consider some provider \(i\). From provider \(i\)'s perspective, there's what the other providers are doing. They have no control over that. So let's call that \(c_{-i}\): the set of LLMs deployed by the providers other than player \(i\). In considering what deviations might be beneficial, the only way that player \(i\) has of affecting the profile of deployed conversation rules is through their own choice of which conversation rule to deploy. In profile \(c\) they deployed \(c_i\), but they consider whether they could have done better by deploying something else instead, \(c_i'\).

So a Nash equilibrium in this game is just a collection of deployed conversation rules, a vector of them, call it \(c^*\), such that simultaneously for every provider \(i\) and every alternative conversation rule \(c_i'\) that they could have counterfactually chosen to deploy, none of them would have been better off by making that unilateral deviation. For all of them, their own utility for the deployed set of conversation rules is at least as high as what their counterfactual utility would have been had they deployed a different rule and everyone else remained fixed. So a Nash equilibrium is a stable state under competition, because nobody can unilaterally change what they're doing to get higher utility.

Now, think about what our benchmark is. Our goal is to prove a theorem that says, okay, in every Nash equilibrium of this game, under some condition, the user does well. What does "well" mean? In our toy example, where the providers knew everything and the full revelation mechanism was available, the user can't do any better than full revelation. That would be great. But trying to say, okay, the user does as well as under full revelation, would require two assumptions that I don't want to make. One is that full revelation is even feasible. That would require that the providers know what the underlying state is, and I don't want to assume that the providers have complete knowledge about what the underlying state is. And the other is that the user knows what to do with that information. Even under full revelation, if the user is not a Bayesian, conditioning perfectly on that information and best responding, they're not going to obtain their optimal Bayesian utility. So I don't want to assume that they're a perfect Bayesian.

Okay, so I want to define what optimal is relative to the strategies that are available to the providers, which might not include full revelation, and also relative to the rationality of the user. If they don't know how to make good decisions, I don't want the benchmark to hold them to that standard. So fix what the user knows how to do: fix some behavioral policy for the user. And fix the set of conversation rules that are available to the providers. They might not have perfect information about the underlying state, so they might not have, for example, the full revelation rule. A perfectly aligned provider would choose which conversation rule to deploy so as to maximize the user's utility once she responds. So let's say that \(c^{\text{opt}}\), the optimal conversation rule, is just the conversation rule amongst the feasible ones that maximizes the user's utility under whatever her behavioral policy is. And let's say that \(V_0^*\) is just the utility the user enjoys under that optimal conversation rule.

14Market Alignment Combines Providers’ Objectives

Okay. So in our little example, the user's utility was the sum of the providers' utilities. And if that's true of the utility functions, a weaker implication of that is that when we looked at the user's expected utility interacting with any conversation rule, it was the sum of the providers' expected utilities when the user interacts with it. The more general condition, which I will call market alignment, is that the user's expected utility when interacting with any conversation rule should be some non-negative weighted combination of the providers' expected utilities when the user interacts with the same conversation rule, plus a potentially arbitrary constant offset: $\(V_0(c) = c_0 + \sum_{i=1}^k w_i V_i(c), \quad w_i \ge 0.\)$ So the condition is that the user's expected utility interacting with a conversation rule should lie in the cone, if you like, or the non-negative affine span, of the providers' utilities. If the user's utility is the sum of the providers' utilities, as in our toy example, that is an instantiation of it. But we don't need it to be an unweighted sum. It can be a weighted sum for arbitrary non-negative weights, and also constant offsets don't matter. And that will be our condition, which we call market alignment. Does that make sense?

Roth: It is, but it is a strictly weaker condition than that any provider in the market is aligned with the user. If we have a market that consists of a billion providers and one of them has the same utility function as the user, of course it also satisfies this condition, because we can set the weight to one for the provider that has the same utility function as the user. But more generally, this market alignment condition can be satisfied even when none of the providers are aligned with the user. The user may be in their non-negative span. Our little toy example is an example of that, where neither of the providers is aligned with the user. One was too aggressive, the other was too conservative, but nevertheless the user is in their non-negative span.

15Competition Delivers the Best Feasible User Value

If the market alignment condition is satisfied for every feasible conversation rule, so you can write the user's utility as a non-negative linear combination of the providers' utilities, then every pure Nash equilibrium of the game (we're not just talking about the best one, we're talking about all of them), at every equilibrium of the game, the user's utility will be the optimal user utility: the utility she could have obtained in a world in which there was a perfectly aligned provider.

So it's saying, under this non-trivial but weaker condition, the user does as well as she would have had one of the providers satisfied the stronger condition, had they been fully aligned, under every single one of the equilibria. 16A Better Rule Benefits Some Provider

So let's suppose we've got some profile of conversation rules, and the user selects one of them, her favorite one. And let's say that that one doesn't give the user her optimal utility. What we want to show is that this profile can't be a Nash equilibrium. The starting assumption, that it doesn't give her her optimal utility, is that if I look at the difference between her expected utility for the user-optimal rule and her utility for the one she selects in this deployment, this difference is greater than zero: \(V_0(c^{\text{opt}}) - V_0(c) > 0\). She's not doing as well as the user optimum.

Now, remember what market alignment means. It means that I can rewrite the user's expected utility at any conversation rule as some non-negative linear combination of the providers' expected utilities at that conversation rule, plus some offset. I'm just going to rewrite both of these terms in that way. Whatever the offset is, it's going to cancel out, because I'm subtracting one from the other. What I get is the user's expected utility at the optimal rule, which is the corresponding non-negative linear combination of the providers' expected utilities at the user-optimal rule, minus the user's expected utility at her chosen rule, which is the corresponding non-negative linear combination of the providers' expected utilities at her chosen rule: $\(V_0(c^{\text{opt}}) - V_0(c) = \sum_i w_i \left( V_i(c^{\text{opt}}) - V_i(c) \right).\)$

Now, the premise is that the left-hand side is positive, so by equality the right-hand side is also positive. And the right-hand side is a non-negative weighted combination. So if I have a non-negative weighted combination of terms that is positive in sum, then at least one of them has to be positive. Pick a positive one. The only way it can be positive is if \(w_i\) is positive, so I can divide both sides by \(w_i\). And it means that it is not just the user who prefers the user-optimal rule to whichever is being selected in deployment, but provider \(i\) does as well. Provider \(i\) gets higher utility under the user-optimal rule than under the rule that is being selected in deployment by the user: \(V_i(c^{\text{opt}}) > V_i(c)\). So that provider would have benefited had the user-optimal rule been available to the user.

17Offering the Better Rule Wins Selection

And they have the ability to cause that rule to be available to the user. Remember, the user, amongst all of the deployed rules, picks the one that is best for her. So the user's utility for the rule she picks is at least as big as her utility for the other rules that were deployed. She picked the best one. And by presumption, it wasn't as big as her utility would have been had she had available to her the user-optimal rule. Now, the user-optimal rule is, by definition, in the strategy space of all of the providers. So provider \(i\), the one who would also prefer the user to be using the user-optimal rule, could deviate to providing it and be guaranteed that after that deviation the user would select it, because the user selects the rule with the highest utility, and the utility she would get from this rule is strictly higher than the utility she gets from any other rule. And so, under that deviation, the new distribution on outcomes would be the one induced by the user interacting with the user-optimal rule, which is strictly preferred by provider \(i\). Hence, this is a profitable deviation for provider \(i\), which means that the initial profile of deployed rules can't have been a Nash equilibrium, because one of the providers has a profitable deviation.

So since the only premise we started with was that the vector of conversation rules was not user-optimal, and we concluded that this was not a Nash equilibrium, and this game does have Nash equilibria, it must be that at every Nash equilibrium, the user obtains her optimal utility.

20Shared Conflicts Can Defeat Competition

Okay. Let me now quickly tell you about a converse theorem. So we gave this condition, and we said under this market alignment condition, which is weaker, and under market competition as modeled by the Nash equilibria of this commitment game, good things happen for the user. The user gets optimal utility. To what extent might we be able to come up with an even weaker condition and get the same theorem? There is some worst-case sense, that I will tell you about now, in which this market alignment condition is the right condition. You cannot weaken it.

Now, worst case over what? I'm going to have to modify the setup a little bit so that I can say worst case over decision problems. In the model that I told you about so far, the utilities were directly defined in terms of pairs of actions and states of the world. Suppose now that the actions and the state of the world are just the intermediate, observable state, and that the thing we really care about is some latent set of terminal consequences.

Okay, so maybe I'm trying to prescribe drugs to patients. Maybe the thing that I can hope to observe is the action I take, what drug I prescribe them, and what the underlying disease is. But ultimately the thing I care about is: do they die or do they recover? Which isn't completely determined by what I do, even if I know their conditions. There's still some underlying randomness. So let's say that the utility functions now depend not directly on actions and states of the world, but on some set of terminal consequences that we might not have direct control over. Did the patient die or not? And to the extent that we have control over these terminal consequences, there's still actions and states of the world, but every action and state of the world induces some distribution on the terminal consequences. Maybe if I do a good job prescribing the right medication matched to the patient's underlying condition, this will induce a better outcome distribution over these terminal consequences: less likely to die. But at best, I have some probabilistic control. So now the utility functions are defined in terms of these terminal consequences, and a decision problem is a mapping from actions and states of the world to lotteries over these terminal consequences.

And here's the worst-case converse. Suppose the utilities, now defined on these consequences, do not satisfy the market alignment condition. That is, it's not possible to write the user's utility function in the non-negative span of the providers' utility functions. Then there exists a decision problem, meaning there exists some mapping from actions and states of the world to lotteries over terminal consequences, such that the theorem we just proved under the assumption of market alignment does not hold. There is a bad Nash equilibrium of this game. So if market alignment does not hold, there is a decision problem such that in this deployment game there is a Nash equilibrium in which the user does not get her optimal utility.

Form of the theorem makes sense? There's a little bit of indirection here, in that I've defined this additional layer in which actions and states of the world map to distributions over terminal consequences. Call that mapping an instantiation of the decision problem, and the converse theorem is in the worst case over decision problems. It's saying: if market alignment doesn't hold, then there is such a decision problem with a bad equilibrium. So it's not an instance-by-instance converse, but rather a worst-case converse. But does the statement of the theorem make sense?

21Harsanyi Finds a Conflict over Two Lotteries

And this will be a relatively direct consequence of Harsanyi's theorem, which you might remember from lecture three. Remember that Harsanyi's theorem was about aggregation under a Pareto condition. There's a bunch of agents; in this case, identify them with the providers. And there's an aggregation rule that satisfies the Pareto condition. In this case, that condition means: for all pairs of lotteries, whenever all of the providers prefer lottery A to lottery B, the aggregate does as well. And the aggregate here we're going to identify with the user's utility function. Then it must be that we could have written the aggregate utility as a non-negative linear combination of the agent utilities, in this case the provider utilities, very similar to market alignment. That's Harsanyi's aggregation theorem instantiated in our setting.

And what we're going to use is the contrapositive of this. If there is no such representation, if we cannot write the user's utility as a non-negative linear combination of the providers' utilities, then there have to be a pair of lotteries, call them the good lottery and the bad lottery, such that the user strictly prefers the good lottery to the bad lottery, but the providers don't. The providers regard the bad lottery as at least as good as the good lottery, or better. That's just Harsanyi's theorem projected into our setting here, and then we're taking the contrapositive.

Once we have the existence of this pair of a good and a bad lottery, we're essentially done. So for each of the parties, both the user and the providers, let's write \(g_p\) to be that party's expected utility for an outcome drawn from the good lottery, and \(b_p\) to be that party's expected utility for an outcome drawn from the bad lottery. What we get from the contrapositive of Harsanyi's theorem is the existence of a good and a bad lottery such that the user strictly prefers the good lottery to the bad lottery, \(g_0 > b_0\), and yet every provider weakly prefers the bad lottery to the good lottery, \(g_i \le b_i\).

26Summary

So just to sum up: we learned about Bayesian persuasion, in which there was a single sender. We learned that a misaligned sender, only through information design, only through trying to persuade the receiver by withholding certain information, could increase her utility at the expense of the receiver's utility. That's the cost of misalignment. And here we asked what happens if the picture changes so that there are multiple providers and the user has the ability to choose which one to interact with. Then we don't need perfect alignment to get the same utility the user would get under perfect alignment. The force of market competition for the user's attention, which we see in the form of outcomes supported in Nash equilibria of this game, is sufficient to get the same outcomes we would get under full alignment, under a substantially weaker condition: this market alignment condition. And at least in the worst case over decision problems, that condition is also necessary. Thank you.
