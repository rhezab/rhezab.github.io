1Hallucination, Calibration, and Missing Mass

So, the last few lectures we've been studying things related in various ways to calibration. For example, last lecture we talked about calibration. We covered something like Aumann's agreement theorem. And the mechanism through which we used calibration to argue for fast agreement was we noticed that if there are calibration errors, then by fixing them, we reduce the error of our model. We reduce squared error, and that can't happen very many times.

Same reason: when you train a model to match some distribution, it will naturally tend towards calibration, in the sense that if there are any defects of calibration that are fixable within the parameterization of the model, it will fix them, because that will reduce error. That's the thing you're trying to get it to do. And today we're going to think about what that can tell us about why we might expect models that you haven't specifically intervened on to correct this issue to hallucinate: to confidently state incorrect answers rather than saying, for example, "I don't know."

2Overview

So we're going to study a really simple model. But we're going to try to understand why, when we train a model to fit a distribution, and in our stylized setting the training data will consist of facts (we'll see what that means)... One simple reason why you could blame your model for saying wrong things is you might say, well, there are instances in the training data where the training data says the wrong things. It's just correctly learning the distribution. We're going to avoid that explanation by imagining that the training data actually contains only true facts. There's nothing wrong in the training data. We're going to see why, nevertheless, we might end up with a model that makes false claims.

And first, from a finite training set, there are things that a model will have no way of knowing. So if we required the model to give what could be a factual answer for every question, then the fact that it had to be wrong sometimes would just mean that, of course, it has to sometimes say wrong things. But we're going to allow the model to say, "I don't know." And so it is not necessary that the model hallucinates, that it ever say something wrong. Its training data consists of only correct things, but it has the capability to express that it doesn't know something. That doesn't count as a hallucination.

But we're going to see a simple mechanism, basically coming from the fact that pre-training will push us to find models that are calibrated, and the training data consists of just collections of facts. It doesn't consist of examples in which the answer is given as "I don't know." So training will essentially penalize saying "I don't know." And we'll have some story of where hallucinations come from, and maybe some pointers for what to do to remove them. Okay.

4Unseen Facts Cannot Be Inferred from Other Facts

So here's going to be our little toy model capturing this, for collections of arbitrary facts, none of which can be logically inferred. Someone has to tell you what the answer is for you to know it. So let's say we've got some collection of prompts, and we've got a distribution over them. For example, "What is the birthday of this person?" It'd be like the uniform distribution over all eight and a half billion people in the world.

And any particular prompt could have \(K+1\) answers. \(K+1\), because one of them is the right answer and \(K\) of them are the wrong answers. So \(K\) here is the number of wrong answers. The total number of answers is \(K+1\) because one of them is going to be right. And for any particular prompt, one of them is right and the other \(K\) are wrong.

And we're going to think about a difficult underlying distribution for answering these questions, in which there's no way to predict the answers better than random guessing unless you've seen it. The way the distribution is generated is: at the beginning of time, I draw a uniformly random answer that will be the correct answer for each of the prompts in the world. So if I haven't seen the answer to a prompt before, my correct posterior belief over what its answer is is uniform over these \(K+1\) possible answers.

And so in this model, because the answers are all selected independently from one another, there's no correlation. There's nothing that I can learn about the answers to questions I haven't seen from the answers to questions that I have seen. That's different than in some settings. That's different than in math, for example. But these are the kinds of arbitrary facts that we're going to think about here.

5Missing Mass Measures How Often the Sample Has No Answer

And I'm going to imagine that the training sample that I'm going to pre-train my model on will just consist of \(n\) i.i.d. samples from this distribution. So \(n\) i.i.d. prompts together with the correct answers for those prompts. So I've seen only correct answers. None of my training points are wrong, but potentially I haven't seen the answers to all of the questions. So let's say that \(O_n\) is just the collection of prompts that were in the training set. Those are the points that I've seen.

And a quantity that will play a central role in our analysis is what I'll call the missing mass: the fraction of the possible questions that I haven't seen the answers to. Okay, so this is just the probability, if I draw a new prompt from the underlying distribution, that it is a question whose answer did not appear in my training data. Let's call that the missing mass, \(M_n\). That's the part of the distribution I know nothing about.

6Forced Answers Fail on the Missing Mass

So, first, I just want to make a trivial observation, which is that by construction, I set the distribution up so that the answers to the questions that I've seen in training have no correlation at all to the answers to the questions that I haven't seen. And the distribution on answers that I haven't seen is uniform over \(K+1\) answers. It's got to be that no matter what predictive algorithm I come up with, when I'm presented with a question whose answer I've never seen before, the chance that I get it right is only \(1/(K+1)\). One of the answers is right out of the \(K+1\) options.

And so suppose my algorithm must answer every question: every time you ask what someone's birthday is, I need to output a month and a day. Then the probability on a randomly drawn question that I get the wrong answer is at least the probability that I get a wrong answer on data points that I haven't seen before, times the probability that I get a question that I haven't seen before. That's the missing mass.

I guess this is useful to understand, but this theorem is almost a tautology. It's saying: no matter what I do, I don't care what the training algorithm is, if the training algorithm must output a model that attempts to answer every question, then the probability that I answer a randomly drawn question wrong is at least \(1 - 1/(K+1)\), which is just the probability of getting an answer wrong if I'm guessing at random, times the probability that I haven't seen the question. There's 364 wrong answers. There's one right answer. And so it's \(364/365\) times the fraction of people in the world whose birthdays I don't know.

7An Unseen Answer Is Still Uniform

Okay, so this is almost immediate, but just to say it: for any prompt that I haven't seen before, over the randomness of the prior distribution, for any answer that I might produce, the probability that that answer is correct is \(1/(K+1)\), because the true answer was selected uniformly at random from \(K+1\) possible answers. And it has no correlation with what I'm predicting, because my predictions are a function only of the training sample. There's no correlation between this answer and my training sample. So the conditional error is \(1 - 1/(K+1)\), conditional on seeing a point I've never seen before. And this is true for every prompt I haven't seen before, and the probability that I get a prompt I haven't seen before is equal to the missing mass. So that's it.

So far, there's nothing particularly interesting that's been said. It's just saying, okay, it's hard to predict the answers to independent facts that you haven't seen. But of course, if someone asks you a question that you don't know the answer to, you don't need to guess. You're allowed to say, "I don't know the answer." So why don't we just now allow models to say "I don't know."

So when someone asks me a question, like "What is this person's birthday?", I now have two choices about how to answer. I can answer with one of the syntactically correct answers, like a month and a day, or I'm allowed to say "I don't know." It doesn't count as a hallucination if I say "I don't know." So it is now possible, of course, to design a system that never gives an incorrect answer to one of these factual questions. I could just write my training sample in a lookup table. 11Calibration Matches Groups, Not Each Fact

So, good. So let's talk about the probability that our generator and the source distribution put on each question-answer pair. So the questions are always drawn from the same fixed question distribution. We're not generating the questions. So both the real distribution and the synthetic distribution have a term that is first just \(\mu(x)\): what's the probability that the question \(x\) is the one that's asked? We differ in the probabilities of the answers that are produced. There's some true distribution on real answers conditional on \(x\). That's \(D(r \mid x)\). It's just 1 for the right answer and 0 for the wrong answer. And our generator has some other distribution, \(G(r \mid x)\), that could put different mass on different answers. These are distributions on question-answer pairs: one the real one, one the synthetic one.

And so for any particular prediction value, for example, I could have a generative model that says that \(r\) is the right answer with probability 70%, I can look, as in calibration, at the level sets of that prediction value. For example, I can look at all of the question-answer pairs that my model puts 70% conditional probability on: all of the question-answer pairs such that my model thinks that \(r\) is the answer to this question with probability 70%. Okay, this partitions the set of question-answer pairs according to the confidence of my model in that answer.

And calibration is just a moment matching condition, saying that for all of these level sets, the level set should have the same probability under the generated distribution, the synthetic distribution, as it does under the real distribution. So semantic calibration here just means that each of these level sets has the same probability under the real distribution and the generated, synthetic distribution. And we'll see towards the end of the lecture that loss minimization, if I'm minimizing log likelihood of my model, is going to get semantic calibration like this. Because if I didn't have it, I could reduce my loss by correcting estimates to be calibrated.

12Uniform Guessing Can Be Semantically Calibrated

So if I look at the model that just guesses birthdays that it doesn't know, and think about its calibration error, well, let's look at the level sets of that model. There's only a few different levels. For things that it knows the answer to, it predicts probability 1. The mass of things that it predicts probability 1 on, under both the real distribution and the generated distribution, is just the set of questions it has seen before, which is just \(1 - M_n\), one minus the missing mass. Okay, so for the level set on which my predictor predicts probability 1, the mass under the real distribution and the synthetic distribution are the same. Perfectly calibrated there.

My model predicts \(1/365\) for all question-answer pairs if it hasn't seen the question before. There's 365 total answers, and it puts the uniform distribution on them. So if I look at the level set of my model corresponding to \(1/365\), the probability under the generated distribution of that level set is just the missing mass. That's also the probability of that level set under the true underlying distribution. Perfectly calibrated there.

And on anything else, including the answer "I don't know," I just predict probability zero. The guessing thing never says "I don't know." 13Abstention Avoids Errors but Breaks Calibration

On the other hand, if I look at the model that doesn't hallucinate, the one that says "I don't know," we'll see that it could actually have quite high calibration error. It puts probability 1 on correct answers that it has seen before. It puts probability 1 on the answer "I don't know" for things that it hasn't seen before. 14A Weaker Calibration Requirement

Okay, good. So now we want to connect... We've seen a particular example in which there were two ways of potentially answering queries. They both get the same set of queries right 100% of the time, the set of queries corresponding to answers they've seen in their training data. One of them handles unseen queries in a poor way. It guesses the answer and therefore has a high hallucination rate. The other one handles it in a good way. It says, correctly, "I don't know." That one has a zero hallucination rate, but it had the high calibration error. We want to turn this example into some more systematic understanding of how calibration and these hallucination rates must relate to one another.

So for this, we'll actually only need a substantially coarser measure of calibration. So rather than looking at all of the different level sets of the model's confidence, let's coarsen them into just two sets: question-answer pairs for which we'll say the model has high confidence, and its complement, question-answer pairs for which we'll say the model has low confidence. Let's say that the high confidence set \(H\) is the set of all question-answer pairs such that our model gives the answer probability larger than \(1/K\). \(K\), remember, is the number of wrong answers. And for everything else, the model has low confidence. So this is partitioning the set of all question-answer pairs into just two sets according to whether the model gives confidence greater than \(1/K\) for that answer given the question, or lower than \(1/K\).

These sets are, of course, just the disjoint union of the more fine-grained level sets we were talking about before. So if we were perfectly calibrated on each of the disjoint level sets, we're also perfectly calibrated on […]. If the source matched the generator distribution on every level set \(B_p\), then it has to also match on the high confidence set.

And since we want to give a more quantitative understanding now, we'll also give a quantitative measure of miscalibration. In this case, the calibration discrepancy that we'll talk about between the source distribution and the generator is just the magnitude of the difference between the mass that they place on this high confidence set, \(\delta_H(G, D) = |G(H) - D(H)|\). If that discrepancy is big, there's large calibration error. In the guessing versus abstaining example, the guesser, because it had zero calibration error even on the level sets, also has zero calibration error in this metric, and similarly the abstainer has maximal calibration error, equal to the missing mass.

15Missing Mass Forces Hallucination or Miscalibration

So the first theorem we'll prove says there's no way around this. Of course, if I've seen the entire support of the training distribution, then there's no need for me to ever hallucinate or be miscalibrated. I just always give the right answer because I've seen it before. What this theorem says is that if you haven't seen the whole support of the training distribution, if in particular there's non-trivial missing mass, then you either need substantial calibration error or you need substantial hallucination rates. In particular, no matter what your model looks like, if I look at the sum of the hallucination rate and the magnitude of the miscalibration, that's at least the missing mass minus \(2/K\), \(K\) being, remember, the number of wrong answers for each question.

So it's not just this one example we saw; these things are in some kind of fundamental tension with one another. So in particular, if whatever your training is doing is resulting in low calibration error, as simple maximum likelihood estimation, moment matching kinds of techniques would do, any kind of log likelihood minimization, if it's forcing the calibration error to be small, it will inevitably result in large hallucination rate, unless you have near complete coverage of the support of the training distribution.

16A Validity Test Connects Generation to Missing Information

Okay. So, to prove this, we're going to conduct a thought experiment in which we're going to think about a made-up classification problem. We're actually solving some generative modeling problem. We're trying to match a distribution; there is no classification problem. But I just want to imagine this classification problem to help us think about how to prove the theorem.

Here's the thought experiment. It's a classification distribution. This is how I generate the data in the classification problem. I draw a random prompt. And then I flip a coin. And if the coin comes up heads, I will pair the random prompt with the real answer to that prompt, and that will be a positive example. Okay, so the features are always question-answer pairs. And I always draw a random question. And if the coin comes up heads, it's going to be a positive example, and the answer paired with it will be the right answer. On the other hand, if the coin comes up tails, the answer that I will pair with it will be a wrong answer, drawn uniformly at random from the \(K\) possible wrong answers.

So this is a binary classification problem. Half of the examples are positive, half of the examples are negative. They're drawn from this distribution. Okay? Binary classification. Again, this isn't actually happening, we're not actually training models, but we're going to think about how, given a generative distribution, we could use that to try to solve this classification problem. And we will use lower bounds on the ability of any method to solve this classification problem to reach conclusions about what the generative distribution must look like.

17On an Unseen Prompt, Both Labels Look the Same

So first of all, say we've trained a classifier to try to solve this binary classification problem. We've got some training data. Fix the training sample. And now suppose at test time we are presented with an unseen prompt. Because of the way we generated the data, for any answer that might be paired with this prompt, we know nothing about what the correct answer was, because the correct answers aren't correlated across examples and this prompt wasn't in our training data. So over the prior distribution, my posterior belief about what the correct answer is is still uniform over all possible answers. There's probability \(1/(K+1)\) that any of the possible answers is correct. So even if you told me this was going to be a positive example, my posterior belief would be uniform, \(1/(K+1)\).

Now, suppose you told me it was a negative example. What would be my belief about the answer that it would be paired with? Well, what I know is that it's going to be paired with a uniformly random incorrect answer. So I could sum up over all of the incorrect answers. Each of them would have probability \(1/K\). But I don't know which is the correct answer. Each correct answer has probability only \(1/(K+1)\). I'm summing up over the \(K\) incorrect answers when I'm computing this probability; the \(K\) cancels with the \(1/K\). And again, my posterior belief over what answer should be paired with the question, given that I know that this is a negative example, is exactly the same as given that I know that it's a positive example. It's \(1/(K+1)\).

If you told me what the label was for this example, that would give me no information at all about what the distribution on completions was, correct completions. And as a result, no classifier that I could possibly train from the training data could hope to have classification error better than one half on any example corresponding to a question that did not appear in its training data. No information about it.

18Use Generation Probabilities to Predict Validity

So now what I want to do is give a way, given a generative distribution for answers that you've trained, to turn that into a classifier, and then use the fact that I know that on unseen examples all classifiers are bad, to conclude that in particular this classifier must be bad, and therefore learn something about what the generative distribution must look like.

So here's one way, given that I've trained this generative distribution, that I could try to use it to solve this binary classification problem that we've imagined for ourselves. Given a prompt and a proposed answer, I could predict that it's a positive example, a valid example, if my generator puts weight larger than \(1/K\) on the answer. Otherwise, I'd predict invalid. I'd predict that it's a negative example. So this classifier will predict a positive label for exactly those question-answer pairs that are in this high confidence set that we defined before: those question-answer pairs for which the generator gives probability greater than \(1/K\). And it will predict negative labels for exactly the question-answer pairs that are in the low confidence set \(L\).

Now this is just some classifier. We know that all classifiers for this problem must have error one half on any example they haven't seen before. Exactly one half. In particular, we know that the classification error of this rule will be one half on all of the examples that it hasn't seen before. How many of those are there? A proportion exactly equal to the missing mass. And so the error of this classifier, just as the error for all classifiers, must be at least half the missing mass.

Okay. So remember, what we're trying to show is that the sum of the hallucination rate for our generator plus the calibration error of our generator is at least the missing mass minus \(2/K\). So since the error rate of this classifier we've constructed is at least half the missing mass, we've now reduced what we need to show to showing that the hallucination rate of our generator plus its calibration error is at least twice the classification error of this constructed solution to our imagined binary classification problem, minus \(2/K\). Okay? That's the new thing we need to show.

27But How Large Is the Missing Mass?

There's just one more thing I want to think about, which is how I can even think about the missing mass. But maybe just to pause for a moment and reflect on what it is we're saying here. Which one are you going to get? Well, what we just saw is that if you train your model to minimize negative log likelihood, then you're not going to have any calibration error. The missing mass is by definition the fraction of the distribution you haven't seen yet. And so you don't have a direct way to think about that, right? How do you know what you don't know?

And so the final thing that I want to show you is a very simple estimator that will give you a very good approximation for what the missing mass is, given the data you actually have from the training set. The idea is there's a fine line between never having seen something before and having seen it only once. You can't tell what you've never seen before, but you can count what you've seen only once, and that'll give you a hint about what you've never seen before.

So for every prompt, let's just count how many times we've seen it in our training data. There'll be some prompts we see many times, some prompts we've seen never, and in particular prompts that we've seen exactly one time in the training data. Those are singletons. And the Good–Turing estimate for the missing mass, which was developed during World War II to understand how many tanks the Germans were producing, is just to estimate what fraction of the points in your training set you've seen exactly one time.

Everything in your training set you've seen at least one time, but some of them you've seen two times, some of them you've seen three times. If you have a very large sample of data drawn from a small support, you expect that you've seen most things more than one time. On the other hand, if you have a small sample drawn from an enormous support, everything you've seen only once. And this is asking: what fraction of points in the training set have you seen only once?

28A Singleton Is New When We Hold It Out

Why should this have anything to do with what the missing mass is? Well, one way to think about it is that a point is a singleton exactly if it is missing from the rest of the data set. A point is a singleton if, when I consider the \(n-1\) other positions, this point would be missing mass in that remaining sub-data set. So one way to express the number of singletons is to sum up over all of the positions in my data set and ask: is the data point in that position missing from the remaining data set consisting of the remaining \(n-1\) points?

And so if I now take the expectation over the draw of my data set, because every point being a singleton is the same event as the point being missing from the remaining data set, the expectation of the Good–Turing estimator, the fraction of singletons, is exactly the expectation of the missing mass on a data set from which I've drawn \(n-1\) points. So my actual data set has \(n\) points. I don't know the missing mass from that. But the Good–Turing estimator is an unbiased estimator for the amount of missing mass I would expect to see if I had drawn a data set of one fewer point. So from a data set of size \(n\), I get a great estimate for the missing mass for a data set of size \(n-1\).

And so the only remaining question is: if I've drawn one more point, how much can one additional draw reduce the missing mass? If we can argue that one additional draw out of \(n\) can't reduce the missing mass by much, then, well, we've got an unbiased estimator for the missing mass for a data set of size \(n-1\), the missing mass can't have reduced by much by moving to a data set of size \(n\), and we'll have a good estimate for the missing mass of the data set of size \(n\).

29The Estimate Is High by at Most 1/n on Average

Let's think about this backwards. Let's think about how much we would increase the missing mass if we removed a uniformly random point from the data set. Or maybe a different way of thinking about it is: suppose I randomly permuted my data set and I removed the first point from it. How much in expectation would that increase the missing mass? It's just the backwards operation. It's the same answer to the question of, if I had \(n-1\) points, how much would I decrease the missing mass by sampling one more data point? Easier to think about backwards.

But there's only two different kinds of things that can happen when I remove a randomly chosen point. The point that I remove could have appeared more than once in my data set, in which case I haven't increased the missing mass. Or I could have removed a point that shows up only once. In that case, when I remove it, I increase the missing mass by exactly the mass of the point that I removed. But points that show up only once, the ones that have any possibility at all of increasing the missing mass, by definition occupy one of the \(n\) positions. Points that show up multiple times could occupy more than one, but singletons, definitionally, occupy only one of the positions.

So if I randomly permute my data set and I remove the first point, which is just equivalently removing a random point, what in expectation is the probability mass that I have removed from my data set? Well, the sum of the probability masses of all of the singletons in the data set is at most one. I'm removing one of them at random. So in expectation, the amount of mass I'm removing cannot be more than \(1/n\).

That means that when I look at the difference between the expected missing mass for a data set of size \(n\), the thing I care about, and the expected missing mass of a data set of size \(n-1\), the thing that I can estimate exactly with the Good–Turing estimate, that difference cannot be more than \(1/n\). I can't reduce the missing mass by adding one more data point by more than \(1/n\). Which means that the Good–Turing estimator, this thing that I can actually calculate on my data, gives me a good estimate of the missing mass. It gives me a perfectly unbiased estimate of the missing mass of a data set one point smaller. And the bias for what I actually care about, the data set of size \(n\), is at most \(1/n\).

Okay, so that's the last missing piece. I can actually figure out what the missing mass is for the problem that I'm solving, and therefore how badly having low calibration error will obligate me to do on hallucinations. Okay.
