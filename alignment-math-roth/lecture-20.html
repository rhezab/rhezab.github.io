1Cooperation Through Simulation

All right. I want to talk about program equilibria, an interesting idea for how you should think about strategic interactions in a world where a principal might not be directly acting on their own, but rather might be deploying, say, a software agent to act on their behalf.

2Overview

And the general idea is that in traditional game theory, when we study equilibria, the premise is that what we have the ability to unilaterally control, and hence the basis on which we define the stability of equilibria, is that we can unilaterally change the action that we take in the underlying game. And by unilateral, what we mean is I can change my action, but I can't change your action. And what equilibrium requires is that play be stable in the sense that nobody should be able to improve their own payoff by changing their own action, holding everyone else's fixed.

But in a world in which we have AI agents, perhaps we're not directly choosing the action we take. Rather, the part of the game that we play is in deciding what agent to deploy. Maybe what we're doing is deciding what piece of software acts on our behalf. And the thing about a piece of software is that it can be inspected. It can be simulated. And this potentially opens up a channel for cooperation, even in one-shot games, that doesn't exist if the premise is that you're playing in the game yourself, just because you are much more opaque than the piece of software. I can't simulate you and see what you would do in different situations.

The idea of program equilibrium is that the thing that I am deploying is a program, and the thing that you're deploying is a program, and your program potentially has access to mine and vice versa. You have the ability to simulate the piece of software that I deploy to act in my stead, and your piece of software in that simulation can decide what to do as a function of what happens when you simulate my agent, and vice versa.

And so although you're still going to be interested in notions of equilibria that say that nobody can benefit through unilateral deviation, what a deviation will now mean is that I will unilaterally deviate to deploy, for example, a different software agent. And although that doesn't change what software agent you deploy, it might change what your software agent does, because it has the ability to simulate mine. That's the basic premise of the field of program equilibrium.

6Users Choose Decision Rules, Not Just Actions

Good. So the idea is that what you and I are going to do to play in this game is we're not going to choose an action like cooperate or defect. And we'll consider the game in which our strategies are which program to deploy, rather than the game in which our strategies are actions. That is a different game. The underlying game that our programs are playing is still going to be Prisoner's Dilemma. But the game that we are playing is defined on top of this. 12The Rule: Sometimes Cooperate, Otherwise Simulate

So here's a simple way to do it. We'll just give a small probability of halting the recursion every round, and that'll be enough to make sure that it eventually stops. So let's say that rather than always waiting for your simulation to return and copying what you do, like the skeptical strategy, with some small probability \(\varepsilon\), I will just cooperate. I'll just trust that you would do the same, without even simulating.

So here's the strategy. Call it \(F_\varepsilon\), given query access to some opponent \(Q\). What it does is it first flips a coin, and with probability \(\varepsilon\), it just returns cooperate. It just cooperates; it doesn't do any further simulation. And with probability \(1-\varepsilon\), it tries to do another level of simulation. It says, okay, I'm going to simulate what your program would do, given access to my program, and to the extent that this simulation returns with an action, I will copy that action. If you cooperate, I'll cooperate. If you defect, I'll defect.

Does this strategy make sense? It's the same thing as the simulate-and-copy strategy, except now with a small escape probability \(\varepsilon\) from the simulation. It's pretty clear that introducing this small probability of not simulating my opponent will get rid of this infinite regress problem. If \(\varepsilon\) is very small, I might still have a very deep recursion stack, but it won't be infinitely deep, because the coins keep needing to come up heads for it to become infinitely deep, and that's not going to happen forever.

On the other hand, by introducing this possibility that I will cooperate even if you don't, I am, of course, reducing your incentive to cooperate. If \(\varepsilon\) was one and I always cooperated, clearly playing this strategy would not form an equilibrium; it would be worth your while to defect against it. And so there's something we need to balance here.

The idea is that my agent and your agent are going to be playing against one another for some randomly chosen number of rounds. 22The Simulator Builds a Shared History

Okay. Here's how the joint simulation is going to work. We'll begin with the outermost layer. This is base reality. This is the real world. And we're going to make recursive calls. Anything below the base level of reality is a simulation. And there is going to be this parameter \(\delta\) that will determine whether we continue for another recursive iteration or not. I guess it's got opposite scaling from the similar parameter \(\varepsilon\): \(\delta\) close to one now will mean we're likely to have very deep simulations.

So with probability \(\delta\), we're going to run another level of the joint simulation. We'll run another joint simulation, and we'll get whatever history it produces, or start with the empty history otherwise. Now, we get this history, either the empty history or this longer history that came from recursively running the simulation. And we will present this history to both assistants. They get the same history, so they're living in a consistent simulation. And we run them, and this causes them to choose actions. Their policies map histories to actions. And we will take this extra pair of actions that they just told us, and we will append it to the end of the history.

And if it turns out we were living in a simulation, then we return this extended history up the chain, one level above us. Or if it turns out that we were at the base level of reality, if this was the top of the recursion stack, then we play these actions. These are the actions that are actually played. And it is only the actions played at the end that determine the user utilities.

So the top level is base reality. The agents don't know if they're at the top level or not. What's happening is the agents see some history. The history is the history of the simulation. They choose an action. And if you are at the base level, those are the actions that are played. Otherwise, that's just appended onto the history, so that there's another input to a version of you at one level of reality higher.

23Only the Last Action Pair Determines Utility

Good. So how deep does the simulation go? Let's say \(N\) is the depth of the simulation, the number of recursive calls, the number of levels we've made below the base level of the real interaction. We can think about how the depth is distributed. To reach depth \(t\), we needed to flip a coin and have it come up "run another level of the simulation" \(t\) times in a row, followed by a final flip of the coin that decided not to run another level of the simulation. That's the only way you can get to depth \(t\). The probability of running another level of the simulation is \(\delta\). The probability of not running another level of the simulation is \(1-\delta\). So the probability that the depth ends up being \(t\) is exactly \((1-\delta)\delta^t\). The recursion ends after a finite number of rounds with probability 1 whenever \(\delta\) is less than 1.

And let's think about the history ordered from the deepest level of the simulation first. The deepest level of the simulation is what responds to giving the agents an empty history. Thinking about this as the history of a repeated game, that's the level at which we get the initial pair of actions that I'll call \(a^0\). Those get passed up the recursion stack. So the simulated versions of the players that played \(a^1\), they saw this history \(a^0\). At whatever turns out to be base reality, capital \(N\), the agents here see the whole history \(a^0\) through \(a^{N-1}\).

So the point is the agents don't know what \(N\) is. They can know what the distribution on \(N\) is, but they don't know for certain whether they are sitting here, in which case their action actually matters, or whether they're sitting somewhere earlier in the recursion.

25A Random Final Round Gives Discounted Payoffs

So we just need to unroll this equivalence. The way we ran the simulation was essentially, at every round, we independently flipped a coin to decide whether today was going to be the real round or not, whether we would run the simulation for one round further. We start simulating people, and every day we flip a coin to decide whether today's going to be the real round or not. But equivalently, I could first generate the infinite sequence of play. I could unroll the whole repeated game. And then I could draw capital \(N\), the index of the final round, from the appropriate distribution, and say that's the real round. We know what the distribution on \(N\) is. We know what is the probability that the final round \(N\) is chosen to be day \(t\). 26Cooperate Unless the History Contains a Defection

So there's a big literature on equilibria in repeated games, but it's very easy for them to sustain cooperation. Consider the following very simple strategy. In a repeated game, I see a whole history of what was done in the past. So a very simple strategy is that I will cooperate so long as historically you have cooperated with me. But I'm not going to forgive defections. If I ever observe you fail to cooperate with me, then I'll never forgive you. I'll just defect forever. It's what's called the Grim Trigger strategy. It just says, look, if the history only contains cooperation, I'll cooperate. If it contains anything else, I'm going to defect.

And it's not hard to show that in the repeated game, and therefore also in the joint simulation model, because they have exactly the same set of equilibria, so long as \(\delta\) is greater than one half, so long as in the repeated game you care about tomorrow at least half of what you care about today, or so long as in the simulation I'm more likely than not to continue the simulation for one more round at any given moment, two copies of this Grim Trigger strategy form a Nash equilibrium and induce cooperation. Because of this equivalence between the simulation and the repeated game, it suffices to analyze this in the repeated game.

27The First Defection Gains One but Loses Future Help

So suppose we're both playing this Grim Trigger strategy. If neither of us deviates, we're both going to be cooperating along the whole path of play. And now I am considering some deviation. If my deviation is going to be different on the path of play, that means there is some round \(\tau\) in which I'm going to do something different. There's only one other thing I can do in Prisoner's Dilemma, so I'm going to defect. So there's some round \(\tau\) in which I'm going to defect.

And before \(\tau\), we're playing in exactly the same way. I'm cooperating, you're cooperating. We're both getting payoff one. There's no change in behavior at that moment. Then, at round \(\tau\), this is when I pull the trigger. This is my time to accrue all of the additional payoff from my defection. And at this round, I get payoff two. You help me; I don't pay the cost of helping you. And I experience the consequences of this at every future round, because, by revealing my true colors, I have caused you to defect at every future round, which means that what I can hope to gain at future rounds is at most zero.

And this is viewing things through the repeated-game lens. Through the simulation lens, this would mean, on one hand, if I'm lucky, and the day at which I deviated was the real world, then I would gain one by deviating. But if the real world was only going to come later, then I would have failed to gain. And what would be relevant is my belief that the real world is today versus at any future day.

What's my expected gain from deviation? Well, before \(\tau\), I haven't gained anything. Everything's the same. I gain 1 at day \(\tau\). That's discounted. So the gain that I get is \((1-\delta)\delta^\tau\): that's the discount factor up to day \(\tau\) times the 1 unit I gain by deviating. And I lose one at every future round after \(\tau\). So that's also discounted. It's more heavily discounted because it's the future, but it's summed up over all of the infinitely many future rounds. And what we get is that my gain is negative if \(\delta\) is greater than one half.

28A Cooperative Equilibrium Is Only Part of Alignment

Okay, so maybe just one thing to point out. Through this lens of program equilibrium, when we generalize single-shot games to repeated games in the simulation world, we're doing exactly the same thing, but morally we're doing something similar at least when we talk about program equilibria even with just unilateral simulation: we are enlarging the set of equilibria. For example, there are equilibria that result in cooperation in Prisoner's Dilemma, which didn't exist for one-shot games in which we had to play directly.

But that doesn't mean that we are making things better; there's just more equilibria. Equilibria aren't necessarily better. Some of them are better, but some of them might be worse. There are more equilibria. In all of these settings, it is still an equilibrium for us to always defect. Right? It's just that there are also equilibria now that involve cooperation. So expanding the set of equilibria may or may not be good, right? You're expanding opportunities to be mutually helpful, but you're also expanding opportunities for collusion and cartels and other kinds of bad behavior.
