2Overview

The idea is, maybe as AI tools advance, their human users will—maybe you should think of them as [poorer] agents compared to the models themselves. We're already getting there in mathematics. A well-defined math problem… I don't recommend you work on the Riemann hypothesis yourself unless you work at OpenAI. But still, we don't want to directly trust the outputs of these models. And so we're put in a funny oversight situation in which we, the weaker judge, are put on the spot to evaluate the outputs of these potentially stronger systems that can solve problems that we cannot.

And so the idea of AI debate is a pretty natural one. It's to essentially set up a trial. I think very much about a trial in the criminal justice system, where there's going to be a prosecutor and a defense attorney. And both of them are AI models. So I might not be able to read through the 10,000-page proof, but my defense attorney can. Now, I don't necessarily want to trust the defense attorney either. That's just another AI model. So I don't want to just entirely delegate to them the verification task. But the idea is that these two models should be able to go back and forth and hopefully boil down the heart of the dispute to a small enough disagreement that, even though I am but a lowly human being, a computationally bounded agent, I can still verify this little chunk. I can't read the whole proof, but maybe through this back-and-forth interaction they end up pointing their finger at the jump from equation 372 to equation 373, and I can look at that and I can say, oh, that step is wrong.

That's the basic idea. The idea is we want to figure out how to arrange things so that, through this interaction between two powerful debating agents, [we] can verify tasks that might otherwise have taken us, say, exponential time to verify directly.

3Can a Weaker Judge Oversee Stronger Systems?

Okay. Yeah, so maybe the thing you should have in mind is something like: ChatGPT outputs for you a 10,000-page proof [of] the Riemann hypothesis, or it could be the result of some very long computation, or anything else. But think about it: maybe in principle there is a step-by-step way to verify the correctness of some claim by applying reasonable logical rules. The only problem is the certificate that you'd have to verify is much too long for you to do.

And we are going to define a precise debate protocol. There will be a claim made, like "this thing proves the Riemann hypothesis." And there will be two parties, one of which is tasked with convincing me that it's true, and the other of which is tasked with convincing me that it's false. And the premise is that I can't read through all 10,000 pages. I don't have time to check all of the derivations line by line. But I can check, for example, any one of them. So I'm smart enough to check any one of them. And so the goal of the protocol is to narrow the disagreements about where the mistake is, if there is any mistake, until at the end the disagreement is sufficiently narrow that they can call me in and I can [check it].

Okay. Again, we're going to have a prosecutor and a defender. Just like in the criminal justice system, neither one of them is really rewarded for causing me to believe the truth. The prosecutor wants me to believe the claim is true no matter what. The defender wants me to believe the claim is false no matter what, independently of the truth. But we'd like to define rules of essentially the game so that, when both players are playing well, if the result is true then I will end up believing that at the end of the protocol, and if the result is false I will end up believing that. So the parties don't necessarily care about truth; they just care about convincing me in their assigned direction. But we want the rules of the interaction to be such that when the two parties play this game well, the one that will win will correspond to the one that happened to be assigned the role of arguing for whatever happened to be true. Make sense?

The keyword for the broader literature that this fits into is scalable oversight. We'd like to be able to have methods that allow us to give guarantees about systems that we don't trust. There's still a human being in the end who's making the judgment, but [the method] allows us to maintain this ability to oversee the system as the abilities of the agents grow and the abilities of the human beings don't.

5The Debaters Are Rewarded for Opposite Verdicts

So let's write down a formal model of this. This is going to be a stylized model in the style of complexity theory, if you've ever taken an algorithms or complexity course. So the inputs will be Boolean strings. The thing we're trying to verify is the value of some Boolean function applied to the Boolean string. But this is really quite general, right? The input could be an encoding of a Lean statement of the Riemann hypothesis. The \(f(x)\) could be a certification that this thing is in fact the correct proof of the Riemann hypothesis.

So there'll be two parties. The proponent's job is to convince me that the value of this function is one. And the opponent's job is to convince me of the opposite: that the value of this function, \(f(x)\), is zero. They're going to have a conversation with one another. I can look at the conversation. So I can, for example, impose rules on it. I can impose syntactic constraints on how the conversation is allowed to proceed. That's the design power I have. I can design the interaction.

The conversation is not going to be very long. I need to be able to read the conversation to make sure that they're following the rules. So I don't want the conversation to be, for example, a step-by-step audit of every single step in this proof. That would be too long for me to read. I need a short transcript of the conversation to read. And at the end of it, I, the human judge, output my own judgment. Do I think that \(f(x)\) is zero, or do I think that \(f(x)\) is one? Okay, so this is the oversight part. We get to design the protocol, but ultimately judgment remains in the hands of the human judge.

Now, it is a game. The proponent receives payoff equal to \(J\), and the opponent receives payoff equal to \(-J\). It's some zero-sum game. And neither party cares about truth necessarily. They're not trying to convince me of whatever is true, just as the defense attorney's job is to get their client off even if they think they might be guilty. So those are the presumed incentives here. And what we'd like to do is use our power of protocol design to specify the format in which messages are sent so that when players optimally play this game—play what will turn out to be their dominant strategies in this game—the result is always correct.

6What Must a Correct Debate Protocol Guarantee?

So what we are allowed to specify when designing a protocol are things like: what are the messages you're allowed to send? It doesn't have to be that they talk in natural language. We can define a constrained format in which they're allowed to communicate. We can decide things like turn order. And of course, we're the judge, and you can think of the judge also as being something programmatic that we design. We just don't want it to be one of these AIs that we don't trust. But we also get to decide on what criterion, as a function of the transcript of this interaction, the judge will decide to announce that he believes the answer is 1 or he believes the answer is 0. So that's what we get to decide. We're designing the rules of the game here.

On the other hand, it is up to the agents to decide their strategy. Once the protocol is defined, the agent gets to decide, within the rules of the game, how they are going to respond, what they are going to utter to the other agent, as a function of the transcript of the interaction so far. We can't control what they do, but we'd like to set things up so that when they play this game well to optimize their own utility, the thing that we want to happen, which is to learn the truth, in fact happens.

So, to be clear, we're writing down a very simple stylized setting. And we're going to ask for something very strong: perfect soundness and perfect completeness. You can imagine in more difficult settings relaxing these very strong kinds of desiderata, but since we're talking about a very simple setting, there's no need for us to. We're going to ask for perfect soundness and perfect completeness.

What that means is that if it turns out that on the instance we are debating, \(x\), \(f(x)\) really is 1, then the proponent should have a dominant strategy that guarantees a win. The proponent should have a strategy that forces the judge to announce one, no matter what the opponent does. If the truth is on his side, then the proponent should have a strategy that does not depend on what the opponent is doing that will convince the judge, correctly, that the answer is one. If the truth is on the side of the proponent, the proponent should win the game.

Perfect soundness is just the opposite. If indeed on this instance the answer is 0, \(f(x)\) is 0, then the opponent should have a strategy that, no matter what the proponent does, forces the judge to agree, in this case correctly, that the answer is 0. Okay, so this is just saying that having the truth on your side should be a good thing in this game. If the truth is on your side, you also have a strategy that guarantees that you win this game. You can imagine relaxing this in various ways, but in the simple way we're setting up the problem, we're not going to need to.

7Polynomial Memory Can Support a Long Computation

So, yeah, I'm going to say this in the language of Turing machines, but if you haven't taken automata theory, if you don't know what a Turing machine is, it kind of doesn't matter. It's just some formal model of computation. Whenever I say Turing machine, if you think to yourself "Python interpreter," you're not really losing anything. A Turing machine is just some model of a program.

The premise is, at any given moment, you can look at the current state, whatever is stored in memory, and the code, the rules of the program. Figuring out what the program does if you tried to run it to completion might be hard, just because it might run for a long time. But you can understand it; it's probably like a Python program. I've got a Python program on my computer. I could start it at some state and run it for one step, no problem. But I might not be able to run it on my computer [to completion if] it needs to run for a long time.

So imagine we've got a computer program, and what it does is on every input \(x\) it outputs \(f(x)\). Okay, maybe it's some automated proof assistant. I give it the statement of any provable or disprovable mathematical fact, like the statement of the Riemann hypothesis. And what it's going to do is it's just going to iterate through all proofs of some bounded length and check each of them. And in the end, it'll say, oh, I found a proof, or I found a disproof. That's a program that you could, in principle, write and run on your computer for a small number of steps, but you might not be able to run it for long enough for it to actually find a proof or a disproof of the Riemann hypothesis.

We can categorize programs by the amount of resources they need to solve a problem. Maybe the most familiar complexity classes you've heard of have to do with time. So maybe you've heard of classes like P and NP. This has to do with the problems that a computer program can solve in a polynomial number of steps (P), or, if nondeterminism is allowed, a polynomial number of steps with nondeterminism. But we can also define complexity classes as a function of the amount of working memory they need. It turns out memory is a more powerful resource than time, if I don't constrain time.

And so the class PSPACE is the set of all problems that can be solved by computer programs using an amount of working memory that is only polynomial in the length of the input. So I can do lots and lots of things in PSPACE. Having polynomial space is much, much more powerful than having polynomial time. For example, I can say, okay, I'm going to enumerate all possible 10-page proofs. I will check each one for correctness and I will alert you if I find one that proves or disproves your conjecture. And it might take a really long time to enumerate all 10-page proofs, but I don't need very much working memory, because I don't need to remember all of the previous ones. I just need to remember the counter, to make sure I don't check the same proofs over and over again, and I need to have in memory whatever is the current proof.

So I can do lots of stuff. Anything I can do in polynomial time, I can also do in polynomial space, because it's not using up the space very fast. But I can do stuff in polynomial space that might require exponential time. PSPACE is a very large complexity class.

Just for a classical example: the most famous NP-complete problem is 3SAT. I've got a bunch of logical clauses on Boolean variables. The question is, is there any assignment of those Boolean variables that causes this Boolean formula to evaluate to true? It's NP-hard. So we believe there's no polynomial-time algorithm to solve this problem. But again, there's trivially a polynomial-space algorithm to solve this problem: the naive enumeration algorithm. If there's \(n\) variables, it takes me \(2^n\) time to enumerate over all of them. So that takes exponential time, but it doesn't take exponential space. Any particular assignment takes \(n\) bits. To enumerate through them in lexicographic order, I just need to maintain a counter to make sure I cycle through; I only have to count up to \(2^n\), and that only takes \(n\) bits. So in fact I can do this in linear space. PSPACE gives me more than that. If I use quadratic space or cubic space, that would also count as PSPACE. Okay, so PSPACE in particular contains all of the NP-complete problems. PSPACE is a big complexity class.

9A Configuration Is a Complete Machine Snapshot

Again, for historical reasons, all of the terminology is in terms of Turing machines. But if you don't know what a Turing machine is, nothing is lost on you if you just think to yourself "Python interpreter." So a configuration of the Turing machine, that's just a memory snapshot of the machine. That's just specifying all of the state that is needed to run one additional step of computation. It's whatever is loaded into RAM, whatever scratch space, whatever scratch files you have; that's the configuration of the Turing machine. And you've got the code of the program, and so if you've got the configuration of the Turing machine and the code of the program, you can run this thing forward one step.

And for problems in PSPACE, the whole point is that although the program might take a long time to run, the configuration isn't too big. The configuration can fit in a polynomial number of bits. That's how we define PSPACE.

So there's some transition rule that's running the program for one [step]. Let's call that \(M\). So for any configuration \(c\), the idea is one thing that we can do efficiently is run the program one step. We can compute \(M(c)\), the next configuration after \(c\). Think about that as: you load the memory snapshot of the machine and you run the Python program one iteration, and now you have some new configuration of the machine. And iterating the program just corresponds to iterating this map. So we can write \(M^t(c)\) for the configuration that we get when we start at some configuration \(c\) and then run the program forwards for \(t\) steps. Okay, that's something we can compute if \(t\) is not too big, but these programs might run for a long time. So if \(t\) is exponentially big, then we can't compute that.

And we know what \(x\) is. You should think about it this way: the input itself and the configurations, those are small objects, polynomial objects. So the judge can make sure that they're formatted correctly. You can't fool me into thinking something is a valid state of the machine when it's going to cause things not to parse or something. And I can also run the program for [one step]. So I can always advance the program from one configuration to the next. But if I want to run the program for \(t\) steps, that'll take time proportional to \(t\). So I can run the program forward for a small number of steps, not a big number of steps.

10Make the Final Configuration Known

So, just to simplify the notation, we are going to [assume], without loss of generality, [that] the program has a canonical ending state. Okay, so we want there to be a canonical configuration that I'll call the accepting configuration, \(c_{\text{acc}}\), that any answer of one will end up in. So if there's somehow multiple different states that you can end up in when the answer is one, we will just modify the program, in ways that don't change the fact that it uses polynomial space, to always merge those different accepting states into one canonical state. So without loss of generality, there's a canonical accepting state. Without loss of generality, there's a canonical rejecting state.

And another thing that would be a nuisance of notation, but isn't a big deal: if I want to say, okay, what state is the program in if I run it for a million steps, I don't want you to say, oh, that's ill-defined, because the program actually halted at 999,998 steps. No: if the program ends up in a terminal state, in an accepting state, early, we'll just say notationally that it stays in that state for all subsequent steps. So even if the program halts before a million steps, it makes sense to ask what state it's in at step a million. And if it halted in the accepting state, it's still in the accepting state at step a million. If it halted in the rejecting state, it's still in the rejecting state at step a million.

Again, this is just a without-loss-of-generality notational thing. It doesn't change the nature of what you can compute. This is just to give things a canonical representation that will make it easy for us to talk about it.

11Exponentially Many Steps Always Suffice

Cool. The first very simple observation is that although problems in PSPACE might take more than polynomial time to solve—it could take exponential time to solve—there's never a need for a problem in PSPACE to take more than exponential time. And the reason's pretty simple. PSPACE is defined by a memory limitation. It's problems that can be solved with working memory of at most \(m\) bits, where \(m\) is some polynomial in the input. And, well, how many strings are there of at most \(m\) bits? There's at most \(2^m\) of them.

Now, the program has no memory other than the configuration. The configuration is everything you need to know about the state of the program. So the premise that we have a program that computes \(f\) means that on every input \(x\), the thing halts. It outputs 0 or 1. It doesn't run forever. If it ran forever, then it wouldn't be a function that computed \(f\). But if we ever hit the same configuration twice, it would be guaranteed to run forever. It would be in a loop, right? Because first I get the configuration \(c_1\), then I run for a while, and I eventually get the configuration \(c_2\). If I ever go back to \(c_1\), and now if I keep running, I'll get to \(c_2\) again, and then \(c_1\) and \(c_2\). I'll run forever. Because the configuration is the entire state of the program. There's no additional memory that would cause it to do different things.

So the fact that I know that there's only \(2^m\) distinct configurations of \(m\) bits, and that I'm dealing with a program that always halts and outputs something, means that I know that it just can't run for more than \(2^m\) steps. Because there's only \(2^m\) different configurations. If it ran for more steps than that, it must have visited the same configuration twice, and that would prove that on that input, the program didn't halt. So problems that can be solved in a polynomial amount of space, in particular, can always be solved in at most an exponential amount of time. We're going to use this fact. Does that make sense?

So, with all of these canonicalizations in mind and this observation: we're interested in the question, is \(f(x)\) equal to 1? And if we have a computer program for computing \(f\), then the question of whether \(f(x)\) is equal to 1 is exactly equal to the question: if I run the computer program from the initial configuration for \(T\) steps, where \(T\) is \(2^m\), do I end up in the accepting configuration? I know that this thing is going to halt after at most \(2^m\) steps, because it has polynomial-size memory. 13A Midpoint Splits One Claim into Two

Okay. So the claims are always about what happens if we run the computation forward for some number of steps that's a power of two. This means that the claim is always about a computation whose length is a power of two. There's always a midpoint. So if indeed it is true that when I start at \(c\) and run for \(2^j\) steps I get to \(c'\), then there should be some midpoint configuration \(z\) that's exactly in the middle. So if I start at \(c\) and run for half as many steps, \(2^{j-1}\) steps, I end up at the midpoint. And then if I continue the computation from there, start at \(z\) and run for half as many steps again, \(2^{j-1}\), I get to \(c'\).

If there's a correct midpoint, then this decomposes the initial claim—that if I start at \(c\) and run for \(2^j\) steps, I'll get to \(c'\)—into two smaller subclaims. If I start at \(c\) and run for \(2^{j-1}\) steps, I end up at \(z\). That's claim one. And then, if I start at \(z\) and run for \(2^{j-1}\) steps, I end up at \(c'\). So a midpoint splits one of these assertions into two simpler sub-assertions. And it does so in a way that preserves the truth value of the initial claim. So the initial claim is true if and only if there is a midpoint whose corresponding two sub-assertions are both true.

The assertion that if I start at \(c\) and run for \(2^j\) steps I'll get to \(c'\) is the same as the assertion that there exists some midpoint \(z\) such that if I start at \(c\) and run for \(2^{j-1}\) steps, I get to \(z\), and if I start at \(z\) and run for \(2^{j-1}\) steps, I get to \(c'\). These are equivalent in truth value. If the right-hand side is true, the left-hand side is true, and vice versa. This is going to be the basic fact that underlies the design of the debate protocol.

So the proponent, the thing they get to do is they get to propose, for the current claim they are defending, a midpoint. [If the] claim is true, there is a midpoint that makes both subclaims true. Now, we can't just go and check both subclaims. That would end up with some exponentially growing tree of things we would need to check; we'd have to run in exponential time. But that's what the opponent will save us from having to do. Rather than checking both subclaims, the opponent will point to which of these two subclaims they believe to be false. They're trying to convince us that the composite claim is false. Well, if the composite claim is false, then it's got to be that at least one of the two subclaims is false, no matter what midpoint was proposed. We don't have time to check them both, because that would result in some exponentially growing computation. The opponent will tell us which one to check.

And you might ask: do we really need a debate? Do we need two smart parties here? Maybe there's some stochastic version of this where we just have the proponent. 19Soundness: Every False Parent Has a False Half

What about soundness? Well, we need to establish that there's always a choice the opponent can make that will cause us to continue to be debating a false claim. Suppose the claim we started with—that if we start at \(c\) and run for \(2^j\) steps we get to \(c'\)—suppose that's false. Then for any midpoint proposed by the proponent… since the claim was false, there isn't a real midpoint. Could it be that both of the subclaims induced by this midpoint are true? Well, no, it couldn't be. Because if they were both true… I ask the initial claim: if I start at \(c\) and run for \(2^j\) steps, do I get to \(c'\)? Well, no. And another way to write that is: if I start at \(c\) and run for \(2^{j-1}\) steps, and then run for another \(2^{j-1}\) steps, do I get to \(c'\)? And this gets to \(z\); the fact that this gets to \(z\), that's the left subclaim. The fact that this gets to \(c'\), that's the right subclaim. If they were both true, the initial claim would have been true as well. The initial claim was false, so at least one of the subclaims has to be false.

Which means that if the initial claim is false, for every proposed midpoint, at least one half is false. And so the opponent always has a winning strategy. They're given two choices of subclaims that they want to continue debating. At least one of them is false; they can always choose the false one.

20Soundness: Keep One False Claim Alive

This is a winning strategy if the claim is false. The initial claim is that \(f\) is 1, so that if we start at the initial state and run for \(2^m\) steps, we get to the accepting state. 21What Would Practical Debate Need to Reproduce?

Obviously, there's some gaps between what we've proven, which is essentially a theorem in complexity theory about powerful provers, and what you would need to implement in a more realistic setting. In general, I think that gap is a legitimate open research gap. You all should please solve it. But at a heuristic level, the ingredients you need to at least implement something like this are three.

So first is that the debate should be able to make some quantitative progress towards narrowing the claim. The whole point is that we start with some claim that is too hard for the human judge to verify. And what we need to do is get to narrower and narrower claims until we get one that is simple enough for the judge to actually verify. And we need the truth value of the initial claim to be maintained through this narrowing. The thing the judge can verify should actually be related to the thing he cares about, in the sense that it should have the same truth value. So that's what's happening here. And if you were going to try to make something like this work in real life, you'd somehow need some aspect of that.

You also need that eventually you get to a narrow enough subclaim that the judge can check it on their own. So you're making progress towards something; that something should be something the judge can actually check.

And finally, this only works if the debaters are playing the game well. And so it's not enough that there are winning moves in the game; the debaters need to be able to find them. [We] kind of just wished that away by assuming they could solve these PSPACE-hard problems, but of course that's a real issue.

And anyways, there's a lively literature on AI debate.
