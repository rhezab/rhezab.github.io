2Overview

So, yeah, we're going to learn about an old theorem today, Arrow's impossibility theorem, but the motivation fits the modern RLHF pipeline pretty well, I think. So, in a summary slide: in a reinforcement learning from human feedback pipeline, what's going to happen is we're going to get these kinds of labels, "I like this one," from various human labelers who might very well have different preferences. We're going to try to learn a reward model that can predict those preferences, that we're going to use downstream for further training. And so somehow we're trying to compress a bunch of incomparable human labels that come from human preference data into a single training objective.

And so what we're going to think about today, what Arrow's impossibility theorem is about, is: first of all, if we're going to try to learn a single set of preferences that is representative of many people, what are some very basic desiderata that we might want of the aggregated set of preferences? And unfortunately, what we're going to see, the reason Arrow's theorem is called Arrow's impossibility theorem, is that there's essentially no way to aggregate preferences while satisfying even these two very basic desiderata. Meaning, there's one way to satisfy these two desiderata, but it is to give up on aggregating preferences and implement what is called a dictatorship: just following one person's preferences and ignoring all of the rest. That's the plan for today.

3Human Comparisons Become a Training Objective

Okay, so maybe a sort of high-level schematic. It's sort of useful to have some picture of RLHF in your mind. Given a particular prompt \(x\), when we talk about preferences, the objects we're going to imagine people have preferences over are completions, the thing the LLM might say in response to your prompt. And to make things simple to think about, we're going to imagine that the prompt is fixed. There's one prompt, and we're just thinking about what we should do for that one prompt.

So people, given the context, have preferences over what the language model should say. There's various things the language model could respond with, and we're going to sample a bunch of them. Language models are stochastic, so we can get a bunch of samples. And we're going to present those to human annotators in pairs, and we're going to say, which of these two completions do you like better, \(a\) or \(b\)? And we're going to gather a bunch of binary comparison data from the labelers. People will go and read these two completions and they'll say, I like the one on the left better, or I like the one on the right better.

At which point we try to train a reward model that tries to map the prompt, the context, which is for the purposes of this lecture fixed, together with the completion, to a number, the idea being bigger numbers are better. And then, downstream of that, something that happens beyond what we're talking about in this lecture, the reward model is used to fine-tune the language model.

4Reward Fitting Contains an Aggregation Problem

So, again, we're going to zoom in on just the preference aggregation step in this lecture. So there's just one prompt. There's a set of candidate completions that we'll fix. There's infinitely many you could imagine, but we're not going to use infinitely many to gather human data about. We have some fixed pool. Call that set \(C\). We've got labelers who compare pairs. For this lecture, we won't worry too much about the distribution of comparisons. We'll just assume that we've gotten some comparisons for every pair. And the goal ultimately is to learn some scalar reward model that assigns a number to each one.

What I want to observe is that having learned a scalar reward model, we have induced a single ranking on all of the candidates. Because although the reward model is assigning numbers to these completions, we can just sort them from highest to lowest. We don't have cardinal utilities from the labelers. We only have comparisons. So at best, from each labeler, all we know is their ordering from favorite to least favorite of the completions. And one consequence of learning a reward model is that we have specified what we think the ordering should be. So the question of Arrow's theorem is, okay, what should that ranking be?

6Pairwise Majority Decides Each Contest Separately

One thing that might seem very natural is to just take majority votes on pairs of completions. If I'm asking in isolation the question of which completion should I prefer, \(a\) or \(b\), it's a binary question. Maybe I just have people vote. Okay, let's poll all the labelers: is \(a\) better than \(b\) or not? And that's pretty natural. Let's call that the pairwise majority relation. This determines: in my population, are there more people who prefer \(a\) to \(b\) or not? If the answer is yes, the pairwise majority relation also prefers \(a\) to \(b\); otherwise it doesn't. So maybe that's the first thing you would think.

But although that's a pretty natural way to answer the binary question, do we as a group prefer \(a\) to \(b\), it is not clear that this is going to work to produce a coherent set of preferences that can be represented as a transitive ordering. Which is, again, implicitly the thing that we must be doing if we're learning a reward model, because a reward model assigns a number to each of the completions, [which sorts them] from highest to lowest.

7Coherent Individuals Can Produce a Cycle

And the problem that arises immediately when you start thinking about examples like this is that, well, let's see, should we prefer \(a\) to \(b\)? Well, yes, because when we vote on which is better, \(a\) or \(b\), there's two votes for \(a\) is better than \(b\). Type 1 prefers \(a\) to \(b\), type 3 prefers \(a\) to \(b\); there's only one vote for \(b\) is better than \(a\). So majority vote says \(a\) is better than \(b\). When we ask, should we prefer \(b\) to \(c\)? The answer is yes. There's two votes for \(b\) is better than \(c\). Type 1 votes for \(b\) is better than \(c\), as does type 2; only type 3 votes the other way. And when we ask, should we prefer \(c\) to \(a\)? The answer is once again yes. There's two votes for \(c\) is better than \(a\), type two and type three. There's only one vote for \(a\) is better than \(c\).

So far so good. But unfortunately this has produced a cycle. This is not a transitive ordering, because we think \(a\) is better than \(b\), we think \(b\) is better than \(c\), but we think \(c\) is better than \(a\). Okay, so there's no single ordering, and therefore there's no scalar reward model that will correctly represent these pairwise majority preferences. Makes sense?

This is just an example. This isn't the impossibility theorem that we're going to prove in the lecture, but maybe it's giving you some sense that there's going to be some difficulty in the aggregation. We have these different preferences. The issue is not that people are irrational. The issue is not that there's any voter here who, when we ask them, is \(a\) better than \(b\), is \(b\) better than \(c\), is \(c\) better than \(a\), somehow violates transitivity. Everyone has coherent preferences, but pairwise majority vote does not fit together into a set of coherent preferences. It induces this [cycle]. Yes?

8Why Ask for a Full Ranking?

And Arrow's theorem was originally about elections. Arrow's was a theorem from 1951, before we were learning reward models. And in the original model, you might have said, okay, well, this difficulty that's arising, both in the object the impossibility theorem will be about and in the example of the failure of pairwise majority voting, arises because you need to aggregate these preferences into a full preference order. Whereas in an election, maybe you don't really need to do that. Everyone might individually have preferences over all of the candidates running, but ultimately you just pick one.

But the reward modeling application doesn't have room for that objection. Because if you're learning a reward model, you actually are coming up with a full ranking. And it's not just the first completion that's relevant for training. You're using […].

And so the example we've seen, all it shows is that if our goal is to aggregate individual preferences into some good aggregate ranking, we can't use pairwise majority. But maybe there's some other natural rule we can use, and Arrow's theorem is going to be about characterizing what all such rules must look like, subject to hopefully natural axioms. Okay.

9The Aggregation Object

Okay. So let's write down the objects we're talking about. We're going to have some finite set of alternatives. These are the things that people have preferences over. Think about it as, for a fixed prompt, a set of possible completions. And we're going to assume that people have strict preference orders on this set of alternatives.

We're going to assume the preference ordering is complete. That just means you have preferences: I can ask you about any pair, \(a\) and \(b\), and you will express some preference, \(a\) is better than \(b\) or \(b\) is better than \(a\). It's not like I can't ask you about some of them. You have preferences about all of them, every pair. And your preferences are transitive. So for any individual, if I ask you, is \(a\) better than \(b\), and then I ask you, is \(b\) better than \(c\), and the answer was yes to both of those questions, I don't need to ask you, is \(a\) better than \(c\)? Your preferences are transitive. That's something we're going to assume.

And let's just give notation. We're going to think about mappings from collections of preference orderings to one aggregate preference ordering. And so the domain of these mappings will be sets of preference orderings. So let's give some notation for that. Let's say that \(L(C)\) is the set of all preference orderings over these alternatives. You can just identify each one with some permutation over the alternatives. So the input to an aggregation problem is just some collection of \(n\) preference orderings. We're going to assume there's \(n\) people, \(n\) labelers in the population. Each one of them has a full preference ordering over a set of possible completions, but people are different. So each person might potentially have a different preference ordering.

And the kind of objects that we're going to study, which maybe we'll call a social ranking rule, is just a mapping that takes as input the preferences of, say, \(n\) labelers, and outputs a single aggregate preference intended to represent the collective preferences. So this is the kind of object that is induced by a reward model. We elicit by pairwise comparisons everyone's preference ranking, we train a reward model, and by the reward you get another ranking of completions. So this is the kind of object we're going to study. It takes as input \(n\) preferences from \(n\) different people. Each of them are complete, transitive preferences, but they might be different from one another, and it outputs just one complete, transitive preference intended to somehow represent their aggregate. Make sense? Questions?

10Axiom 1: Pareto Unanimity

So now I want to ask, what are some hopefully minimal properties that it might be nice if this aggregation rule satisfies? There's lots of aggregation rules, but what are some things we want? And hopefully these axioms appear to be minimal, unobjectionable things that you might want.

So the first one is called Pareto unanimity. We talked about it just briefly in the overview lecture last time, but it's really quite weak. What it says is, if all of the human labelers uniformly and unanimously agree that \(a\) is better than \(b\), then the aggregate preference should also express that \(a\) is better than \(b\).

But one thing I want to note here is the requirement on the left-hand side is that there's unanimous agreement. Everyone thinks \(a\) is better than \(b\). Only then is it requiring that the aggregate rank \(a\) better than \(b\). If there's even one disagreement, if \(n-1\) people think \(a\) is better than \(b\), if there's even one person who disagrees, then this axiom is putting no restriction at all on what the aggregation can do. It can do whatever it wants, so long as there's not unanimous agreement that \(a\) is better than \(b\). All this is saying is that if the labelers unanimously agree that \(a\) is better than \(b\), then a good aggregation rule should also assert that \(a\) is better than \(b\).

The axiom makes sense? The intention is that this should be very reasonable. You might imagine asking for something stronger. Before you saw this cyclic example, maybe you would have hoped that you could satisfy a majority vote rule: if the majority of people think \(a\) is better than \(b\), then the aggregate should think \(a\) is better than \(b\). We already know that's not going to be possible. That's the cyclic example. This is asking for something much, much weaker than that. […] If there's unanimous agreement on some comparison, the social aggregation shouldn't reverse that unanimous [agreement]. Okay.

11Axiom 2: Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives

Here's another one. It's called independence of irrelevant alternatives. It says the social aggregation rule, when determining whether \(a\) is better than \(b\), should depend only on the comparisons that the labelers have, only on the beliefs that the labelers have regarding \(a\) and \(b\). It shouldn't be that there's some secret third option unrelated to \(a\) and \(b\) such that if I change that, it changes what the social ordering is between \(a\) and \(b\).

Okay, so it's saying something like: if on the one hand everyone thinks that \(a\) is the best and \(b\) is the worst and \(c\) is somewhere in between, and on the other hand everyone thinks \(a\) is the best and \(c\) is the worst and \(b\) is somewhere in between, then this can affect how we rank \(c\) relative to \(a\) and \(b\), but it shouldn't affect how we rank \(a\) and \(b\) relative to one another.

So formally it's saying, suppose we've got two different profiles of labeler preferences. They could be different, but they are not different in how anyone in the profile of preferences ranks \(a\) relative to \(b\). Okay, so for each person \(i\), if person \(i\) thinks \(a\) is better than \(b\) on the left, they also think \(a\) is better than \(b\) on the right, and vice versa. So between the left and the right preferences, people might have changed how they rank \(a\) and \(b\) relative to third options, like \(c\), but nobody has changed how they rank \(a\) and \(b\) relative to one another. Then the IIA axiom asks that the social aggregate that comes out of both of these profiles should rank \(a\) and \(b\) the same way, whatever that way is. It's not constraining it to rank \(a\) better than \(b\) or \(b\) better than \(a\), but it's saying if it ranks \(a\) better than \(b\) on the left, it should also rank \(a\) better than \(b\) on the right, because nobody's preference between \(a\) and \(b\) has changed. Does that make sense?

These are the two axioms that go into Arrow's theorem. Questions? Yeah.

13Borda Count

There's plenty of aggregation rules you could think of, so you might start trying to think about more clever ones, and maybe we can find more clever ones that satisfy our two reasonable axioms. So here's one that has a name, and as we'll see, this one is foreshadowing a bit. We said, look, the standard RLHF pipeline first fits a reward model, and a reward model in particular induces some ranking over the completions. It's going to implement some social aggregation rule. And it turns out, we'll see in a few lectures, it implements a natural social aggregation rule, one that is natural enough that it has a name in the classical social choice literature, called the Borda count.

Okay, so here's an example of something in the design space for how you might try to come up with a social aggregation rule. Suppose I have \(m\) alternatives, and each person ranks them from best to worst: first, second, third, fourth, through \(m\)-th. Let's say that everyone gives some number of points to each alternative in proportion to how highly ranked they are. So I'll give my first-choice completion \(m-1\) points. I'll give my second-choice completion \(m-2\) points, and so on. My last-choice completion will get 0 points. Everyone does this. The Borda count is just going to sum up these points as assigned by each of the \(n\) labelers. So ranking something first is akin to assigning it \(m-1\) points. Now that we're assigning some number of points to each thing, we can just sum them up. And that gives us some ranking. We just sort by that. We have an arbitrary rule for breaking ties.

The equivalent way to describe this method of ranking, which will be used later in the class, and you can think about why these are equivalent, is that I can imagine the following experiment. I'm going to draw one of the labelers from my population uniformly at random. I've got a particular alternative in mind; I'm trying to assign it a score. And then I'll draw another alternative, a challenger, from the \(m-1\) other alternatives, uniformly at random. And I'll ask, did the random labeler I chose prefer \(a\) to the randomly chosen challenger \(b\) or not? Every alternative will have some probability of winning this random challenge. And an alternative way of describing the Borda count is I'm just going to rank the alternatives by their probability of winning this random challenge.

So this is some aggregation rule, not necessarily a good one, but this is just some way to take individual ordered preferences and come up with some global ranking. We'll see eventually that this is what standard reward modeling does.

14Borda Violates IIA

So what's the Borda count for \(a\)? Well, the first two voters rank it first, so they give it two points each. Two, four. The next two voters rank it second, so it gets one point for each. Five, six. So the Borda count for \(a\) is six. What about the Borda count for \(b\)? Well, the first two voters rank it last, so it doesn't get any points from them. But the second two voters rank it first, so it gets two, four points. So the Borda count for \(b\) is four. And what about \(c\)? Well, the first two voters rank \(c\) second, so \(c\) gets one point from each of them, one, two. But it gets no further points because the remaining voters rank it last. So the Borda count for \(c\) is 2. And so for this first set of preferences, the Borda counts are 6, 4, 2.

Now here's another profile, \(P'\). And what I want to note is that between \(P\) and \(P'\), the only thing that has changed is how people rank \(c\). Everybody agrees, in both \(P\) and \(P'\), on the relative ranking of \(a\) and \(b\). Voter one, in both \(P\) and \(P'\), thinks that \(a\) is better than \(b\). Voter two, in both \(P\) and \(P'\), thinks that \(a\) is better than \(b\). Voter three, in both \(P\) and \(P'\), thinks that \(b\) is better than \(a\). Voter four, in both \(P\) and \(P'\), thinks that \(b\) is better than \(a\). Okay, so the only thing we've changed when moving from \(P\) to \(P'\) is where \(c\) is in the ranking. We haven't changed anyone's opinions about which is better, \(a\) or \(b\).

But when we compute the Borda counts, what does \(a\) get? Well, \(a\) gets two, four points, but no more. So the Borda count of \(a\) is now four. \(b\) gets one, two, three, four, five, six points. The Borda count of \(b\) is now six. And the Borda count of \(c\) remains two. So in our aggregate ranking at \(P\), we rank \(a\) first and then \(b\). But in the alternative ranking, we rank \(b\) first and then \(a\), even though nobody's relative preferences for \(a\) and \(b\) have changed. So the Borda count violates independence of irrelevant alternatives.

17Proof Roadmap

Okay, so the proof is going to proceed through a series of lemmas. We're going to design artificial scenarios in which we can identify one pivotal voter. And then we're going to use the axioms to lift the fact that in these constructed scenarios this voter is pivotal to the fact that they must be pivotal in all profiles. So we constructively identify who the dictator is, given a rule that satisfies these properties.

So first we're going to show a technical lemma that says, if everyone has an extreme view of \(b\), meaning everyone thinks \(b\) is either the very best or the very worst, then, if our aggregation rule satisfies our axioms, the social aggregation must also put \(b\) at an extreme, either the very best or the very worst.

And from that, we're going to construct profiles in which people take turns moving their preference of \(b\) from the very worst to the very best. And we're going to note, in what would be called a hybrid argument in cryptography, that there must be in this sequence one change in which someone's vote pivotally changes the social ranking of \(b\) from the very worst to the very best. So this is some voter who is pivotal at this artificially constructed pair of profiles. And then we're going to use the axioms to lift the pivotalness of this voter at this artificially constructed pair of profiles to any profile, and show that this voter we identified through this hybrid argument actually has to be a dictator. So that's the high-level overview.

18Step 1: An Extreme Alternative Stays Extreme

And so here's the initial structural lemma. Suppose it's the case that every single voter ranks \(b\) either at the very top or at the very bottom. They could do either one. But what we're presuming is they're not ranking it somewhere in the middle. Everyone has an extreme view of \(b\): very best candidate or very worst candidate. Then the claim is that the aggregation rule, if it satisfies our axioms, must also have an extreme view of this candidate. It must also be either at the very top of the ranking or at the very bottom.

And so, why? Well, suppose it wasn't the case. That just means that in the final aggregate ranking, \(b\) is not extreme, meaning there's something that the aggregate ranking ranks better than \(b\); call it \(a\). There's something that the aggregate ranking ranks worse than \(b\); call it \(c\). And so we're now going to perform some surgery on the profile so that we haven't changed any of the comparisons about \(b\), but we're going to arrange it so that everyone likes \(c\) better than \(a\), and get to some contradiction.

19Proof of the Extreme-Alternative Lemma

So remember, the reason we're going to be able to arrange that everyone likes \(c\) better than \(a\) without changing how anyone [ranks things relative to \(b\)] is because we've assumed that \(b\) is at an extreme in everyone's preferences, either at the very top or at the very bottom. And so there's only two cases. If \(b\) is at the very top, then everyone ranks \(b\) better than everything. In particular, they rank \(b\) better than \(a\), and they rank \(b\) better than \(c\). And so we can just move \(c\) in everybody's ranking so that \(c\) is preferred to \(a\), without changing how anyone ranks anything relative to \(b\). Or, for someone who ranks \(b\) at the very bottom: if they rank \(b\) at the very bottom, they think \(a\) is better than \(b\), and they also think \(c\) is better than \(b\). And similarly, we can just move \(c\) in their ranking so that they prefer it to \(a\). And having done that, that hasn't changed how anyone ranks anything relative to \(b\). If \(b\) was somewhere in the middle, we might not be able to do this without changing people's relative preferences. [We can] because it's at an extreme.

Okay, and so now we apply our two axioms. We're assuming that our aggregation rule satisfies independence of irrelevant alternatives. And because this modification hasn't changed anyone's preferences relative to \(b\), it hasn't changed how the social aggregation ranks anything relative to \(b\). So the original preferences caused the aggregation to rank \(a\) better than \(b\) better than \(c\). Independence of irrelevant alternatives must cause the social aggregation to also rank \(a\) better than \(b\) better than \(c\), because we haven't changed the comparisons for anyone between \(a\) and \(b\) or between \(b\) and \(c\).

On the other hand, everyone now unanimously agrees that \(c\) is better than \(a\) in the modified ranking. And so the Pareto axiom requires that the aggregate ranking also puts \(c\) better than \(a\). And these are transitive rankings, so [\(a \succ b \succ c\)] would require that \(a\) is better than \(c\). So from independence of irrelevant alternatives we get \(a\) greater than \(c\), but from Pareto we get \(c\) greater than \(a\), so that's a contradiction.

We've established this structural fact. It's not yet clear what the relationship is to the impossibility theorem, but it's the structural fact that if the input preferences all rank \(b\) at some extreme, then the output preference also must rank \(b\) at some extreme. We don't know which one, but that's what we've established in this lemma.

30How Can We Escape the Impossibility?

Okay, so, Arrow's impossibility theorem. If the thing that we're trying to do is elicit these binary pairwise preferences from people, is \(a\) better than \(b\), to get human annotations about language model completions, there are just some fundamental barriers that we should know about in Arrow's theorem. Now, that doesn't mean that there's no way forward. It just means you need to grapple with Arrow's theorem along the way.

So, it's already been pointed out that in proving Arrow's theorem, we had to manipulate preference profiles as part of the argument in ways that maybe you think are unnatural. We had to have populations where a lot of people think \(b\) is the very best and a lot of people think \(b\) is the very worst. You might think, okay, maybe preferences of the people we care about don't behave like that. Maybe generally everybody agrees that GPT-5.6 completions are better than […]. We're not going to get this kind of polarizing preferences that needed to be constructed in the proof. And indeed, if you're willing to believe that preferences are restricted, then you can escape Arrow's theorem. For more restricted classes of preferences, Arrow's theorem doesn't necessarily hold. People study when it doesn't. There's a family of preferences called single-peaked preferences where pairwise majority works.

Of course you can say, I don't care about some of the axioms. I'm just going to use the Borda count, so I don't care that it violates IIA.

You can also think about changing the object you're trying to construct. A language model induces a distribution over completions. It's randomized. So it doesn't necessarily need to express a total ordering over the completions. That's just something that you do along the way, because the first step is constructing a reward model, and a reward model is the thing that induces a total ordering. But you can say, okay, well, I'm just going to think about distributions as first-order objects. Okay. We'll think about that in a couple lectures. These are called maximal lotteries.
