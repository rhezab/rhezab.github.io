8Aumann's Agreement Theorem

So let's just remember what that is, and maybe where it leaves something to be desired. Aumann's agreement theorem is a classical theorem from, depending on how you count, either economics or statistics. Aumann was an economist, but this was published in a statistics journal. And it's sort of like a fixed point theorem about Bayesian agents.

Okay, so just to remind ourselves of all of the building blocks: Aumann's theorem concerns perfect Bayesian agents. So the idea is there's some commonly known joint distribution over evidence that you might see, evidence that I might see, and some outcome that we care about. Some binary event, let's say, that either occurs or does not occur. And we know by assumption what this prior distribution is. So if any one of us observed the pieces of evidence that the two of us jointly have, we would, being proper Bayesians, come to the same belief. But that's not the situation. I observe my evidence and you observe your evidence. And so we might have different posterior beliefs.

But the further assumption that goes into Aumann's theorem is that we have common knowledge about each other's posterior beliefs. Okay, so in particular, it is common knowledge that I believe that the event has some \(\alpha\) probability of occurring, and that you believe that the event has some \(\beta\) probability of occurring. And what does common knowledge mean? Well, informally it means not just that I know that you think that the event has a \(\beta\) probability of occurring, but I know that you know that I think that, and I know that you know that I know that you know that I think that, and so on and so forth.

7Common Knowledge Joins Whole Information Cells

The way we formalized this was by thinking about the set of states that are indistinguishable to each of the parties given the evidence that we have. So there might be different states of the world, some of which make the event true, some of which make the event false, but that we cannot distinguish individually given the information that we have. We could join the states that I cannot distinguish by edges in a graph. We could join the states that you cannot distinguish by edges in a graph. And the set of states that is common knowledge is like the connected component in this graph that holds the real state.

The reason why the connected component is the right object to think about is because, when we start going through the iteration, what is it that I know that you know that I know that you know, that's kind of like iteratively traversing alternating kinds of edges in this graph. And when you take that to the limit, you get the connected component.

8Aumann's Agreement Theorem

Okay. So Aumann's theorem says, look, if it's common knowledge that I have some belief and you have some belief, then we actually have to agree on what that belief is. The only way we can have common knowledge of each other's beliefs is if those beliefs are the same thing. Which is to say, we didn't need to actually share the information we observed to agree on predictions. We just needed to share our beliefs that were induced by those observations, at least through sufficiently many iterations of Bayesian reasoning that we reach common knowledge. And we did confirm that common knowledge itself is kind of like a fixed point of an infinite iteration of backwards induction.

11Can Conversation Create Common Knowledge?

But we did in fact confirm that there's a constructive way to get to common knowledge, which is just to have a conversation. I could tell you what I believe. You, being a perfect Bayesian, would integrate over all of the observations that could have caused me to have that belief. That would cause you to have a new belief. I could, in turn, integrate over all of the observations you might have that would have resulted in the conversation we have seen so far, and I would correspondingly update my posterior. And we showed that, although it's maybe not plausible that someone could carry out each of these updates, at least if you could do this, we would converge to common knowledge, and therefore agreement, after merely finitely many steps. In fact, every iteration of this conversation eliminates at least one state that the world could be in from our set of states that are consistent with the evidence. And so the iteration converges in a number of steps that is at most the cardinality of the state space that we're thinking about, which might be enormous, but is finite by assumption. Okay. We've [seen] that.

14We Want Fast Approximate Agreement

And then we started to complain about various aspects of this theorem, and there's lots to complain about. So first of all, if we believe that the state space is finite, we did argue for finite convergence, but it could be very, very, very slow in any kind of meaningful state space. The state space includes not only the thing we ultimately care about, but to embed it in this formalism where I can eliminate states with certainty, I've got to expand the state space to include potentially the signals that you and I would see. Expect the state space to be enormous. We don't have any kind of guarantee that conversations like this would converge quickly.

And this theorem is seemingly premised, very fundamentally, on this model of the two parties being perfect Bayesian reasoners, which is both epistemically and computationally ridiculous. You don't have a common prior over the set of all observations that I might have, cross what you might have, and know exactly how they correlate with the outcomes. Especially if we want to think about this as providing some framework in which a person might interact with a machine, the observations that the parties have are kind of mutually illegible. So where would this prior come from? Even if you have this prior, computing these Bayes updates, again, it's kind of a ludicrous computation to even imagine. I need to integrate over all of the observations you might have made that would have been consistent both with my observations and the entire history of conversation we've had so far, just in order to compute one of these update steps. It's a non-starter.

And so what we're left with is a goal that I want to make good on in this lecture, which is to recover all of the nice things about the agreement theorem. We should have a mechanism through which two parties can iteratively interact. They shouldn't need to communicate very much; they shouldn't need to share their observations, which might be enormous or illegible to the other party. They should be able to speak only about beliefs. They should be able to converge to agreement, or at least approximate agreement. And we want that convergence to be fast; it shouldn't take an enormous number of rounds. And also we want the behavioral assumptions that we require of the two parties to argue for convergence to be computationally tractable. We don't want to require that they be perfect Bayesians, have a correct prior, be able to do anything hard like compute a Bayes update.

But we also would like the assumptions that we place on the parties to be consistent with the world in which they're perfect Bayesians. We don't want to prove a different theorem that doesn't hold if the parties are too smart. We want to assume something that is satisfied by perfect Bayesians. And so everything we will do will also apply to perfect Bayesians. But we want something that's a lot easier to satisfy, both computationally and in terms of what the two parties have to know. Okay, so that's the goal for this.

Now, to be able to achieve fast convergence, convergence to agreement after only a short conversation, we are going to relax our definition of agreement to \(\varepsilon\)-approximate agreement. In Aumann's theorem, agreement meant exact agreement. If we were talking about the probability that it would rain tomorrow, if you believed that there was a 93.5769% chance of rain tomorrow, and I believed that there was a 97.5238% chance of rain tomorrow — imagine those two numbers were the same except for the last decimal, I forget — then we haven't yet agreed. We have to keep talking until we agree to the last decimal.

So that's something that I want to relax. I want to say, okay, well, we can talk about agreement up to the second decimal place, where if I think there's a 93.12% chance of rain, and you think there's a 93.13% chance of rain, we won't continue arguing. We will shake hands and we'll call it a day. Okay, so we'll have some error tolerance \(\varepsilon\), which will just mean that the probabilistic forecasts, mine and yours, at subsequent rounds of the conversation will count as agreement, even if they are not exactly equal up to decimal precision, so long as they are within \(\varepsilon\) of one another. And the thing that we will get is fast convergence to approximate agreement in this sense.

15Repeated Conversations with Outcome Feedback

And because I would like to do away entirely with any kind of Bayesian assumption — I don't want people to have to know anything about a distribution, I don't even want there to necessarily be a distribution, I want an agreement theorem that applies even in adversarially changing environments — I will talk about agreement not for a single conversation, which is what Aumann's agreement theorem and its interactive variant do, and instead think about sequences of conversations that take place over sequences of days, so that we can talk about fast agreement on most days for every sequence, even those that are adversarial.

And again, maybe if there's time, I'll mention this at the end: if there was a known prior, if we really were in the Bayesian setting of Aumann's agreement theorem, you could just imagine that every day the new example was another fresh draw from the prior, and that would lift everything we develop here to the classical Bayesian one-shot setting of Aumann. So what we're going to prove here is truly, in the mathematical sense, a generalization and strengthening of Aumann's agreement theorem, which is to say it all lifts to the same Bayesian setting. We are proving it in a more general setting that allows for arbitrary distribution shifts in sequential environments and does not require known priors. Okay, so does the protocol make sense? It's very much like the conversation protocol of Aumann's theorem, but now iterated across days, and there's now no probabilistic process. That's one of the strengthenings here. The sequence of observations and outcomes can be arbitrary. They can communicate other stuff too. You'll see that what we need for this to work, we're going to require that the predictions satisfy some simple statistical tests that are variants of calibration. 16Ordinary Calibration Is Not Enough

Yeah, so first, just to knock down a super dumb idea. Once I've said this, you might think, okay, maybe it's just good enough if both parties make calibrated forecasts. Whenever they speak, if I look at the sequence of predictions they make over time, they're calibrated to the outcomes. That clearly doesn't work.

And just to see why that doesn't work, let's consider a very dumb example. There's a state of the world that is either zero or one. And half of the days it's zero and half of the days it's one. Maybe it's even stochastic. The human knows which it is. The AI has nothing to say about which is true, so it just makes a non-informative prediction every day. It just says, okay, there's a 50% chance the outcome is one. And it turns out the human being, in this case, is very well informed. They know exactly which it is. They predict 0 when the outcome is 0, they predict 1 when the outcome is 1.

Now, both of these speakers are calibrated. Conditional on predicting one half, the outcome is one half of the time, so the AI is calibrated. Conditional on predicting 1, the outcome is 1 all of the time. Conditional on predicting 0, the outcome is zero all of the time, so the human being is calibrated. But if all I require is that the forecasts are calibrated, there's nothing pushing them towards agreement or information aggregation. The AI can just ignore the human's forecast and say one half at every round of conversation; the human can rightfully ignore the AI's forecast and continue repeating the right answer at every round at which they speak. Calibration has not imposed any force that pushes them towards agreement. So if we're hoping to get agreement theorems through calibration conditions, we need to condition on something that is responsive to the conversation they're having. Regular calibration doesn't require that the two parties even listen to each other at all.

17The Previous Message Exposes the Ignored Information

So, to get an idea of what that might be, let's zoom in on a pair of predictions and responses. On the days when the human being at the last round predicted zero, and the AI at the following round predicted one half, on those days the AI is visibly miscalibrated. On those days the outcome mean is zero, but the AI predicted one half. Similarly, on the days when the human being last predicted one, and the AI went on to predict one half, once again the AI is visibly miscalibrated: it predicted one half, but on those days the outcome frequency is one.

So the idea is that the AI is calibrated overall, but the conversation with the human being, in this case, revealed a conditioning event on which it was miscalibrated. The AI was calibrated overall, but it was not calibrated conditional on the previous reports of the human being. And that is the calibration condition that we're going to ask for, that we're going to call conversation calibration.

Just to remind you, calibration requires that your own forecast is unbiased conditional on the value of the forecast. Conditional on reporting one half, the outcome should be one half. Conversation calibration will require that you be unbiased conditional both on your own report and on the most recent report of the person you are talking to, the report of the person you are talking to from one round previous. So we look at AI one half and the report of the human being, one, and we ask if the average of the forecast equals the average of the outcome.

Now, a Bayesian is calibrated conditional on everything. So if I have a long conversation with them, like a 50-turn conversation, their forecasts are calibrated conditional on the whole conversation. But that would be quite hard to do, because the number of conversations of length 50 grows exponentially with 50; there's a branching factor in every round of conversation. We know how to use Blackwell approachability to give us calibration for small collections of events, but we would need exponentially-in-50 much data and computation to be able to calibrate conditional on all conversations.

But we're not asking for that. We're asking for something much simpler. We're asking for calibration at each round conditional only on what was said the previous round. You can forget about everything that was said before that. It's kind of like we're looking for calibration conditions that have only one round of memory. Okay, so we're looking at calibration conditional on what was said one round previous, forgetting everything else. That's not that many conditioning events. If the prediction grid is an \(\varepsilon\)-net, it's only like \(1/\varepsilon\) conditioning events, rather than exponential in […]. But what we're going to see is that that's actually sufficient to recover Aumann's agreement theorem. In some sense, the agreement theorem was only using that calibration condition anyhow, not all of the calibration conditions that assuming full Bayesian rationality implies.

19Calibrate Each Reply Given the Previous Report

Okay, let's formalize this idea of conversation calibration. Remember, we want our predictions to be unbiased conditional on the prediction that we have made and also conditional on the prediction that our interlocutor made at the previous round. So in particular, for every pair of prediction values \(p\) and \(q\) and for every round \(k\), let's define this subset of rounds indexed by \(k\), \(q\), and \(p\). It is just the set of days for which, number one, the conversation actually reaches round \(k\); for which, number two, the other guy forecast probability \(q\) at the previous round of conversation; and for which I forecast \(p\) at the \(k\)th round of conversation. This is just sub-selecting rounds that actually made it to round \(k\) by the value of the prediction made at the previous round and the value of the prediction made today. So if there's \(1/\varepsilon\) different prediction values, there's only \(1/\varepsilon^2\) subsets.

And let's let \(r\), again indexed by the round and these pairs of predictions, just keep track of the unnormalized bias at those rounds. It's the difference between the prediction made at round \(k\), which is \(p\) by definition of this set, and the actual outcomes on those rounds. If we're calibrated, these differences should be close to zero. These quantities make sense?

20Conversation Calibration Forces Fast Agreement

Suppose we had exact conversation calibration: all of these measures of bias were exactly zero at every round. Then, if I looked at the fraction of conversations that had not yet concluded by round \(R\), I would find that it was at most \(1/((R-2)\varepsilon^2)\). So independent of the complexity of the prediction problem, independent of the sophistication of the methods you're using to make predictions initially, depending only on the threshold at which we define agreement. If you ask for smaller and smaller values of \(\varepsilon\), more and more precise agreement, you need to talk for longer and longer, but that's all it depends on.

Just solving for a different variable: if I want the fraction of conversations that do not end before \(R\) to be smaller than \(\delta\), then what I find is I need \(R\) to be just two — that's the two opening rounds, I predict something initially and you predict something initially — plus \(1/(\delta\varepsilon^2)\) rounds of conversation beyond the two opening rounds. If you work a little bit harder, you can get the \(1/\delta\) dependence down to \(\log(1/\delta)\), but we'll just leave it \(1/\delta\) here. And this is the same bound that you can get for variance.

Okay. And the idea is actually quite simple. When we had this stupid example of two calibrated predictors, one that knew the outcome, the other that knew nothing, they were just speaking past each other, not listening to one another. The issue was, when the human uttered their perfect prediction, and then the AI went back to their uninformative prediction of one half, the AI was able to make a calibrated forecast without adding any useful information to the prediction. In fact, removing useful information from the prediction. They made a worse prediction.

What we're going to show is that conversation calibration removes that possibility. If I want to be calibrated conditional on what you say, there's really only two ways I can do it. I can just echo what you say, which is to say I can agree with you. Or, if I'm going to consistently disagree with you and still be calibrated conditional on what you say, it must be that I have added useful new information that you did not already have, in the sense that my predictions meaningfully decrease squared error relative to your prediction.

And that's really what's happening. Agreement's really just a corollary of this. The interesting thing is that conversation calibration forces me, if I am going to disagree with you, to add new useful information that was not already present in the forecast, as measured by reducing the squared error of my predictions. And fast agreement is a corollary of that, because I just can't, for very many rounds, meaningfully reduce squared error before squared error would be forced to be negative, which can't happen.

So fast agreement is maybe a corollary of the more interesting thing here, which is that conversation calibration forces you to either agree or add information. Merely reaching agreement wouldn't be interesting. I could design an AI model that would always agree with you, no matter what you said. In fact, we're designing such AI models. They reach fast agreement, but that's not interesting. What's interesting here is the reason we reach fast agreement is because we are forcing the models to add information if they can. If they can't, they have to agree.

So we're going to prove an upper bound on the length of the conversation. But in fact, if the conversations are that long, that's great. The only way the conversations can be that long is if we reduce error to zero. That's going to be how we know that they can't go longer than that, because error can't go below zero. In fact, if the conversation is not very useful, if the two parties aren't adding much information at each round, the conversation will be much shorter than this. Long conversations, given what we're going to prove, are a good thing. They mean that we're driving the error lower and lower. 39Summary

Okay. So to summarize: Aumann's agreement theorem says something powerful about information aggregation. It says that we don't need to be able to share all of our observations with one another. That might be hard. Our observations might be very big and difficult to express to the other party — if I'm an LLM, all of my training data. We don't need to actually share our observations in order to come to agreement. We only need to share our beliefs. At least in the original version of the theorem, we might need to share our beliefs iteratively back and forth for a very long time, but at least eventually we would come to agreement.

Now, that theorem was seemingly premised on the implausible assumption that the two parties were Bayesians, and everything that was entailed with that: a correct common prior, the ability to perform updates. But what we've seen is we didn't actually need all of the implausible parts about being a Bayesian. We only needed the plausible, easy part: the ability to be calibrated marginally and conditionally on a small amount of information, what the other guy said yesterday. What we showed is that if you are calibrated conditional on what the other guy said yesterday, then the only way you can continue a conversation without just agreeing with what the other guy said is to add information in a way that decreases error. And you can't decrease error forever, and so you have to agree quickly. That's it.
