5Pairwise Comparisons

So, how do we elicit preferences? A very easy kind of preference to elicit is: here are two options, which of these do you prefer? And if you've used essentially any of the commercial AI systems, ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, you've probably at some point encountered something like this. You write a prompt, and rather than giving you a response, it gives you two responses and asks you which of these two you prefer.

Okay, this isn't the only kind of question you could ask of someone. I could say, how much do you prefer response A over response B, and ask for a number. But this is the easiest kind. It's not clear how someone should fill out "I prefer this response 2.3 times as much as the other one." And so that's not generally what people gather. People just gather these kinds of binary comparisons: do you like A or B?

But what you can learn about someone's preferences from pairwise comparisons like this is somewhat limited. Say I prefer A to B. You can't tell from that whether I'm basically indifferent (A and B are both pretty good, but on the margin I prefer A), or whether A is the only one that is acceptable and B is complete garbage. Here are two bad responses. But if the one on the right was just entirely racial slurs or something, I'd have a very strong preference for the other one. I might still prefer the one on the left here, but it's only mild. And that's just something that's not identified if you're only collecting binary preferences. So binary preferences are, in some sense, easy to elicit, but they're just not expressive enough to, even in principle, elicit full details about people's preferences. And so we should think about what we can learn about preferences just from binary comparisons.

The other thing we should think about is: ChatGPT asks this kind of question of all of its users. Its users are different people, and they have different preferences. Not everyone will agree about which of these two things is the best. And so when I go through the exercise of trying to aggregate these preferences across many users who have different preferences into a single behavior for the model, we need to think about what are we even trying to do when doing that aggregation? And what are the limits of what we can do when trying to aggregate fundamentally different preferences into a system that will essentially instantiate one set of preferences?

6The RLHF Pipeline

So, a bird's-eye view of the common alignment pipeline, what's called RLHF, reinforcement learning [from human feedback]. Initially, for some prompts and completions, gather human preferences. Maybe we'll pay labelers, or we'll just poll our users in production. And it's this kind of binary preference data: what do you like better, completion A or completion B? So we'll collect a bunch of preferences of this binary comparison sort.

Then we will train a reward model. Ultimately, we want to modify the weights of the language model to produce better, more preferred completions compared to less preferred ones. We don't want at every iteration of that to have to go and bug our human labelers, and so it's useful to try to build first a reward model that can take as input a prompt and a pair of completions, or one completion, and tell us how good that is, so that we can try to optimize the language model downstream to produce high-quality [completions]. So as an intermediate step, we produce this reward model from the preferences, which takes a prompt and a completion and tries to just put a number on how good that is. And of course, given a set of completions, that induces some total ordering over them. We can order them by how the reward model assesses them.

Okay, so at this intermediate step, we're trying to aggregate preferences from people who might have different preferences into some model which is inducing some single set of preferences, some single total ordering over these completions. And then we're going to use that to train the [language model] […].

7Basic Impossibility [Arrow]

And so this will take us back to the very early days of social choice, where people like Arrow studied: okay, what are the fundamental limits for this exercise? You have people whose preferences are represented by a transitive ordering over alternatives. It's exactly the thing we're getting when we get some of these pairwise comparisons. But you have \(n\) different people who have different orderings. And what you're trying to do is aggregate them into one single ordering. That's what the reward model is doing. It's taking the different preferences of all the people, and it's trying to aggregate them into a single order.

Already in the 1950s, Arrow asked: okay, what are the limits of what you could do? What do you even want? What are your goals, even, when you're trying to come up with an aggregate preference ordering given the preference orderings of a bunch of different people? And it's not clear, but you can start modestly. You can start asking yourself: maybe there's different notions of what is a good aggregate ranking, but why don't I start by just asking for some really simple things that you'd think you want out of any aggregate?

So here's one, which is called Pareto efficiency, but is super simple. It just says, look, if every single person out there prefers completion A to completion B, then you should probably learn a reward model that ranks completion A over completion B. Right? This isn't constraining you at all if there's any disagreement. This axiom doesn't specify at all what your aggregation needs to do if there's any disagreement at all. All it's saying is, if there happens to be unanimity, if everyone prefers A over B, then the reward model you learn should also prefer A over B. This seems sensible.

Here's another one that seems sensible. It's called independence of irrelevant alternatives. So it says, all right, suppose I've got a population of labelers. I'm going to focus on how they rank A and B in proportion relative to one another. Some people like A better than B, some people like B better than A. [There are] not just two completions out there. There's this other completion, C. Okay, suppose I now modify people's preferences by changing only how much people like completion C, without changing how they rank A and B relative to one another. So I have two sets of preferences, my initial set over here and my modified set over here. And in both of these sets of preferences, every individual agrees on the relative ordering of A and B. If Susie prefers A to B on the left, she also prefers A to B on the right. If Bob prefers B to A on the left, he also prefers B to A on the right. The only thing different between the left and the right is the relative preferences they have for this different completion C. The independence of irrelevant alternatives says that the social aggregation function, however it ranks A and B, should do it the same way on the left and the right, because nobody's preferences with respect to A and B have changed between the left and the right.

So there's some anecdote about independence of irrelevant alternatives: Sidney Morgenbesser, who's a philosopher, going to a diner, and the waitress says, "Would you like the apple pie or the cherry pie?" [He orders the apple.] She comes back and says, "Oh, I forgot to mention, we have a special today. We also have blueberry pie." "So in that case, let me change my order. I'll get the cherry pie." A weird interaction, because normally people respect the independence of irrelevant alternatives. Okay, so this makes sense. Yes?

And so what does Arrow say? It says, look, if you're trying to come up with a rule to aggregate different people's preferences, and you want that rule to satisfy both of these axioms, Pareto efficiency and independence of irrelevant alternatives, then there's only one way to do it, and it's to have a dictatorship. So this is a very negative result. 8Alternatives to Learning a Reward Model

Okay, maybe in light of this we should think of alternatives to the standard pipeline, where at this intermediate stage you learn the reward model. It is really the reward model that is inducing this total ordering over completions. It's not the LLM itself. The LLM is stochastic. You type in a prompt, and it'll give you a distribution over different [completions] every time. And so the object that Arrow's impossibility theorem binds to most strongly is not the thing we're trying to build in the end, the LLM, which is giving us a distribution over completions, but rather the reward model. Maybe we can avoid that intermediate step.

So given a prompt, let's just [think about] some distribution over completions. And now let's just think about how we might evaluate two distributions over completions and think about which of the two is better for some population of users. So say we know that our users come from some distribution. I'll just identify users here with the preference ordering they have. Now I've got two candidate distributions over completions of prompts, \(p\) and \(q\). And I want to know, how should I even think about which of these two is better?

Well, here's one way to think about it. Here's a thought experiment. Okay, I'm going to sample a random completion \(a\) from distribution \(p\). I'm going to sample another completion \(b\) from distribution \(q\). These are two things that ChatGPT could get back to you with when you ask a question. And then I'll sample a random user from my population. And I'll just ask them, "Hey, user, do you prefer \(a\) to \(b\), or no?" And if the answer is yes, I will say, okay, \(p\) won this time. And if no, I'll say \(q\) wins.

And of course, this is a stochastic comparison. I'm sampling completions, I'm sampling users. And so for any two distributions \(p\) and \(q\), there will be a probability that \(p\) wins and another probability that \(q\) wins. Just say that \(p\) defeats \(q\) in this tournament if it's more likely to win. Maybe they're tied, but there's going to be a probability that \(p\) wins and a probability that \(q\) wins if we conduct this thought experiment.

And so, having set up this thought experiment, you might ask: well, can I find a distribution over completions that defeats everything, or at least ties them? If I have two distributions that are identical competing against each other, of course each one will win about half the time, just by symmetry. But can I find a single distribution over completions that defeats or ties all challengers? Maybe that's what I want to use. Because if I have something that is defeated, maybe I should use the thing that defeats it instead. So can I find a distribution that defeats all challengers?

It's not obvious that I can, by the way. This is kind of the thing that plagues aggregation rules and that Arrow's theorem is related to. You could just have cycles. I could find that \(p\) defeats \(q\) and \(q\) defeats \(r\) and \(r\) defeats \(p\), and maybe there is no [winner] like this. So it's not obvious what the answer to this question is the first time you think about it: is there a distribution that defeats all challengers? But it turns out the answer is yes. In the older social choice literature, this is what's called a maximal lottery, and it was kind of rediscovered in the AI alignment literature, [which] called it a von Neumann winner, and then people realized these were kind of the same thing.

And so this is a different way you can think about aggregating preferences, taking advantage of the fact that, by their nature, LLMs are stochastic algorithms. We can associate with a prompt a distribution over completions. We don't have to fix a single completion. And trying to do this as our objective avoids the whole preference model step, which is the thing that was artificially inducing a fixed ranking over [completions]. And so you could set this as the goal: try to find a distribution over completions that defeats all challengers. And this is the goal of some sub-literature that is called Nash learning from human feedback. Nash learning because, as we'll see when we study these things, \(p^*\) is kind of the minimax equilibrium, or the Nash equilibrium, in a particular zero-sum game that you construct, in which your strategy space and your challenger's strategy space are just distributions over completions. So you want to find the Nash equilibrium of some particular zero-sum game to solve for this object. That's why the particular training procedure is called Nash [learning].

9Utility Functions

Okay. Good. So far, we've just talked about preferences as orderings: I prefer A to B or not. But that doesn't get at the strength of my preference. Maybe I'm on the fence between A or B, or maybe A is way better than B. And oftentimes it is convenient to model people and agents as having utility functions, which capture not just ordering but strength of preference.

So for example, if there's a set of actions I can take, and there's maybe a set of states of the world that are decision-relevant for my actions, maybe I would model an agent, or myself, as [assigning] a number to each combination of action and state of the world, saying, okay, this is how much utility I get if I take action \(a\) in this state of the world. Maybe if there's randomness, either inherent in the world or because of my own uncertainty, I would try to choose actions that maximize my utility in expectation. When is that a reasonable modeling assumption?

And we might set out goals when we're trying to figure out what is desirable, what we should be working towards. Maybe maximize social welfare. Maybe I want to arrange things so that when I sum or average everybody's utility, that's as big as possible. So a basic question is why I'm doing this. 10Social Welfare Aggregation

So, okay. In principle, let's say I can ask you what do you like better, A or B. It looks pretty easy to ask you about for a fixed [pair of completions]. In principle (it might be hard to get you to spend the time to answer the question), I could ask you about distributions, or lotteries, as well. I could say, look, here's one distribution over completions. Here's another distribution over completions. You're going to have to sample from them a bit to learn about them, but please do that. I could ask you, do you prefer distribution \(p\) or distribution \(q\)? I could ask you about pairwise preferences for lotteries as well.

And it turns out that if your preferences over lotteries satisfy four axioms that are called the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms (they're all kind of reasonable): [completeness] is just that you have a preference. So if I ask, [you] would prefer [one or the other]. Transitivity just means, okay, if you claim to prefer \(p\) over \(q\), and you claim to prefer \(q\) over \(r\), then you should also claim to prefer \(p\) over \(r\). Continuity is about: your preferences shouldn't change discontinuously as I add randomness. So if you prefer \(p\) over \(q\) over \(r\), then there should be some extent to which I can randomize between \(p\) and \(r\) so that you're indifferent between that and \(q\). It shouldn't be that you discontinuously go from preferring the lottery over \(q\) to not. There should be some point of indifference. And independence is similar to independence of irrelevant alternatives: if you prefer \(p\) to \(q\), then for any alternative \(r\), you should prefer a lottery that mixes some fraction of \(p\) with \(r\) to a lottery that mixes the same fraction of \(q\) with \(r\).

So all reasonable assumptions. And if your agent satisfies these assumptions, then I can always represent their preferences as a utility function. In fact, if I can represent your preferences as a utility function, you obviously have to satisfy these axioms. So these are necessary and sufficient conditions for it to be sensible to represent human or agent goals as utility functions. This is mapping out when we're justified in modeling goals as utility functions.

It turns out that if we're going to try to aggregate preferences also with a utility function, if the social preferences also satisfy these axioms, then if we want the social preferences to satisfy the Pareto property, the same one from the Arrow axioms (that if everyone universally prefers \(p\) to \(q\), then so should the aggregate), then the only social goal that can accomplish that is to maximize either social welfare or some weighted social welfare. We can weight different people differently, but maximizing weighted sums of utilities is the only thing we can do in that case, which justifies, in general, that as being [the objective].

13Calibration

Now, okay, so here's just a picture of the kind of thing we want to happen. Again, we want to have some statistical model that, given some context or prompt, can predict something. It's not the true distribution on states of the world given context, but something that can be used as if it is. And without knowing who our user is (we've got many different downstream users who care about different things, they've got different utility functions), we'd like this thing that we predict to be useful for all of them in deciding what to do. Different ones of them might take different actions given the same prediction, because they care about different things.

Now, it's hard to predict the true probability of \(y\) given \(x\), but there's a substantially weaker statistical condition called calibration, which is easy to satisfy. It just asks that our prediction be unbiased, conditional on the value of the prediction itself. So if we predict [a 20% chance of] rain tomorrow, calibration asks that on the days that we predict that, conditional on that prediction, it should actually rain 20% of the time. So it's just a simple self-consistency thing. The predictor should just be statistically unbiased, conditional on the prediction.

And we're going to make these predictions. What users are going to do… they ultimately need to choose actions. So they're going to come up with some policy that is going to take as input our prediction, and it's going to decide what to do: a mapping from predictions to actions.

A nice fact about calibration is that, even though it's much weaker than perfect learning, predicting the true distribution on \(y\) given \(x\), it is actually both necessary and sufficient, and exactly characterizes, the following property. Calibration is necessary and sufficient such that for all downstream decision makers, no matter what their utility functions are, if they're thinking about, amongst all of the policies they could possibly use mapping forecasts to actions, what is the best one, they will all come to the conclusion that the best one is to just best respond to the forecasts. That is, the best thing to do amongst all policies you could possibly use is to treat the forecast as if it was the true conditional probability, even though it's not necessarily.

Okay, so calibration provides this very nice interface between prediction and decision making that is agnostic to what the downstream utility function is. This condition doesn't mention the downstream utility function at all, such that you can plug these predictions into any downstream utility function and get this guarantee that the best policy to apply is this very simple best response policy. [That's what's] nice [about] calibration. [Predictions are] going to be ultimately part of some decision-making pipeline where you don't necessarily know what the goals are further down the pipeline, maybe because there are many such goals, because the same prediction component is going to be used in many different decision-making [problems].

And it turns out that, in part because calibration is so simple, it is possible and easy to produce calibrated forecasts in settings that would seem very difficult. We don't need to have a really well-behaved learning setting where there's a fixed distribution and we have samples from this distribution and we assume that at test time we face exactly the same distribution we trained on. No. You can have a fully adversarial setting where the distribution is shifting arbitrarily and unexpectedly over time. Or maybe there is no distribution; the sequence of predictions you need to make are about adversarial events. Using ideas closely related to no-regret learning and Blackwell approachability, which you'll learn about (it's fun), you can guarantee that you make a sequence of forecasts that of course might not be correct in any platonic sense (in an adversarial setting, correctness doesn't even really parse; there is no underlying distribution), but that are calibrated, and therefore plug into decision-making pipelines in very nice ways.

And there's a variant of calibration called decision calibration that gives you efficient generalizations of this to pretty high-dimensional settings. We don't have to be talking about predicting the probability of rain, a binary event. We could be talking about complicated events, maybe trying to figure out what your diagnosis is. Maybe there's a thousand different diagnostic codes in the hospital. We can still talk about decision calibration in ways that have [this] nice interface.

18Collaboration

And so the question is: how can we make decisions that successfully integrate the expertise of both parties, in a way that improves on either one's performance alone? And you might think, okay, well, everything that's decision-relevant is known by one of the parties, and they will make the decision. But an important premise here is that it's kind of hard to do. I can't [communicate everything]. [The AI is trained on] data [I can't read], and I can't express all of my contextual knowledge and observations in the text box.

So this is related to something, again, studied by an old theorem in economics. It's called Aumann's agreement theorem. And Aumann's agreement theorem talks about a stylized setting. It says, look, there's two perfect Bayesians. What does it mean that you're a perfect Bayesian? Well, it means that I have some correct prior belief over the joint distribution of everything I might observe, everything you might observe, and the outcome. So do you. And we have different observations. So you and I might initially have different posterior beliefs. But what we're going to do is have a conversation with each other. I'm going to tell you my posterior beliefs about some event. You, being a perfect Bayesian, will integrate over all of the things I might have seen that would cause me to have that belief, and will update your belief. You have a new posterior belief; you tell that to me. I similarly do a perfect Bayesian update. I have a new belief, I tell it to you, and we go back and forth. And Aumann's agreement theorem says this doesn't continue forever. We'll eventually converge to the same belief, and in an accuracy-improving way. Of course, I can't become less accurate as a perfect Bayesian by conditioning on more stuff. So this is a way to come to accuracy-improving agreement and, in some settings, provably aggregate information through some low-bandwidth channel. We're not talking about our observations. We're only talking about our beliefs.

Now, it's kind of hard to implement Aumann's agreement theorem, because all of the objects it speaks about are completely implausible, both computationally and epistemically. I don't have prior beliefs over the joint distribution of what I and ChatGPT know about the patient that's walked into my office. It's not even clear what it would mean to have such a thing. And if I did, conditioning on all of the things that ChatGPT might have observed that would cause it to have some belief, and updating my posterior belief over that integral, that's an implausible computation. So Aumann's agreement theorem tells a nice story, but it's not a computationally or epistemically implementable story.

But it turns out that you can actually get the same conclusion, in fact a stronger conclusion: you get fast convergence to accuracy-improving agreement in quite general settings, even without all of this Bayesian machinery. It's going to suffice that in the conversation that we have, in my part of the conversation, in every round, the things that I say are not correct the way they would be for a Bayesian, but are just calibrated, conditional on what you said at the previous round. If you satisfy the same condition, [we have] conversation calibration, and we recover Aumann's agreement theorem completely. And because we have fast, tractable algorithms for guaranteeing this kind of calibration, this gives us a very communication-efficient way to aggregate beliefs in an accuracy-improving way. We don't need to talk about the observations we made that caused us to have those beliefs. We only need to talk about the beliefs.

22Delegated Approval

Finally, the last thing that I want to tell you about at this point is delegated approval. So we've got these agents that can do increasingly interesting things over a long time. You can fire up Codex and you can give it a task that's going to take 10 hours, and you can press go and maybe come back and see what's done. But you don't trust that it's going to correctly advance your goals everywhere. You're worried it might delete your hard drive or something. You might not want to let it do arbitrary things without getting your approval.

So you could conservatively require [approval for] any step that requires taking an action that's hard to reverse. That removes a lot of the benefits of automation. It's very annoying if I set Codex on some long-running task and I go into the office and I come back and I find that one minute into the task, it was like, "Hey, can I check out this website?" and it's just been waiting for me the whole time. It removes a lot of the benefits of automation if I make it ask me for permission for everything.

Now, Codex has this great feature. [I can] have it ask me for approval for everything. I wouldn't want to do that; then I could never walk away. I could give it full access to my computer and let it do anything without ever asking anyone for permission. That looks kind of scary; it's written all in red. So there's this thing that I, and probably all of you, choose, which is this middle option that's called "Approve for me." It's great. It does stuff, but it never asks me for permission. But what it's doing is it's firing up another GPT agent. And whenever it wants to ask someone for permission, it doesn't ask me, it asks this other agent. That agent approves it. So things are great.

[The] question is, when is a setup like this actually giving me any extra protection? Is this some circular thing? I have the main driving agent behind this approval barrier because I don't trust it to correctly advance my interests. Why should I trust another agent, which is probably a similar agent, to correctly advance my interests? Do I need to have another approval agent one layer down? Is this turtles all the way down?

Generally, you can set up a model that looks kind of like this. You've got some stateful MDP, you've got your driver agent, your main GPT agent, I'll call it the proposer. And whenever it wants to take an action, it'll ask for approval, not from you, but maybe from a panel of agents that you've delegated to give approval. And you, in designing this mechanism, get to figure out what the authorization rule is. Maybe I'll let them do it if they all unanimously agree it's a good idea. Or maybe if it's almost unanimous, I'll let them do it. Here's where the design ability is. And if this rule approves, I'll execute whatever they proposed. Otherwise, I'll use some baseline policy, maybe ask the human being. And then whatever action is taken is taken, which actually changes the state of the world, and this is just a closed [loop].

Are there conditions [weaker than] full alignment that will promise me safety relative to the baseline policy? That no matter what, no matter what the proposer agent tries to do, I'll always have expected utility from running this control loop that's better than the baseline policy? And it turns out that you can exactly characterize this. You can give an if-and-only-if condition. And the answer is very similar to the market alignment idea. It suffices that these reviewer agents have utility functions that contain my utility function in their non-negative span. [It's not] guaranteed to hold, but it's much weaker than individual [alignment].
